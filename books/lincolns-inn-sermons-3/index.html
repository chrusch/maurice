This volume follows the Church’s year at Lincoln’s Inn from Advent Sunday 1857 to the first Sunday after Easter 1858, a winter shadowed by the Indian Mutiny, a commercial panic, and anxious debate over how to commend the Bible to Hindus and Muslims. Maurice reads each season’s texts as words about the present: the Judgment is something prophets and apostles longed for, because it is Christ revealed as the true Head of the nations; the Psalmist’s thirst for God is every person’s thirst; and the Bible is a book of freedom, not of restraint.

Against evidences, parties and a religion of the favoured few, the sermons return to one conviction: Christ is Lord of every human being, present to each conscience as light, judge and deliverer, so that His law of bearing one another’s burdens, His Sacrifice and His victory over death belong to the whole race.

  3. The Judgment Day an Object of Intense Desire Advent Sunday

    Prophets and Apostles longed for the Judgment because Christ, who came in humility as the true Head of humanity, will be revealed to take the nations for His inheritance.

  4. The Gospel of Hope Second Sunday in Advent

    Paul treats the Scriptures as books of hope for Jew and Gentile alike, so the Bible is recommended to Hindus and Muslims by the hope it kindles, not by apologetic arguments.

  5. Tests of the Deliverer Third Sunday in Advent

    John’s doubt in prison teaches ministers to own their questions and send doubters to Christ Himself rather than to defences; His healing acts reveal a King who conquers the will by humility and sacrifice.

  6. The Christmas Gathering Fourth Sunday in Advent

    Christmas gatherings rightly foretell Christ’s final gathering of all in Himself, warn against the opposite spirit of separation that would embody Antichrist, and link the Nativity to the Sacrifice that comforts the bereaved.

  7. The Hungry Filled; the Rich Sent Away Empty St. John’s Day (Sunday after Christmas)

    The centurion’s trust in a Word that commands shows the Kingdom to be an inward sovereignty, so strangers from east and west enter it while the children who loved darkness are cast out.

  8. Christmas Charities Sunday after Christmas

    The Incarnation reveals a Father who stoops to raise His children, so true Christmas charity neither gives lazily nor withholds but awakens the poor to faith, labour and foresight.

  9. God’s Government of Us; its Ground and its End First Sunday of the Year

    Life is vain only when lived apart from the Lord who governs it, and at each new year His goodness, forbearance and longsuffering, which are His Spirit at work, call us to repentance.

  10. The Glory of the Chosen Nation Realized in the Illumination of the World Sunday after the Epiphany

    God alone manifests His Son to the spirit, whether by the Magians’ star or Paul’s preaching, so no book or Church replaces faith, and the nations’ longing ends in seeing the Father.

  11. The Thirst of Human Beings in All Ages Second Sunday after the Epiphany

    The Psalmist’s thirst for the living God is every person’s thirst, so the Psalms belong to all, and the poor and unbelieving are reached by Christ the deliverer, not by evidences.

  12. The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal Third Sunday after the Epiphany

    Far from restraining desire for freedom, the Bible teaches what freedom is: our real bondage lies in the will, and the Son who adopts us as children breaks it, for individuals and nations.

  13. The Curse of Concealment; the Blessing of Discovery Septuagesima Sunday

    Whatever exposes hidden evil in a man’s conscience is the very light of Christ that stirred Pharisees, publicans, apostles and Judas, and it calls even the sleeping baptized to wake and rise.

  14. The Goading of an Adversary the Impulse to Prayer Sexagesima Sunday

    Prayer springs from the adversary that torments every person, so in the widow’s parable God’s justice is a reason to pray for deliverance, and the cry itself gets an answer.

  15. St. Paul an Enemy Not of Seeming Evil but of Actual Evil Quinquagesima Sunday

    Abstaining from the appearance of evil means shunning every actual form of evil, not whatever merely looks wrong, since Christ and Paul both risked looking evil; it follows from proving all things.

  16. How Self-Examination is Possible First Sunday in Lent

    Self-examination is no fiction in which we are plaintiff, defendant and judge at once, because Christ is in each of us as a distinct Judge, and knowing this frees us from morbid self-consciousness.

  17. St. Paul’s Thoughts and Acts Not Determined by his Own Love but by Christ’s Second Sunday in Lent

    The love that constrains Paul is Christ’s love bearing down on us, not ours for Him; it shapes judgment as well as feeling, since all were dead and He died for all.

  18. Stillness in the Confidence of God’s Triumph Third Sunday in Lent

    Psalm 46 grounds a stillness within action in God’s presence and His promise to exalt Himself among the heathen, so restless times and missions rest on trust, not on force, bribes or argument.

  19. The Law of Christ the Law of Humanity Fourth Sunday in Lent

    Bearing one another’s burdens is the law of all humanity, not of a select few, and competition, though real in every heart, cannot be what holds society together.

  20. Christ’s Baptism of Fire Fifth Sunday in Lent

    Christ’s fan and unquenchable fire are His present work of separating wheat from chaff in each heart, nation and Church, the burning indignation of perfect love against everything contrary to love.

  21. The Great Sacrifice and the Feast of Deliverance Sunday before Easter

    Christ Himself, not a theory or a type, is our Passover, whose complete sacrifice for all makes the Feast a thanksgiving for grace received, to be kept without malice or any claim of merit.

  22. Christ the Lord of Life and the Conqueror of Death for All Times First Sunday after Easter

    Victory over the grave is God’s gift through Christ to all, confirming rather than mocking older hopes of immortality, and it is received through faith and conflict, not claimed as natural right.

About this digital edition

How the text was made, and the printer's errors corrected

The original book

Sermons, Volume 3 by Frederick Denison Maurice.

This edition

Produced 2026-09-29 from a page scan of the printed book, as part of a project to make F. D. Maurice’s works freely available in every useful digital form. The guiding principle throughout was fidelity to the printed text: Maurice’s own spelling, capitalization, punctuation, ligatures, and typography rule, except where a printing error was identified and deliberately emended (each such change is listed below).

How this text was made

The transcription pipeline combines automated tools, AI models, and page-by-page human review:

  • OCR and conversion. The scan was OCRed (ocrmypdf/Tesseract) and converted to structured text (marker), then cleaned by a language model (claude-haiku-4-5-20251001) working chunk by chunk.
  • Human review of every machine change. Every word the cleanup model altered was reviewed against an image of the printed line. Footnotes were verified against the printed page and renumbered uniformly.
  • Quotation marks were normalized to the printer’s own conventions and validated by a nesting checker; every remaining irregularity was inspected against the page image. The final text has fully paired quotation marks.
  • Italics were restored (the OCR text layer records none) by a vision model (claude-sonnet-5) cross-checked against a deterministic measurement of letterform slant on the page images, with per-word statistical calibration derived from the book itself. Every page was then reviewed by a human editor against the print. This edition carries 295 italic spans.
  • Small capitals and ligatures (æ, œ) are reproduced as printed.
  • A whole-book audit (claude-sonnet-5) checked structure, headings, footnotes, and Greek; its findings were reviewed by a human editor against the print before any were applied.

The text contains 0 footnotes and 103 small-caps renderings of the print.
