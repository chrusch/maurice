Part II.— (Continued.) of the Catholic Church and the Romish System

Chapter V. On the Relations of the Church with National Bodies

Oaths, marriage, property, and just war rest on a national order visible already in the Ten Commandments and in pagan Greece and Rome, carried forward into the nations of Christian Europe.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Section I.

The Old Testament—Ancient Pagan History—History of Modern Europe—General Inferences.

The question how the Old Testament dispensation relates to the New has already come up in several forms. So far, though, we have considered only the signs that all Christians believe have passed away. The Quaker dissolves them into spiritual ideas, while we suppose them exchanged for other signs of higher truths. When we turn to what is called the moral side of the Jewish economy, the dispute takes another shape. One set of Christians insists that the precepts of this kind in the old Law are permanently binding. Others say they have no authority except where the Gospel law of love re-enacts or contains them.

Those who hold the first opinion usually confine it to the Ten Commandments. They do not positively deny that the sacredness of the Divine Code may extend to parts of the Jewish institutions; they leave that open. But the Commandments stand out clearly and awfully. They were proclaimed amid thunder and lightning, the Jews took them as written by the finger of God, and mankind has received them as Divine. Their opponents see no reason to separate the Commandments from the rest of the ritual, either to honour or to dishonour them. They say: “The historian declares that both came from the Lord, so you cannot distinguish them by authority. Nor can you distinguish them by character. The Sabbath is as much a positive institution as the cities of refuge. If you admit the distinction between the moral and the positive, both are transitory; if you reject it, neither is.”

I could not take this up in the last section, but some hints given there may help now. I have maintained that the Scriptures interpret signs which we find actually existing among us and which need an interpreter. The New Testament has explained Baptism, the Creeds, Forms of Worship, the Eucharist and the Orders of Ministry as they exist in modern Europe. It shows them to be signs of a spiritual and universal society, what such a society means, and what place each sign holds in it. If these signs had perished and no dream of such a society remained, Scripture would be a very puzzling book. While they last, it is indispensable to anyone who wants to understand his position. The early parts were necessary from this point of view because they disclose part of the meaning each sign embodies, so that we gradually perceive its full import and connect it with human life. So when we meet in these early records customs, institutions and ordinances that God has not preserved, we presume they are to be viewed historically, by the light of what He has preserved. If someone asks whether they might usefully be restored, we can test that by the light Scripture gives on the whole dispensation. But where we find signs that have survived every change of circumstance and become part of modern society’s life, we must want an explanation and look for it where it can be had. It may matter how the change of period has affected them, but the first fact is that they exist. That compels us to ask where they come from and what they rest on.

Anyone who looks at the Ten Commandments will see that they are definitive and conservative, not creative or constitutive. They presume facts, principles and institutions already existing, and they denounce the violation or forgetting of these. The first presumes that an unseen Being brought the Jews out of Egypt; He is their deliverer and Lord, and they are to acknowledge Him as such. The second presumes Worship, men’s tendency to make objects of it from things they see and handle, a relation between worshippers and the Invisible Lord, and His government from generation to generation. The third presumes the practice of appealing to God’s Name, invoking Him as one who knows whether a man is guilty or innocent. The fourth assumes the institution of the Week, explains what it signifies, and warns against forgetting the distinction between the six days and the seventh. The fifth presumes the Paternal Relation and makes respect for it the condition of staying in the land given to the nation. The sixth presumes a community interested in the life of each member. The seventh presumes Marriage, the eighth Property, and the ninth Tribunals where one man may give witness about another. The tenth affirms a bond of Neighbourhood, the same bond all the rest suppose, and declares that even coveting what is a neighbour’s violates it.

Most readers will grant that these facts, institutions and principles were closely bound up with the life of the nation brought out of Egypt. But ancient history shows that some of them were closely bound up with the life of every nation it speaks of. The Greeks and Romans were very different from each other, yet both differed from the slaves and barbarians, whom we know chiefly through them. Where did the difference lie? Apart from intellectual superiority (though “apart” is hardly right, the two were so involved), they plainly had a clear sense of great landmarks in human society, the violation of which was evil. They believed an awful Unseen Power had fixed these landmarks and preserved them. Among the chief they reckoned the sacredness of life, of the paternal relation, of marriage and property, of appeals to the Divine name, and of tribunals to right wrong. They also reckoned the law of neighbourhood binding those of common ancestry living in one place, and the majesty of law kept up by the majesty of worship.

But two of the Commandments have no counterpart in Greek or Roman legislation. They recognised an unseen Majesty from which law proceeded; they did not forbid confusing that Majesty with visible things. They recognised sacred seasons tied to the sun and moon; they did not recognise a Week, a division of time resting on some law other than the astronomical. The Week defines human life by its two principles, action and rest, and connects both with the life of God. His rest and action are the patterns of ours, yet He is always at rest while we work and always at work for us while we rest. It joins the Divine to the common while hallowing the distinction between them. It signifies that palace and hut, even master and cattle, come under one government, and it makes each night’s sleep an image of the final repose of spirit, soul and body, each in its proper object. Let anyone consider how the sensual tendencies of pagan worship affected political life. Let him consider the difficulty every philosopher found in reconciling a living, acting God with one resting in his own beatitude, and the greater difficulty of finding any common ground between the philosopher’s thoughts and those of ordinary men, who felt that the God they feared must interfere in all their dealings. Then let him say whether the second and fourth Commandments do not get as much illustration and confirmation from the feeling and conscience of the old world as the sixth or seventh.

Be that as it may, not only some of these institutions but all of them exist among us. The Jewish order of time, so far as the week goes, is now as much part of modern Europe’s institutions as marriage or property. All three may be held in more or less reverence in different places, but every nation of Christendom recognises them and builds them into daily dealings.

On what authority do they rest? Here another difference of opinion begins. The ordinary statesman answers: “They are national provisions of more or less importance, taking their sanction from each nation’s own legislation, and given a useful, artificial sacredness in the eyes of the vulgar by the tradition of a mysterious origin.” The ordinary religious man answers: “They have nothing to do with the merely political or national life of any society. They are religious ordinances appointed by God Himself, binding on all because He appointed them.” With the statesman I agree so far as this: I regard these institutions as belonging emphatically to particular nations as such, to England as England, France as France, Germany as Germany. I do not regard them as universal in the sense in which I have called Baptism and the Eucharist universal, as having nothing to do with space and time. If the religious man objects, I reply that I must make the distinction because Scripture makes it. “I am the Lord thy God” is the sanction of the code, clearly a distinct, national sanction. But I differ from the statesman here: I do not think national society is a lie or stands on a lie. If I did, I should wish it to perish and be sure it must. Unfortunately he does think this. He knows he cannot maintain the institutions he thinks necessary for his nation, and for every nation, by his own rules and conventions alone. He is forced to ask help from the faith men everywhere have had that a Divine ruler governs not only mankind generally but their own commonwealth, and this faith he calls a delusion. I believe the imposition and dishonesty are in him. Mankind’s conviction is safe and honest, and will in the end prevail against all the frauds that have tried to support it and only weakened it.

So in my conclusion I agree with those who take the common religious view. But the difference in our premises is not slight, and it has the most practical consequences. We are told first that we must not view these Commandments merely as parts of a national institute, yet evidently they are. We are told not to receive them as merely outward and formal, since they contain the essence of morality, yet they do have a markedly outward and formal character. Every one of them refers to formal institutions. Only the last touches an inward habit of mind, and the exact detail of “Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour’s wife, nor his man-servant, nor his maid-servant, nor his ox, nor his ass” shows that the habit is noticed for the sake of the act, not the act for the sake of the habit. Far from wanting to overlook this, I want to dwell on it, because I believe it is where the Commandments’ importance lies, and the proof that the new dispensation has not superseded them. They set the Divine seal on what belongs to man as a creature of flesh and blood, living in a particular place, with a definite circle of human relations and earthly associations. They declare these settled by a Divine order and taken under Divine cognizance, and they bring outward, ordinary acts into judgment.

This view seems to spare us some serious confusions. We can enter into the peculiar character of the Jewish nation without losing sight of its connexion with all mankind. We can connect our own lives with those of the people of the old world whom we read about and feel close to, despite differences of race, language, and religious faith and knowledge. Above all, we can be rid of the Manichæan notion (always tied to a low view of the Old Testament) that the visible universe and ordinary social relations are creations of an evil spirit, to be lightly esteemed by those who have perceived a higher economy. This accursed doctrine clings closely to the hearts of us all, and was perhaps never more threatening than today. History gives no reason to believe it will be fully exposed until we acknowledge the sanctity, grandeur and divinity of national life. But if we admit these positions and have reached them this way, other questions press on us. The objectors to the Commandments’ authority based their most plausible argument on the difficulty of separating them from the surrounding history. That difficulty seems slight when we meet records of things with nothing corresponding among us. Assume a nation, take the Jews as the specimen, and you assume hundreds of regulations suited to their condition and so not to ours. But if principles of national life have shown themselves amid these regulations, we must expect the history to illustrate those principles and so unlock facts that bear on our own conduct. And it does. It is an essential part of the idea of a Jewish commonwealth that transgressors be punished and in certain cases put to death. The judicial Oath belongs to the religion of the Jewish state, and every Jew would have understood the third Commandment in reference to it. Still more clearly the Jew was taught to see War, in certain cases, not as permitted licence but as a solemn duty, undertaken in full confidence that it was God’s will and that God would help him through it. These feelings and convictions belonged in the same way to every great nation of antiquity, as nations. They formed part of their religion and were the means of showing the qualities we most admire in them. Courage, self-discipline, order and faith were all tied to the conviction that national life is more precious than individual life, and that hundreds of thousands of individuals are cheaply sacrificed to preserve it. If it is said that these qualities were mixed with opposite ones that also found satisfaction in wars and judicial severity, no sane man will dispute it. What I contend is that they were opposite qualities however intermingled, and so if we hate the one we must love the other, or all moral distinction is effaced. And I say further that if we attach any sacredness to Jewish history as the Divine specimen of a national life, we cannot refuse to believe that the other nations of antiquity were justified in their deep conviction that God has not given swords to men in vain. There are occasions when the magistrate, bound by his allegiance to God, must cut off the offender against the majesty of law, and when the ruler must call on his subjects to punish the removers of landmarks, the corrupters of the earth and the oppressors of mankind.

Even so, this would give us only an historical interest in this part of the Jewish records. What brings them home to us is that every nation of Christendom resorts to judicial Oaths, inflicts Punishments (in some cases capital), and believes War under certain circumstances a duty. So the question becomes weighty: is this meant to be so or not? Does the Old Testament, which we have found a key to the main problems of national life, justify these convictions? It is no use saying Jewish precedent justifies only oaths, punishments and wars undertaken at God’s express command. I have maintained that every nation ought to regard itself as having the Lord for its King. If we do not recognise that principle, the Commandments mean nothing to us, and the institutions they speak of have no authority beyond human convention. On this doctrine, asking what acts a nation ought to do or omit means asking which acts accord with the purpose for which God appointed it. These need not be the acts the Jews performed, since each nation has a different purpose from theirs. But the convictions I speak of are common to all nations as nations. They have been guiding maxims assumed in examining particular cases. Men’s conscience has said: because oaths, punishments and wars are in themselves authorised by God, and can be justified only in the cases and under the conditions He approves, we must earnestly consider what those cases and conditions are. The question is whether this primary assumption is warranted by the book that sets forth the Divine principles of national society. It is idle to ask where the New Testament gives authority for wars, oaths or punishments. I do not find authority in it for any of the distinct institutes of national life. The Manichæans and Anabaptists were right that the New Testament has no distinct precept about property, and that the first sign of a Church was men not calling their goods their own but counting all things common. There was nothing unnatural in their feeling that, since in the resurrection men neither marry nor are given in marriage, the kingdom of Christ, the kingdom of the risen life, must look with some contempt on the union of the sexes. Nor was it surprising that they inferred, from the Gospels’ repeated mention of our Lord’s controversies with the Pharisees over the Sabbath, that the distinction of days was abolished. If their first maxim was true, that we must look to the New Testament for the whole law of man’s life, all their conclusions were inevitable, even where actual passages contradict them. If it can be shown that these things, or any part of our national creed, are denounced in the New Testament, we must of course give them up, even at the cost of painful perplexity about the nature and permanence of moral principles. Whether they are so denounced I shall consider presently. What I maintain now is that the mere absence of the kind of language found on every page of the Jewish Scripture is no more proof that these things do not concern us than a political economist’s silence about moral philosophy proves he disbelieves it.

It will be said that the two cases are not analogous, or only so because writers on political economy ought to take account of moral principles. If the universal and the national society are both meant for man, any book setting forth the character of one must touch the nature and laws of the other. Be it so. I do not deny it, and I especially want the reader to feel its importance. I do not expect to find the principles of the universal society developed in the Old Testament, nor those of the national society in the New. I do expect to find each illustrating and sustaining the other, and under either dispensation signs that they are distinct but inseparable. Such signs force themselves on me in the Jewish records. We have seen how the germs of a universal society were planted in the heart of the Jewish commonwealth: the priest, the sacrifices and the tabernacle testified to what is human and general, as the king, the judge and the law testified to what is peculiar and exclusive. This sounds paradoxical, and to a Jew it would sound very much so. He would say: “Our likeness lay in what you call peculiar, our difference in what you call common.” That is plausible, but I think I have explained why it is not true. The Hebrew was set apart from other peoples so that he might be steward of a possession meant for all. He was cut off to witness to a Being not perceived by the senses, not material, therefore not divided, and not belonging to this or that place. When a member of the nation forgot that He was the Lord God of the Hebrews and tried to break the chain of this peculiar polity, he failed in his function and failed to witness for a God of the whole earth. When he refused to see that his covenant could expand to include all nations, he lost his peculiar position and became a member of a sect instead of a citizen of a kingdom. This is certainly a paradox, but it is the paradox of Scripture, well worth study. So I say there was a human element in the national society, and the ways in which it was joined to the national element yet shown to be distinct were remarkable. The priestly tribe is one of the twelve but is cut off from the rest, placed under particular conditions, and supported by a particular kind of property. From the start of the commonwealth the offices of priest and lawgiver are carefully separated. Both are Divine, but neither may intrude on the other. This is not because the priest’s office is limited to what are technically called religious services. He examines the leper and pronounces whether he may enter the congregation of the Lord, and he distinguishes clean from unclean meats. Whatever involves direct oversight of the internal, in man’s physical or moral life, belongs to the priestly part of the commonwealth. Whatever involves the outward regulation of society, whether it concerns bodily comfort or behaviour, seems to belong to the legal part. Each presents the unseen Lord to the Israelites: the one as a Judge taking account of all their acts, the other as one who spies out all their ways and knows what passes within, dealing not with crimes only but with sins, and able to take away the source of evil as well as its fruit. At the same time, as there is no division between man’s inner and outer life, and none in God’s character as Lord of the outward and the inward world, so there is none in the Jewish economy between the offices that represent Him in these characters. The priest’s sacrifice is needed to hallow the troops the king leads out to battle, and the king takes part in every ecclesiastical reform. We feel that the Jewish commonwealth is one society, not a national body plus an ecclesiastical one, but a body that could not be national if it were not ecclesiastical, nor ecclesiastical if it were not national.

We find the counterpart of these signs in both of the great pagan nations, with a remarkable and characteristic difference. In the heart of each Greek state we may see a priesthood with important functions, but far more striking are the sacred grounds of Elis and the common temple of Delphi. These were signs and pledges of a fellowship between Greeks and Greeks that differences of race, and the antipathies of democratic and aristocratic governments, could not sever. In Rome, on the other hand, priestly influence is built into all the national institutions, and the name of every conspicuous office in the republic recalls the union. That union gave Roman society much of its solidity after the principles it was founded on had been set aside, after the emperor had become “the king of kings and lord of lords,” and his kingdom had turned into one of those Scripture calls Babylonian: resting on mere power, effacing national distinctions, and exalting the visible above the invisible.

We have considered the kingdom of Christ as the direct and formal opposite of this kingdom. So we must now take up the question we raised before examining its signs: does it resemble the Roman world in hostility to national society, or is it meant to be, as it was in embryo, the quickening spirit of national society? We have seen that it had its cradle in a nation, and appeared there when the old spirit had departed, when the Jews were calling themselves Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes and trying to turn their kingdom into a system or a sect. We have seen that the disciples of Jesus at once went back to their countrymen’s old language. They refused to be called a sect of Nazarenes or Christians, proclaimed their Master heir to David’s throne and King of Israel, and declared themselves, and invited all men, to be his subjects.

The Jews understood such a claim, and denounced and persecuted it. To the Romans it seemed utterly ridiculous. The Christians were one of the Jewish sects, perhaps the most extravagant but certainly the least rebellious, and the Proconsuls in the provinces were willing to protect them against their countrymen’s fanaticism.

It was a little different when they were brought face to face with the Cæsars. An instinct seemed to tell Nero that something in their position was incompatible with his, though what it was could not be clearly understood until the Jewish polity had been destroyed. Then this body was seen still to remain, still using language apparently drawn from the old Jewish dreams that had now been so signally refuted. The Christians still spoke of themselves as members of a kingdom. They did not keep the exclusiveness that had made the Jewish pretensions comparatively harmless. They addressed all people in all the provinces, bound their converts into a corporation, and held them together by a strange freemasonry.

The most philosophical emperors were least able to regard such a fellowship with indifference. Out of kindness or contempt they might deal gently with individuals, but the society was an anomaly incompatible with the government’s safety. As that government came closer to a military despotism, the contest with the spiritual society grew sharper, and it became clearer that one or the other must fall. When Rome had become almost Asiatic under Diocletian, the Cross had gained a power that the mild emperor might be inclined to tolerate but the state could not endure. Yet the most vigorous of all the persecutions failed. The new kingdom could not be put down, and under Constantine the eagle did homage to it. Then the Church’s organisation became connected with the empire’s. The civil dioceses coincided with the Bishops’ jurisdictions, in Italy as in the provinces, the ecclesiastical officer gained a civil position, and the emperor exercised some jurisdiction in spiritual affairs.

Here was a worn-out despotism, founded on the acknowledgment of mere power, joining itself to a body founded on the acknowledgment of a King who rules in righteousness, whose strength, in himself and in his disciples, was made perfect in weakness. The Church had not sought the alliance. As a proof of her Master’s dominion she was to receive it with thanks, and as part of God’s dealings with mankind she was to enter it, not as a state that could last but as one that must hasten a new order of things. Moving the centre of government prepared the way for the coming revolution. The Byzantine monarchy might in some respects be considered a new one, arising just when Christianity was recognised and not necessarily founded on the old military maxims. But those maxims were implied in it; there was the same effort, however checked by other influences, after universal dominion. Its break-up by the Mahometan conquests proved that such dominion cannot exist alongside Christianity. In the West the proof was clearer still. The century after Constantine saw the dissolution of a fabric that had stood a thousand years. Charlemagne re-established it as a Christian empire, and in the next generation it was shattered again. Meanwhile the Christian Church was at work on the barbarian tribes. What was the nature of its work? Had the Bishops followed their own notions of what was best, they would of course have made Europe one great society, with a common language and hardly any territorial or political distinctions. Such a dream would have seemed most pious, carrying out the idea of the Divine commonwealth. The history of the Middle Ages abundantly shows that they entertained it, strove at various times to realise it, and found the old Roman jurisprudence a help in the attempt. But how was it defeated? I answer: by the influences which they themselves, acting simply as Churchmen in their appointed vocation and not as agents of a preconceived system, brought to bear on the tribes. The ecclesiastical society was the main instrument in creating within each tribe a distinct national organisation, altogether different from the ecclesiastical though acting in concert with it. By the ecclesiastical or Catholic spirit, the peculiarities of character and intellect in each tribe were developed. The Bishops called themselves an Order, said they had their commission from an invisible King, and said they were a link with generations past and to come. When a chieftain who came into land formerly under Roman government found this order established and submitted to it, he began to think differently of his own office. He thought of it less as conferred by individual strength or cunning, and connected it less with sword and conquest and more with some claim of religion. If these first notions of a government resting on ordinance died in the bud, or were choked by the ruder feelings of power bound up with them, the tribe stayed barbarous, became divided, and was overcome by another more capable of cultivation. If they bore fruit (and this happened sooner when Bishops invaded ground previously pagan than when they converted pagan invaders of their own), the military chief over a district, or the bretwalda over several, became the king anointed with oil, doing homage for his authority to Christ and passing on his kingdom in a hereditary line. He has, of course, his council of chieftains who share with him the soil he has conquered. The Bishops, having greater wisdom and a sacred character, are invited to join its deliberations. Gradually the whole council comes to regard itself as an Order, bound together by a different tie of allegiance to their sovereign than mere fellowship in arms, holding their lands by a different tenure than mere conquest, and recognising relations between themselves and their dependants. Here is the first form of a national society. If it stays at that, it is still only a feudal society, but under the same moral influence it may gradually develop until one portion of the community after another feels itself an order and becomes an integral part of the nation. While this goes on, we find signs that the spiritual society itself has taken a national position. It had first established itself as a missionary body in some great city, which became the home of its Bishop or overseer, and then sent its presbyters into the districts. The Bishops become permanently tied to the cities, and the districts are parishes, recognised by the civil body as connected with an ecclesiastical jurisdiction. The owners of the soil do homage to this arrangement, and part of the produce is set aside for the teacher of the district where it is grown. Individuals’ wealth is freely given to found permanent endowments in particular districts, or for particular districts in diocesan centres. And it is felt more and more that the spiritual officer, as among the Jews, deals with all that is internal, all that lies beyond the sphere of sense. This conviction gives rise to schools and universities: first some enlightened ruler founds them, then some noble or commoner interested in the welfare of a neighbourhood provides funds so that those he cares for may share in the education of the land.

I am not aware that any of these statements applies exclusively to one nation of modern Europe. With different modifications, this is the history of how modern society was formed. The modifications are interesting and important because they illustrate another point I have mentioned: how the characters and institutions of the nations took their distinct form, so that there is far more difference between Englishmen, Frenchmen and Germans now than there can have been between Saxons, Franks and Burgundians twelve hundred years ago. Still, the modifications were only part of the cause. Similar tillage working on different soils will bring out all their latent peculiarities and show what each was meant to bear. This tillage has been carried on by the universal society. As we have seen, it has not acted by its own notion, and it has not cast Christendom into the mould a Churchman would naturally have tried to use. A higher will, another power, has plainly been at work, crossing human calculations. Yet if we trace the history of modern Europe, we see that by some means a witness has been borne to the very constitution which Scripture makes known. The form of national society that the Old Testament invests with such sacredness is reproduced by the New Testament society that seemed to have displaced it. As before a spiritual element proved necessary to uphold a legal society, so now a legal element, a body expressing the sacredness and majesty of law, is shown to be necessary for the objects the spiritual and universal society exists to fulfil. How each is necessary to the other, and what duties each has to perform for the other’s sake, men have constantly asked themselves, and have invented the most opposite answers. Those who have gone with us in our earlier inquiries will feel that this question, far from being obsolete, has never occupied men’s thoughts so much as now. They will feel too, perhaps, that though men’s speculations have done comparatively little, the world’s experience has done much toward answering it. The legal power can no longer help the spiritual by persecuting its enemies, and the spiritual power can no longer help the legal by throwing a fictitious sacredness around it. On the other hand, the spiritual power cannot make men feel that a Being is the Judge and punisher of evil acts unless it can show His authority impersonated somewhere. The legal power tries in vain to convince its subjects that a Being can renew and mould the will unless it can show how that influence is exerted. The Church wishes to make men feel they are subjects, but its own influence especially aims at setting them free. The State wishes to have a free, intelligent people, but has only the power of keeping men servants. If any great work is to be done for man, and God’s gracious purposes toward him fulfilled, one would think these two powers must be meant to act and react on each other continually, learning from each new error their distinct functions and their perfect harmony.
