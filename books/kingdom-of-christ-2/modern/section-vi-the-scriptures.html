Section VI.—The Scriptures

Scripture records a real, developing kingdom, so its canon, its inspired but undictated language, and its miracles all confirm one order, against rationalist dissection and the Romanist habit of hiding the Bible from the laity.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

In an earlier chapter I asked what certain signs of a spiritual and universal constitution meant, signs that appeared when we studied the actual world and its history. We needed help in explaining why such a constitution should exist and yet be acknowledged by men as the exception rather than the rule. We found that help in the documents that make up our Bible. They profess to reveal the Divine constitution for man, revealed first to a particular family, then to a particular nation, and through them to mankind. This revelation is a history. The acts of the family and nation, and the acts by which their possession becomes universal, embody the discovery. The oppositions that arise, outside and inside the family and nation, against the principle they are founded on explain the contradiction between man’s will and the order in which he is placed. They make us aware of two societies, one formed according to God’s order, the other based on self-will.

The Bible declares that this constitution is the true one and will last for ever, and it speaks of a society grounded on it that will last for ever. So we wanted to ask what signs of such a society exist in the world now. We found some that seem to imply it, and we asked whether they match the signs set down in Scripture. We have therefore turned to the Bible for two things: to clear up our difficulties about God’s meaning in His universe and to show how far that meaning is effectual for us today; and to make known both the nature of the order we are placed in and the outward shape of the body that expresses it.

It may seem that the purpose and character of the Scriptures have already been investigated as fully as this book allows, especially since the subject came up while we discussed the opinions of different religious bodies. But any reader will see that if the Bible is what it has seemed to us to be, it must not only interpret the signs of a spiritual and universal kingdom but must itself be one of the most remarkable of those signs. If so, by considering its relation to the other signs we may perhaps resolve some difficulties that much trouble the modern student.

I. (1.) This view of the Scriptures’ general purpose seems to show how particular books were ascertained to belong to them, or to have no claim. To conceive a canon of Scripture is the same as to conceive Scripture itself; if one is necessary, so is the other. If one is formed by human agency, there is no difficulty in supposing the same of the other. Regard the Bible as an isolated thing, and it is hard to see how the authority to fix what it is could belong to anyone not employed in writing it. But look on it as the witness of a permanent kingdom, and as part of God’s plan for building up that kingdom. Then there is surely no difficulty in supposing that wisdom to decide, with all needed practical exactness, which books held the authentic history of this kingdom was given to men whose offices showed that they could not do even their ordinary tasks by any wisdom of their own.

(2.) Looking at the Scriptures as the sign of a spiritual and universal kingdom, we can reconcile several schemes of interpretation that often seem contradictory and exclusive. Some men look for a mystical character, a secret cabbala beneath the letter. Others insist on the strictness and sufficiency of the letter and indignantly reject every hidden meaning as inconsistent with the simplicity of a revelation. But every sign of this kingdom we have considered so far has had this double character: it pointed to a relation that is invisible, mystical, transcendent, while being in itself plain, definite, visible. The relation was real and permanent, so the sign had to be one that did not fluctuate, did not draw its strength from men’s notions, and spoke to all. We would expect the same principle in this further sign: the more simple, accurate and historical the outward clothing, the more it would be felt to embody a higher principle.

Again, there is a great controversy over whether each part of these records has one definite meaning, tied to a particular event and crisis, or may apply remotely to some other crisis, or to a series of undeveloped events. If the Bible is the history of the gradual development of a kingdom fixed on permanent principles, the natural supposition is that it always shows those principles in relation to some present or approaching contingency, yet explains similar contingencies to the end of time. If we refuse to acknowledge the first event, we lose the principle. If we restrict it to some event of our own choosing, we depart from the letter without gaining anything for the spirit. We may be right that the event we fix on falls under the law that the passage makes known; we are almost sure to be wrong in confining the law to that event. These conclusions follow from the belief that the Scriptures are not to be considered apart from the spiritual kingdom.

(3.) This also seems to solve another problem, which many find harder: where are the interpreters of this book to be found? How can it be a lawgiver and yet be subject to the rules of interpretation of those who are its subjects? The difficulty is the same as before. Take the Bible as a solitary fact, the Word of God addressing man, without asking what that Word says man is or what order it says he is placed in, and there must be endless puzzles. In what position, of simple acceptance or earnest inquiry, are such utterances to be received? Is each man to grasp them alone, or do his fellows help him?

But suppose the words speak as they seem to, of men placed in a Divine order and addressed by God within it. Then the words will be realised just so far as we use our position, and missed just so far as we reject it. How Christ’s ministers help us to a practical insight into the facts and principles of this book, and how we are likely to go astray if we dispense with their help, is exactly the difficulty assumed in all education. Or is it more correct to say that here we find the key to the puzzles of ordinary education, since we reach the point where God proclaims Himself the Educator and marks out those through whom He will educate? The ministers may forget their task and stifle their disciples with words and notions instead of calling out their personal life and understanding. That is still the problem of daily life repeated, except that we see more clearly against whom the sin is committed and where the responsibility lies. And the difficulty of men teaching from a book that they admit is above them is just that of every magistrate and judge. He is set to bring out the meaning of the Law he both administers and obeys. He may put himself in its place, read himself into it, or keep it from men instead of guiding them to intelligent submission. But he does so at his peril, and falls under the sentence, if of no earthly superior, then of One who in this spiritual kingdom holds His constant and acknowledged court of appeal.

II. These questions are debated among those who agree that the Scriptures have Divine authority. Let us now consider the objections of those who reject the notion of a Bible altogether, or who see no special reason why the books we hold sacred should have the name.

The objections may be stated like this. ‘Ever since the critical spirit and knowledge of modern Europe were applied to these documents, it has grown harder to maintain the claims made for them by the elder Church and the Reformers. If their inspiration, their paramount authority over all other books, and their fixed and peculiar character are true, then finding any unauthentic record among them, any report that will not bear sifting, even a considerable error in the reading of a text used to support some opinion, must shake the credit of the whole scheme. Those early critics who wished to assert the general authority of the book were honest in beginning these fatal inroads. But if they were not stopped at first, they cannot be stopped now. A principle of criticism admitted for a part must be applied to the whole, and whatever maxim has stood the test with other books must be applied to these. Nor will the evidences thought so conclusive in the last century help us now. The book’s credit was supposed to rest on its recorded miracles, on the agreement of its facts with the general testimony of antiquity, on the admirable character of the four narratives at its centre, and on its ideal truth and consistency. But those miracles are the very stories we require to have accounted for. The testimony of antiquity has been shown only to establish certain habits of thought and feeling in different nations, which themselves account for what was thought peculiar in these records. The four narratives, severely analysed, prove very hard to understand either in their internal history or in their relation to outward events. And the supposed ideal consistency of the whole record has been examined and shown to explain the phenomena of Christianity, but unsatisfactorily for those who see it as embodied in a series of facts.’

This is, I hope, a fair statement of the objections now current in all parts of society. Even when they do not appear as a complete system of arguments, they are all the more effective, because they suggest that much has been left unsaid which would be conclusive if it could safely be uttered.

I begin by admitting that I do not see how those who look on the Scriptures merely as documents contrived to instruct individuals, a witness to each man of what has been done for him and of God’s plans for him, can meet some of these arguments. I do not mean they cannot meet them practically, and in what seems to me an honest and effective way. If they resolutely hold fast what they have felt and found true in their own lives, and say, ‘This we have learnt and received; the Bible taught it us, and we cannot give it up for any arguments,’ I believe their position is safe and impregnable. It is not prejudice but reasonable and sound, founded on the first and wisest maxim of ethical philosophy: keep what you have; add to it if you can; but never let anything you have realised be snatched from you. My fear is that few in our day will be content with this. They will leave it with their arguments and evidences, trying to prove how and why a book of the character they impute to the Bible must be Divine and perfect. Here I think they will be defeated. This logic is no part of their realised truths but something extraneous. Worse, they do not yet know what they are arguing about. They may have drawn those individual facts from the Bible, but the Bible itself plainly assumes to be something else, a collection of historical documents. The question is how that assumption connects with the quality they have discovered in it.

Are we then to hope that those who are willing to treat it mainly as a collection of historical documents, and defend it as such, will hold their position? I think such writers will bring forward much that is valuable, and that their opponents can answer only with great difficulty and some dishonesty. They will do more: they will leave on thoughtful and sincere minds the impression that there are facts in the world now, and there has been a series of such facts, which these books may explain. But here again the difficulty is to find how these facts cohere, how they relate to the doctrines and principles these books embody, and why we must suppose any Divine oversight in arranging and preserving them.

Are we then to say, as the objector claims we must, that criticism is wholly inapplicable to these records; that they must be taken for granted on some authority, whether primitive antiquity or the Church today; and that all further inquiry is to be discarded? There is plausibility in this opinion, and more than plausibility: a truth is hidden in it that we must not deny. As long as we receive the Scriptures at all, we must take them for granted in educating our own minds and our children’s. We cannot begin by being critics or by making them critics. If we do, we and they will be miserable critics and nothing else. But do we not treat other books the same way? We take them for granted too; in merely reading or teaching them we do not criticise their sources or the conditions of their authority. There comes a time, however, when other books are put to this trial, and it has been God’s will that the book we consider pre-eminently His should be put to it also. It is a solemn question whether we shall dare to pretend that we will take better care of His book than He has, or affirm that it cannot bear tests that we believe ordinary literature can bear.

This brings us to the main question I wish to put to the reader: what has been the character of the criticism to which the Scriptures have mostly been subjected during the last century and a half? I do not ask whether it has been sound, learned or devout; it may have been any or all of these. I ask what its object has been. For the last century the safest answer comes from the object of all criticism then, whether concerned with body or soul, the universe or its creatures. Nearly every philosopher thought it his life’s business to analyze: the operations of the mind, himself, his fellow-creatures, the being of his Maker. Was all this labour wasted? I say no. I believe much was learnt. Many false notions and phantoms that men had transferred from themselves to the objects of their study were got rid of, and many idols were thrown down, broken and trampled, which had beset the caves of thoughtful men and the market-places of busy men. But the great lesson this method left us was its own utter incapacity to lead to the apprehension of any truth, though it might defeat some error. So every step toward real, profitable inquiry in our day, in physics or metaphysics, has been a step out of this method, toward investigating the powers and principles of things as they exist, and not, except for subordinate purposes, toward reducing them to elements. Above all this change has come in literature. There the analytical spirit of the last age showed its full power: every book was cut into elements, whatever elements the critic disliked were cast out as worthless, and nothing was done to study a book as a whole or to discover the purpose that informed it. The Scriptures were treated the same way. That they form a whole, felt as a whole by innumerable minds for many centuries, was more and more overlooked as unimportant to the critic and philosopher. He could not deny they had a common name, but his business was to show what separate items made it up, and to pursue his inquiries with as little reference to it as possible. Of course it was part of the ordinary philosophy of the time that everything in the book speaking of invisible powers should be explained away. The aim was to discover how many of its elements could be kept without infringing the period’s maxims in physics, metaphysics and ethics.

About these inquiries I say, as I said of all others from the same period, that I do not believe they were useless or will ultimately be mischievous. The student of seventeenth-century physics sees a multitude of strange theories and superstitions accumulating that needed some whirlwind to sweep them away. The student of theology must likewise confess that a number of hard, dogmatic abstractions about spiritual objects, and not least about the books that treat of them, were darkening the heavens and making men’s path along their common earth less clear. We might conjecture that some fiery process would be needed to destroy these, though we could not judge what kind. God ordained that it should be this destructive analysis, and we cannot doubt that what He appointed was best. Many obstructions to perceiving what is real and substantial have been cleared from the young theologian’s path, and it is his own fault if he seeks them again. He may, if he will, be less entangled than his forefathers in the abstractions and conceits of the intellect. And here, as in the other cases, the analysts have given us this great blessing: they have proved their method too feeble to explain any one living fact or lead to any one important discovery.

So when the objectors say the Bible ought to be tried by the same rules as other books, we can perhaps go a good way with them, provided we understand what they mean. I believe it always will be tried by the same standard as other books; the habits of mind we cultivate toward one we shall cultivate toward the other. When all books are cut up into elements, the Bible will be too. When books, and the whole literature of a nation, are considered in relation to their principle or idea, the Bible must be studied on the same principle. Then the question is what this principle or idea is. We have considered the view, so prevalent now, that tries to separate the idea from the event, making the one common to all ages and the other its accidental, temporary clothing. I have tried to show how inadequate this doctrine is to the phenomena of history. It turns living ideas into mere notions of our minds, and so legalizes and stamps with authority the very superstitions it seeks to remove. It confounds the permanent and the transitory in trying to distinguish them, and destroys human progress in trying to assert it. If this modern idealism could be entirely separated from the old analytical method that it professes to supersede and despise, we could easily prove the insufficiency of both. The chief strength of each lies in a vague notion that one is the expansion and full development of the other, and in a loose impression that the belief in inspiration, miracles and a Gospel history, partly subverted by the one, has been completely subverted by the other.

The facts, especially in recent German history, which prove that the ideal system could not have arisen without a vehement religious protest against the analysts, are unknown or ignored. We are asked what hope there is of keeping our obsolete notions of a Divine order and a Divine book when each age has furnished its own refutation of them. Our answer is: ‘No hope at all, if what you call our notions are only notions, and are not founded on eternal principles and truths. But the belief that they have this foundation is strengthened, not weakened, by the history of these attempts to confute them. It is strengthened by the fact that no adequate answer to the particular charges against the Bible has been offered, except by those willing to speak of it as it seems to speak of itself, as the revelation of a Divine kingdom.’

1. Looking at it in this light, I ask first what difficulties lie in the old notion that the writers of the book were inspired men. On the principle of a spiritual kingdom, inspiration is not a strange anomaly but the proper law of the world. No one ought to write, speak or think except in acknowledgment of an inspiration, and no one can really do any of these without one. Does the habitual confession of divine teaching by these writers, their referring everything to God, brand them as impostors? Would it not rather seem the mark of true men? You answer that it is the mark of fanatics, and ask where I draw the line. I draw it here. A man doing the work he is set to do may believe he is inspired with power for that work. A man doing some other work he is not set to do may say he uses powers received from above, but he is violating the purpose for which they were given, and his will obeys an impulse contrary to the will of the Giver. That is fanaticism and confusion. So the real question is not “Were the writers of Scripture inspired by God?” but “Were they in a certain position, appointed to a certain work?” The argument drives us, as the book itself does, from what we read to what we read of. Was there such a society as the book speaks of? Was there such a nation as the Jews? Had they a history, and a meaning in it, and does this book explain both? The question of inspiration belongs with these and cannot be viewed apart from them. If there is no spiritual kingdom and no kings, priests or prophets appointed by God, I cannot show that the Scriptures had a right to describe them. If there were such men, I find it just as hard to see how we could do without such a record, or how anyone but He who formed the society for his own glory and his creatures’ good can have caused that book to be written.

It will be objected that this evades the difficulty. On the common theory, it is not merely the men but the words that are inspired, and every milder theory that denies verbal inspiration or dictation is inconsistent and a mere subterfuge. But two words are being used as synonyms that mean very different things. Verbal inspiration is a phrase I dislike, since it seems to rest on a violent and scarcely grammatical ellipsis. Still, I fully accept the meaning I suppose to be latent in it. I cannot imagine inspired thoughts that do not find suitable words, and I can scarcely separate a writer’s language from his meaning, least of all in a book of the Bible. Its peculiarities of language seem to me strangely significant. Yet its greatest peculiarity, if I may be pardoned the solecism, is its universality: it can be translated into any dialect that has a living, human quality and is not merely the echo of passing impressions and animal needs. But because I see this link between the inbreathed thought and the spoken word, I must reject the notion of dictation as monstrous and heretical. None is more at variance with the letter and spirit of Scripture. If any hint of it exists, it is in the giving of the divine Code, which was a formal, literal document and so is shown to come formally and literally from its Author. Yet see how carefully we are warned against the notion, natural to the sensual and idolatrous heart, that Moses was a mere mechanical utterer or copyist. Why are we told he went into the thick darkness? Why do we hear of his forty days of communion, and read of his sympathy with his people, his prayers, meditations and murmurings? It is so that we may understand the human privilege of spiritual intercourse and see it as the great privilege of the most honoured seer. That is the object of all Scripture: to withdraw us from outward, sensual impressions of the Divine Majesty and make us feel that our relation to him is real and spiritual, and therefore able to show itself in outward words and acts. Rejecting dictation is thus no concession to the Rationalist but a necessity of our own faith. It remains for him to show how different readings in manuscripts, the rejection of books now counted canonical, or even historical inconsistencies and mistakes in the inspired writers, would affect our belief. The more sacred we feel the language to be, the more we want to find what it really is. If there is no Bible, such investigations are idle; if there is, they must be most interesting. They are healthy mental exercise, if rightly conducted, since they require reflection on the whole mind and scope of the text, calm use of judgment, and faith that truth exists and is willing to reveal itself. Again, anyone who believes there is a book whose distinct office is to explain the nature and conditions of a kingdom he has actually been brought into must ask in the same simple, honest spirit whether any of its documents is genuine. The kingdom exists. He is not afraid of losing it, or his place in it, even if God chose to take the book away. But he has no fear that God will, and no doubt that it is his good pleasure to tell us what he is and what we are. That the book has revealed these truths to himself and to thousands for generations he is certain. Whether a particular portion was necessary to the rest he is willing to consider reverently, not doubting that He who upholds the kingdom and gave the book will not let any intruder remain in it or any integral part be taken away. Does this admission open the door to unlimited scepticism, since nearly every book has come under some modern suspicion? The answer, I think, has been given already. Let those talk about interpolations in Shakspeare who know what Shakspeare is. I do not care that Theobald or Pope judged certain passages unsuited to their taste, and I care just as little for the taste of the analysts or modern idealists about passages or books of Scripture. Their taste is no law of criticism, and I think it a very low and bad taste. Let them bring external evidence and we will weigh it earnestly and impartially, though cautiously, because their taste mingles with their words and they assume a maxim we utterly dissent from as if it were part of a fact we may grant. I am not afraid of touching one corner of the edifice for fear the rest will fall; I believe it will prove much firmer than we have supposed. The books that even religious men threw aside at the Reformation, because they seemed to have no direct bearing on individual life, will be found to carry their own evidence when they are seen as meant to develop the order and life of the spiritual kingdom.

The same principle applies to alleged mistakes or inconsistencies in the admitted parts of the records. Suppose such errors prove the writers dishonest, or touch some part of the revelation of God’s character or the development of his kingdom. Then they fall under the last case, and count, to that extent, against the genuineness of the document containing them. Suppose instead they are merely incidental to the narrative, affecting neither the meaning of the facts nor the writer’s integrity, or are correctable by comparison with another account of the same events. Then I have no right, in advance, to say from the existence of a Bible that there will be none. I see no promise to that effect. I see no reason why God may not have chosen to teach us by this very means: letting the ordinary differences of opinion and eyesight among witnesses help us study the real character of a fact, and letting the usual confusion over details lead us away from them to what is real and substantial. I can see no reason in advance, either in the nature of a Bible or in the meaning of inspiration, why this may not be so. I even fancy I see reasons in the analogy of God’s dealings, and in man’s tendency to dwell on the minute details of an event for their own sake rather than to find its meaning, why such a discipline may suit us best. If it turns out otherwise, I will acquiesce readily, but my faith in the Bible will be no less than it is now. For the Bible will not let me have faith in it apart from faith in God, and whatever I find to be his way of training me and my race I hold to be the right way, by which we may be trained to all goodness and truth. I do not want to lay down a chart of the road we ought to be led by; that would be presumption.

On this showing, the charge of departing from the ordinary rules we apply to other books applies far more to those who make it than to us. We claim that the Bible should be looked at as a fact, a most pregnant fact in human history. It stands apart from other books, and we want to know why and what distinguishes it, just as we want to know what distinguishes the writings of Cicero from other writings or Roman literature from other literature. If we find in it what entitles it to be called, as it commonly has been, The Book, or the Book of Books, we show no contempt for books but a high regard for them. We declare that there is a book which directly and formally connects letters with the life of man and the order of God. Just so far as it fulfils its idea, it becomes the key by which all books may be interpreted, translating them into significance and fixing the value and position of each. We also declare the temper in which a book should be studied. Nothing seems to me more preposterous than the notion that we can change our habits of mind as we turn from one subject to another. To suppose we can be reverent at one moment and irreverent at another, humble before one person and proud with another, is a flagrant violation of ethics, a tampering with our moral being. I therefore agree with the Rationalist that it is neither practical nor reasonable to speak, as some do, of a right to sit in judgment on all other books and a duty to submit our judgment to the Bible. If we think our proper position in reading Cicero or Shakspeare is that of judges, we shall not think otherwise when we study the Scriptures. And while we fancy ourselves judges of Cicero or Shakspeare, we shall not understand them. The posture of children or learners is the profitable one in every case. It is not safe to aim at being a judge in any case, for our minds generally, and most of all for the judging faculty itself. That faculty is turned awry at first, and blunted and stupefied afterwards, if it is not sent to school and does not carry through life the docility that school is meant to give. But what is the schooling? We all know how hard docility is to acquire, in every age and especially our own. Some books tempt us to the proud, condemning spirit; feebleness is stamped on them, they claim a right to judge others, and we feel we may safely indulge our propensity. Hence the wisdom of the old notion that only the best books, those carrying a kind of authority, should be set before boys. Once drilled by them in deference and humility, they may venture, if their calling requires it, on the worst, for they will then have the true discerning spirit, of which the judging spirit is the counterfeit. The one perceives the real quality of the food offered; the other merely sets up its own partial, immature likes and dislikes as the standard of good and evil. But experience shows even this is not enough. Starting from the meanest books, modern critics have gone on to higher ones and asked why they may not exercise private judgment on one as much as another, and pronounce sentence on Herodotus or Livy as on any modern compilation. The Rationalist goes a step further and asks why not on your so-called inspired books too. It is good that the question is put, because it brings matters to an issue. It lets us say: you wanted to be rid of these inspired books because you said they affronted other literature; learn that they are the needed protectors of other literature. If there is a book of which we can say, “Here God is speaking to you; be silent and listen,” we can keep down the saucy, insolent temper that makes whatever it touches unintelligible. We can cultivate our judgment because we can make it not a private judgment. Private judgment means the judgment of a man cut off from his fellows, a savage’s judgment. A man has a right to it if by right you mean the power to exercise it, for he has the power to be a savage. He can say, I will not be educated or led out of my own partial apprehensions and those of my age; I will not be a reasonable being, I will not be a man. But he may also, if he will, be taken under the training and discipline of God himself, to be led out of private judgment into knowledge of the mind of Him who weighed the mountains in scales and the hills in a balance. He may have a book set before him that unfolds the scheme and purpose of the universe and its Creator, and be led slowly to understand his own connexion with that universe, with those who have lived in it, and with its Author. If he will be taught from this book, every other book he reads becomes part of the same divine institution, and God trains him by that too. He learns to trace the course of God’s government, and to see how different men, consciously or unconsciously, gladly or unwillingly, have been carrying out some part of his designs for his creatures. So he descends through the different grades of thoughtful inquirers, each humbly seeking in his own field the meaning of their Lord, until he comes to the lowest point, the latest new criticism or the latest tavern speech defending the inalienable right of men to think what they like. Even there he will find something true, something that could not have been said if there were no Bible in the world.

2. This view of the relation between the Scriptures and other books may help with the question of miracles. The analyst of the last century held that the miracles could be traced to natural causes. The idealist of our day holds that they are inventions, witnessing to the belief in something supernatural that men have because they have spiritual faculties. Both agree that miracles are stumbling-blocks, not helps, to belief in the doctrine and facts of Scripture. I cannot regret that either opinion was put forward. Unphilosophical as I think them, they may clear our minds of a great confusion and help us reach a great truth. We have been used to calling miracles the chief evidences of Christianity. If that means that a miracle or prodigy, as such, proves the divine commission of whoever performs it, we have the strongest reason to reject it, for the Bible commands us to. We dare not believe anything merely because something that strikes us as a departure from ordinary experience or law is done to confirm it; Scripture warns us we shall see such wonders and must beware of being deceived by them. Again, the Bible is above all a book of laws, explaining the divine order of the universe; if it is not that, it is nothing. Can we suppose that violations of law and infringements of order would be its great signs and witnesses? It is not surprising, then, that the analyst is eager to resolve the miracles into natural causes, that is, to connect them with the general order of the world. Yet the other class of rationalists is also right that in all times and ages men have felt after a power not bound by the rules of ordinary visible experience, superior to them and able to transgress them. He is right that the acknowledgment of such a power lies deep in the human heart, and that we keep demanding instances and proofs of it. Both assertions are true; the difficulty is to reconcile and apply them.

Suppose the Bible were the revelation of a spiritual kingdom or constitution of man such as we have described, the history of a divine power redeeming man from a slavery he brought on himself. Then I think we may see at least a dawn of light on this controversy. In the portents Livy records, I do find signs of a feeling in men’s minds that there is something supernatural, that the powers of the world are not all that men have to deal with. But the feeling contradicts itself in trying to express itself, since it does homage to the powers of nature in the very act of seeming to rise above them. Under such a system there could be no liberty for the human spirit and no brave investigation into the mechanism or energies of the universe. Before these blessings could be gained, man’s sense of the supernatural must be justified and purified. He must know that when he dreams of something above himself, he dreams of a reality; that nature is not what he is dreaming of; that he is not to tremble before nature but to claim the dignity of a spiritual creature, to understand and subdue it. He must know he is not the victim of blind natural agents, and must have more than a vague conception of the power he serves. The miracles of the Bible may have that effect on him. Every one recorded in the Old Testament is recorded expressly as a witness that Jehovah, the I am, the personal God, the Lord of the spirits of all flesh, is King of the world, and that gods of sense are not its kings. Every miracle in the New Testament is recorded expressly to show that the Son of man is Ruler of the winds and the waves, sustainer and restorer of animal life, healer and tamer of the human spirit; and that those who are God’s adopted children in him are, while doing his work, not servants of visible things but their rulers. These miracles say to the poor man: “Do not fear the mighty, unfathomable ocean, for your King and Friend has calmly walked upon it. Do not fear disease, for he who took your flesh has mastered it. Do not fear the more terrible powers that master the understanding and heart, for Christ has also cast out devils. Walk boldly over this earth as free men united to this triumphant Deliverer. Dread only separation from him, and the failure to trust and cling to him enough, which would let you sink again under the bondage to nature and death from which he bought you by his life and death.” And they speak also to the better-taught man, according to his needs and temptations. He does not so much need raising above natural fear of outward things, since a calculation of probabilities or a habit of facing difficulties may easily give him that victory. But the very means of his deliverance become occasions of fresh bondage. He acquires a drowsy, dull sense of an ever-moving system of chances. He does not idolize the powers of nature, but he worships its evenness and persistency. This is a wretched and degrading faith, far more to be loathed than the living, half-human idolatry of the peasant. Yet unless there is some demonstration that spiritual power is superior to mechanical, that the world is subject to God and not to chance or nature, and that there is an order far more beautiful than that of sun and stars in which men are meant to live and in which all that is great and noble in them is fully developed, I do not see how this materialist superstition can fail to become every nation’s creed and to bring decay on all institutions, political life, feeling, affection and hope. With the other faith, men have been able to pursue physical science. The world has appeared to them as a solemn subject of study, not a tyrant to bow to. They have learned that the customary links connecting a fact with its highest principle may be suspended to make that principle manifest. So they have risen above the slavish notion that sensible experience is the law to which things are subject, and have been able to defy it. Not only the astronomer but every chemist who has truly investigated the powers of material things has moved in this line, humbly asking nature her secrets and receiving answers that are most satisfactory yet most contrary to our sensible expectations.

If we are asked why we reject the analyst’s doctrine of these miracles, we answer that accepting it would mean refusing, not acknowledging, the true order of the world. We do not believe the world is governed by natural causes, and we do not know what the phrase means. We confess, and rejoice to confess, that there is a habitual, appointed course of things; that each agent, voluntary or involuntary, has its place in the scheme; and that no link of it will ever be needlessly broken. But we say no dishonour is done to these agents when He who assigned their places keeps them in their relation to each other, gives them their powers, and withdraws the veil that hides himself as the prime worker. In so doing he explains the meaning of his ordinances, the secret of their efficiency, and the reason of their abuse. In this sense we say the miracles are evidences of the truth of Christianity. If Christianity is the manifestation of a spiritual kingdom, the satisfaction of the dreams of past ages, and the exhibition through all later complications of the law that governs them and the Giver of that law, then we cannot see how it could enter the world without miracles, or how those miracles could fail to be such as the Bible says they were.

Note. Every reader of the Gospels will have seen that our Lord’s signs did not satisfy the rulers of the Jews. They wanted another kind of sign, “signs from heaven,” glaring tokens the eye could recognize, not “powers” exercised on the inner man and calling out an answering effort of spirit. Our Lord’s words express the difference beautifully: it is a sinful and adulterous, or sense-bound, generation that seeks such signs.

3. The records of these miracles are so integral a part of the Gospel narratives that in speaking of the one I have necessarily anticipated much I would have said of the other. The real difficulty men have felt in studying them is this: how can we accept narratives reporting such strange stories as true without higher evidence than almost any other writing needs? Once this suggestion is made, every difficulty harmonists have had in fitting the different narratives together, every doubt about the authorship of any of them, and every question about a common source has encouraged scepticism. It has been said that, far from there being more evidence for these stories, there seems to be less than for the other parts of the record.

Our last inquiry seems to lead to these conclusions. Either the strange stories are in accordance with the scriptural idea of the Founder of a spiritual and universal kingdom, or they are not. If not, no evidence could establish the authenticity of the document containing them, for they would be self-contradictory, and we should be bound to reject them because we believe in Jesus Christ the Son of God. If they are in accordance, we should need evidence to account for their omission from any record claiming to give the history of such a person. We should have a right to ask why he gave no signs that he came to connect the visible with the invisible world, why he did nothing to break the yoke of custom and experience or to show men that the constitution he claimed to reveal has a true foundation. Take away the miracles, and these records have an inexplicable chasm and inconsistency that would take a vast amount of wit to explain.

Note. We are sometimes told that Mahomet understood the true nature of miracles far better than the Apostles, since he said that the growing corn, the rising sun, and life and death were miracles. Very likely he did, and they were fine words. But what effect have they had? Have his best disciples, even in the highest days of Arabian culture, risen above the prejudices and illusions of sensible experience? Still, the difference is important. It shows how closely the kind of miracles recorded in Scripture belongs to the idea of the Incarnation. Rejecting that idea, Mahometans rightly reject the signs and forms that connect the visible with the invisible world, and there is the secret of their slavery.

When this difficulty is surmounted, the other perplexities will not, I believe, be very serious. The reader of the Gospels is then no longer haunted by the thought, “I wish that story were away; I wish I could have the morality of these discourses and of Jesus’s life without being asked to acknowledge something marvellous.” He has seen that the marvel belongs essentially to that morality. First, he will see that the failure of harmonists to bring the facts of the different Gospels into connexion or chronological order has nothing to do with the books themselves. They exist, they have exercised a wonderful influence on the world in their present form, and they have made known the same living Person. If they could be fitted together, or contrived as supplements to each other, I do not see that they would serve their purpose better. I am willing to listen to those who say they have found such an agreement, and to believe them if they make their point. But I am not in the least troubled when they fail.

A revelation to men of their Lord and King must be altogether wonderful, and I could not conjecture beforehand how its records would be composed. They might have come as annals. That is not generally the form from which we learn most about a character, since the story of a few days or hours often shows it more clearly, but it might have suited such an occasion. But suppose another form has apparently been adopted. I have a set of documents presenting a life in many different circumstances, all human and intelligible, the wonder lying not in them but in him who acts through them and in the meaning he shows to be latent in them. I can compare these documents and learn more of the meaning of the life by seeing how the events appeared to different men. Then I am not anxious to disturb this order in favour of another that I think would suit us much less. Second, the existence of these documents is not affected by any theory about their authorship or construction. Omit all the names we associate with them, or say the names point to no known persons, or that these were no eye-witnesses but reporters of a current tradition, or that they all drew on a common tradition, or that they communicated with one another or did not. Still the books exist, and the secret of their influence, and the strange impression men have that they show a real being, the Lord of man and the image after which he is made, must still be explained, with one of these hypotheses or without. Each may be worth considering, may mean something and teach something; but if all prove untenable, the marvel is not got rid of. To that the theological student must address himself. He must look it fairly in the face and confess that all his criticism must be preceded by the acknowledgment that there is something to criticise. He may be tempted to turn from the commonplace remark that these records strangely lack the contrivances by which men usually build up a hero. The divinity the writer believes he is exhibiting shows itself not occasionally but habitually in the simplest and lowliest forms of human life, and no narrative is an exception. Not once in all these traditions, handed down by affectionate, credulous, ignorant disciples, is there an attempt to prove their Master’s heavenly origin by linking him with outward greatness. The repetition of such remarks may be dull, because books of evidence forced them on us and we learned them by rote before they came to us naturally as characteristics of what we were reading. Yet they are true, and no thoughtful reader can pass them over, any more than a peculiarity in the style of an ancient classic. So there is a growing conviction among the more intelligent sceptics that some scheme must be found to explain these difficulties. The whole history must be accounted for, not by fault-finding with details, but by dividing the person it speaks of into his historical and his mythical attributes. This is the experiment I have so often noticed. It may be applied to the Gospel narratives, but unless it solves all the facts about a spiritual and universal kingdom that we have been considering, it is worth nothing. Show that there is no such constitution for mankind, and you have refuted the Gospels, which are built on the assumption that there is. But if there is such a kingdom, we must know who is its Head and Lord and how he established it. These Gospels have given men that knowledge for many centuries. We believe they have much more to tell us that we have not been able to receive, but which the events of our time and these very controversies will make known. Feeling that they are given us and have deep reality in them, we cannot be unwilling to have them scrutinized. If anything is in them that was not meant to be there, we doubt not it will be brought to light, and that He who brings it will make his own truth clearer by the discovery. But that they contain what no other books in the world contain, and no other parts of Scripture contain, a key to all that is written elsewhere, we believe has been and will be made only more evident by the questions raised about them in these and former days.

The Romish System.

I have argued that a Bible without a Church is inconceivable. The Church’s appointed ministers are the appointed guides into a knowledge of the Bible. Private judgment is a false notion. Inspiration belongs to the Church, not merely to the writers of the Bible. The New Testament miracles introduced a new dispensation and were not just strange acts belonging to one time. And the Gospel narratives must be received as part of the Church’s necessary furniture. Is there not an obvious tendency toward Romanism in these positions? Do they not all belong to the system I have denounced? Let us consider.

1st. I have supposed that the Bible and the Church interpret each other. The Church exists as a fact, and the Bible shows what that fact means. The Bible is a fact, and the Church shows what that fact means. What I complain of in Romanism is that it has overlooked how these two parts of God’s scheme relate. It hid the Bible from men on purpose, so that the Church might be exalted. And it proved that the Church could not be exalted while the Bible was hidden. With no book to explain to the whole body its own position, that position became unintelligible. Men did not know what it was to be Churchmen, because they could not learn it from this book and nothing else could tell them.

2ndly. So my notion of the minister’s powers differs from the Romanist’s. He thinks the minister has power and commission to hide the Bible from the laity. I think the minister has power and commission to lay it open to them. Everyone has an appointed work. When we refuse our own, or do not acknowledge another’s different appointment, we miss some good that was meant for us. So I do not think the laity will understand the Bible if they reject the teaching of their appointed ministers. Nor do I think a minister can be a true teacher, or exercise the powers God has given him, if he puts himself in the place of the Bible and does not encourage the laity to read it and digest it.

3rdly. I believe the Bible and the Church were intended to raise men out of their private judgments, and to guide each person who will be guided into the truth meant for all. The Romanist claims an authority for the Church against private judgments. But it is not an authority that calls forth men’s spirits and draws them out of the narrow circle of their private experiences and conclusions. It is an authority that crushes the exercise of their spirits and keeps them from freedom. So this authority has itself become the tool of private judgments. Half the inventions of Romanism are private judgment’s: either the priest’s condescension to the narrow feelings and judgments of his people, or the creations of his own judgment. Both show the need of the universal law and standard by which both should have been tried.

4thly. The presence of the Spirit, the source of all inspiration, in the whole body of the Church, and in each member so that he may fill his own appointed place, is involved in the idea of our baptism. If we disbelieve it, we acknowledge no Church at all. Because of this gift, we believe every member has a capacity to understand the high privileges won for him, and that the ministers can educate their flocks to grasp them. Our complaint against the Romish system is that it will not let us act on the faith of this inspiration. It supposes inspiration is given to certain persons at certain times, for the sake of startling effects. It supposes an inspiration residing somewhere in the Church to settle what men must and must not hold, to keep down questioning, and to fix the sense of Scripture. But an abiding Spirit, who guides into all truth and tolerates no falsehood, and who can open the Scriptures to different ages according to their different needs — that Spirit, that inspiration, it will not let us recognize.

5thly. Our difference on miracles is also easy to understand. The Romanist asks: if you recognize miracles as connected with a spiritual kingdom, and not merely with a certain book, why will you not recognize the miracles we believe in? Why not suppose they may occur in the nineteenth century as well as the first? I answer that I neither affirm nor deny anything about how often, or in what periods, God may have suspended the workings of intermediate agents to show that He is always their Author and Mover. That must be settled by careful study of historical evidence, and I would be very sorry to dogmatize about the result. Those who believe miracles assert order and do not violate it, and prove the constant presence of a spiritual power rather than its occasional interference in the world, will be the least inclined to expect such signs often. They hold that what was recorded as fact in earlier ages becomes law in ours. We are to own Him who healed the palsied man in every cure the ordinary physician works, and Him who stilled the storm on the Lake of Gennesareth in the guidance and safety of every ship that crosses the ocean. That effect would be lost if we were led to despise what is daily and habitual. Still, it would be presumptuous to say that in modern history it has never been needful to break the idols of sense and experience by the method sanctioned in old days. Far less would I underrate the piety, or criticize the wisdom or honesty, of men who, missing or overlooking intermediate powers they knew little about, referred what they witnessed straight to its primary source.

But these admissions only make me reject, all the more solemnly, at least nineteen-twentieths of the miracles recorded in Romanist books of later times. We need not collate the evidence. Usually there is very little to collate, and where the evidence seems best and most respectable there is a grand preliminary objection. I dare not believe such miracles because I believe the miracles of the New Testament. Scripture expressly tells me there are miracles I am not to believe, which should make no impression on me at all. I do not want to enter into the honesty or dishonesty of those who report them; that is between their consciences and their Creator, and they best know whether they are lying for God. But the character of a miracle determines my judgment of it. Is it to lead me to worship the Visible or the Invisible? Is it to deliver me from sensible things, or make me their slave?

Does the Romanist advocate say I have no right to ask these questions? I know he does, and I will tell him why. He says so because a secret root of unbelief lies in his mind, a secret doubt whether anything is true, which finds refuge in the thought that everything may be true. This is a very common tendency today, the natural reaction against the scepticism of the last century. Many men in France and Germany, and perhaps as many in England as in either, have passed or are passing, not through gradual stages but per saltum — at a leap — from universal doubt to universal credence. They carry the same habits of mind into both, and can tell themselves, complacently and with some truth, “We have not really changed. We acknowledge no standard now any more than before. We have only substituted the new why not? for the old why?” It does not surprise us to hear such men, who twenty years ago would have laughed at us for believing in the raising of Lazarus, now fiercely denouncing anyone who doubts the miracles in the Tyrol. The argument “where are you to stop?” was the one they used in their contemptuous manhood, and it still seems conclusive to them in an old age just as contemptuous and just as sceptical. We can only repeat that we stop when we arrive at the exact contradiction of what we have believed. We have received our Lord as the Great Deliverer, who led our captivity to sense captive. We stop when we meet people who would bring us back into that captivity.

The Bible, we believe, is meant to cultivate in us a habit of distinguishing, and used faithfully and humbly it does so. If you who have not used it or believed in it show that you have not acquired that habit, we have only one more reason to give thanks that God has not hidden the blessing from us or our children.

6thly. These last considerations apply remarkably to the Gospel narratives. It is said: “The Church preserved these histories of our Lord’s life, and you receive them on her authority. You know little about their writers, and accept them because they are given you as part of the canon. But the Church has also sanctioned many histories of the saints and thinks them profitable for her children. Granted they concern lesser persons and can never matter as much as the Gospels, where do you draw the line? You admit Church authority in the highest case; why not in a lower?” I answer that by God’s providence, through His Church, these records of its Lord and Head have been preserved. They were preserved for many great purposes, but especially that there might be a standard in the world by which all other acts and lives may be tried. Take the Gospels out of the canon and leave only epistles stating spiritual principles, and the principles lose their meaning for want of a true personal object to refer to. Worse, this end is wholly lost: no character is set before men showing the image after which they were formed, in connexion with the life of this earth. If the Church has preserved these books for me and told me their purpose, I am not obeying her when I lose sight of that purpose, or when I fail to bring all other books and lives to this standard. I do not do so to condemn them or find what is wrong in them; I have no commission or power for that. I do it to see how far I may safely follow them. If I find records of men who all profess this image as the one they should be conformed to, I am bound to admire gratefully every feature of their lives caught by reflection from it. I may often judge wrongly. I may mistake a bad copy for a good one, or disown a true one because I lack the spiritual cultivation to understand its form and colouring. Still, the more I study the original under the guidance given me, the more I must believe and hope that I will gain the faculty of telling the true from the counterfeit. And I must look to the Church to help me, to keep teaching me how to see the traces of the Divine model in the human imitation, and what in it came merely from the accidents of the time, or from human self-will and frailty.

I believe the Church, holding the Bible in her hand, can give her faithful disciples such help. My charge against the Romish system is that it has hindered the Church from exercising this prerogative and forced her to exercise a very different one. What I mean is best seen from how this system has used the word “Saint.”

The Gospels teach me, and the Church in all ages teaches me, to acknowledge our Lord as one who perfectly identified himself with humanity, with all its sorrows and sufferings, even its sins; because He was without sin, He could bear the sins of all men. We feel this character of essential humanity so strongly to be our Lord’s, and it so manifested, and alone could manifest, His divinity, that it may be called the grand object of the Church, in her Advents, Epiphanies, Lents, Passion Weeks, Easter-Days and Ascension-Days, to exhibit it. Every true saint in the world’s history has felt this was the character our Lord would especially seek to produce in His disciples: largeness of heart, sympathy with all our race, fellowship in its sufferings, grief for the sins that hold it down. The most conspicuous saints of the Romish calendar acknowledge these as most high and Divine, and with them go humiliation, suffering, and indifference to good or ill report. But now the counterfeit system comes in: “What a great and glorious thing to be a saint, above the rest of men, unlike them! What a fine thing to be humble, self-denying, submitting to persecution and shame! What glory for those who can outdo one another in this race! What an honour to be enrolled in this calendar, what fame here, what rewards in the life to come!” Who does not feel at once that a new image has been introduced, the very opposite of the one we were considering? It has come in, no one knows how, under the very names and words that seemed so sacred and beautiful; yet see how frightful and deformed it is. Will anyone dare say there has not been, or is not now, a system that sanctions this image, honours it, and holds it up for imitation and idolatry?

We are not bound to say of any particular person that he has given himself up to this system or caught this image. We may believe, and rejoice to believe, that in every age there have been multitudes, and that today there are numbers in every country of Europe, who, whatever their outward professions and symbols, confess the true image in their hearts and conform to it in their lives. Such persons belong to the Catholic Church. They witness to her permanence, and to the day she will come out bright and beautiful from all her corruptions, as a bride adorned for her husband. But their existence only makes us see more clearly, and hate more fervently, the system that has taken the name and affected the powers of the Church. It only makes us more sure that this system will be destroyed by the brightness of His coming, who is the true and only Pope and Potentate, the real King of Saints.
