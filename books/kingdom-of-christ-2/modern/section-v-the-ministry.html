Section V.—The Ministry

An unbroken succession of bishops, preserved despite Christendom’s divisions, embodies Christ’s headship and absolving power, a claim the Quaker reduces to inward impulse, the Presbyterian denies, and the Romanist turns into a vicar’s authority.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

It is commonly observed that a priestly caste always has three marks. It claims lofty dominion over people’s minds as well as their bodies. It lays a very heavy yoke on both. And it is opposed to everything humane and expansive.

Anyone who reads history can see how much warrant there is for these charges. These evil tendencies have not been confined to one set of circumstances or one religion. They have shown themselves in Judea and Christendom as well as in Hindostan. So they may fairly be counted as belonging to human nature, and as likely to beset anyone who, anywhere and under any conditions, takes up the office of religious guide and authority.

The impartial inquirer, especially in modern times, is ready to admit that this institution has not been merely fruitful of evil. But he rightly says the benefits cannot be limited to any one system. Memphis, Delphi, and the Brahmins of the East did much for civilisation. And he says it remains to be proved that the idea of priesthood does not involve tyranny and narrowness. At certain periods the tyranny may have been useful, and the narrowness was common to all classes.

This is indeed the real question. Suppose the fundamental principles of a priestly caste, beneath its outward varieties, are the ones just set down. Then it should be got rid of as soon as possible, and it will vanish when truth and honesty win the victory over fraud and falsehood, as we are bound to believe they finally will. But suppose instead that the forms and language of the Christian Church embody an idea of priesthood exactly opposite to one that aims at dominion, restraint on the human spirit, and confining men to certain districts and habits of thought. Then we may ask whether this is the idea men in all ages and religions have been feeling after. Perhaps the good attributed to them came from reaching some apprehension of it, and the evil from losing sight of it or contradicting it. And perhaps the triumph of truth over falsehood would then be shown in the full accomplishment of this idea, not in destroying the institution that has witnessed for it and preserved it.

These facts are indisputable. 1. The whole priestly caste in Christendom bears the name of ministers, or servants. From the Bishop of Rome down to the founder of the newest sect in the United States, everyone who deals with the Gospel or claims a divine commission takes this name for his office. 2. The most remarkable power these ministers have claimed, and the one for which they have been most honoured, is the power of absolving, or setting free. The claim has been practically universal. Luther believed he was to absolve as much as Tetzel did. Whoever says a minister’s sole office is to preach the Gospel says so because he thinks that is the way to absolve. There are serious differences about the nature of the power and how it is exercised, but none about its existence or its connexion, in some way, with the Christian ministry. 3. In Christian Europe, ever since it became Christian, the most conspicuous order of ministers has claimed a universal character. The overseers, or Bishops, have felt themselves to be the bonds of communication between different parts of the earth. Each one’s jurisdiction is confined to a district. But by the nature of his office he has held, and been obliged to hold, fellowship with people in other districts, who speak other languages and keep other customs. Such an order may be far more dangerous than a priesthood confined to one region, and rulers of particular countries have felt it so. Yet it is plainly of an entirely different kind. Whatever it has effected, it seems to aim at wider commerce and fellowship among men. At first look it is more comprehensive than any civil order one can think of, unless that be one attempting universal conquest, which destroys its character as an order by the attempt. Yet episcopacy has not been an accidental overgrowth on other forms of priesthood. Where it is recognized, it has been the root of all other forms and is believed to contain them. Because of the importance given to the Eucharist, it has been held that the Church must have an order corresponding to the priests of the old dispensation, the difference being that their sacrifice was anticipatory and ours commemorative. This office has been joined with the absolving power. Yet it has always been supposed to be included in the Bishop’s, and where it is given to a distinct class, that class gets its authority from him. The Church has likewise acknowledged an order with distinct functions, whose main object is to provide for men’s bodily wants or only to announce spiritual truths. Even this has been understood as a delegation of powers belonging to the Bishop, which he has no leisure to discharge, and where episcopacy is recognized no one can undertake it without such delegation. So an office implying an attention far removed from what the word “priest” ordinarily suggests seems to have been the characteristic one in the Church, including all others and the source from which they arise.

I spoke of “the countries in which episcopacy is recognized.” We should consider what these countries are, lest we draw an inference the facts do not warrant. Let the reader first recall the Eastern Church over the last fifteen or sixteen hundred years. Let him think of Constantinople in its glory, the sects that broke from it, the horrible contentions between them and their common mother, and the degradation every part of it has suffered under the Ottoman power. Then let him reflect that in whatever countries, fortunes, or strifes, all of them have preserved this institution. Next let him consider the different circumstances in which Christianity was preached and adopted in the nations of the West, and the different influences and racial characters. All these nations, amid all their conflicts with the East, preserved this institution, without one clearly established exception, until the sixteenth century. Then let him consider the Reformation, with its separation of nations, its violent conflict with the old system of Europe, and its excessive magnifying of individual faith. Even so, the institution was preserved in all the Latin nations and among the Teutonic nations in England, Denmark, and Sweden. It was rejected, not without great reluctance, in parts of Germany, Holland, Switzerland, and Scotland. In each of these some witness of it has been kept, and in at least one there are those who think it more necessary now than ever. Further, it has passed to America and established itself in colonies founded by Puritans and Quakers. It grew up there after England’s influence had ceased, and without any state patronage it is becoming an instrument for spreading the Gospel from those colonies to many parts of the world. These are Episcopacy’s pretensions to be a Catholic institution.

What I have said implies that this institution has permanence as well as universality. It implies too that this permanence differs from that of a custom which took its significance from some local accident and then kept itself going through a body created to conserve it. We have seen the institution maintain itself among bodies in vehement disagreement, and reappear when all local habits were against it. How, then, has it been preserved, or seemed to be? By an act of consecration performed through three existing Bishops. According to the faith of all the nations and ages that have kept it, this signifies that the person newly entering the function receives the same kind of authority and gifts as those first endowed with it.

These facts touch the very heart of the questions I have been discussing in this work. Is there any meaning in the words Kingdom of Christ? Do they mean what they seem to mean? Are there facts in the world’s history showing that they denote something really existing? We have found a series of facts all pointing the same way. They prove a universal and spiritual society, which keeps its existence amid the greatest perplexities and contradictions, and whose conditions are inexplicable unless it is connected with and upheld by an unseen power. But all the signs we have considered so far exist for the sake of men, and so they also imply the agency of men. On the character of that agency the whole character of the kingdom depends. Without certain magistrates or officers it is not a commonwealth, nor a kingdom in any accepted sense of the word. Even societies that boast most of self-government have officers, whatever the tenure of their office. So we must either give up all we have maintained, or steadily consider this question: what kind of officers would be consistent with the kind of kingdom those other signs speak of? Since all those signs pointed to an invisible presence and were meant to admit men into it, these officers must be constituted for the same end. They must bring before men the fact that they are subject to an invisible and universal Ruler.

If so, they must also show Him to men in the character and offices He actually came to perform. If He came not to be ministered unto but to minister, and His exercise of power was a ministry, theirs must be so too. They can see themselves only as ministers. They cannot suppose their power diminished by acknowledging this, or that it will be real unless exercised with continual remembrance of it. One chief part of His work was to absolve men from past evil, from the power of present evil, and from the danger of future evil. All men coming into the world continually need this absolution. If He makes Himself manifest through men in any way, we must suppose they would be called especially to represent Him as Absolver. His greatest purpose was to bind men together in one family, and when He ascended on high He took the office of Head and overseer of that family; all His other acts and services to men are implied in this. So the highest office of His servants must be to exhibit Him in this character, and so to make known that His kingdom is a real kingdom that rules over all. Finally, Jesus Himself when on earth received a formal and outward designation to His office. It signified on what terms He held it: not as a separate independent Being, but as one with the Father, honouring Him in all His words and acts. So it seems reasonable to expect an equally formal and visible designation to witness to men that the offices fulfilled for their sakes by creatures of their own flesh and blood are not held for any qualities or merits in those creatures. They are held from Christ and under Christ, by persons who can show His character truly only so far as they do their work faithfully.

But is there anything in the New Testament’s language that accords with these anticipations and explains these facts? One would think this language, like that about the institution of Baptism, must lie on the very surface of the record and yet connect with its deepest announcements. Otherwise it can be no authority for institutions that claim to embody the whole character of the new dispensation. A few casual hints could never warrant fallible men in supposing they were meant to be Christ’s ministers and to present Him before men. Still less could they excuse sinful men who take on themselves, in God’s name, to absolve their brethren. Least of all could they justify an order that takes so singular a position and claims such high functions as the Episcopal. I think the mere appearance of such an office, even in the time just after our Lord left the world, is not a sufficient reason for its claiming to be an estate of His kingdom, unless He expressly and formally instituted it Himself.

So we turn to the Gospels to ask whether they give any guidance. We meet at once the history of the choosing and appointing of a set of men clearly distinct from every class in the Jewish polity. They are carefully connected with that polity: their number recalls the tribes into which the nation was divided, they were all Jews, and their first commission was strictly confined to the house of Israel. But this only makes the peculiarity of their office more remarkable. From the first, when they were least able to understand the nature of their service, the plainest indications were given that it was meant to go beyond national limits. Even while they were falling into great confusions about their place in the world, and had to be reminded again and again that the kings of the Gentiles exercised dominion over them but it was not to be so in the Church, they were assured in the strongest language that they were to do a wonderful work and be endued with wonderful powers. Whoever received them would receive their Master, and they were sent forth by Him as He was sent forth by the Father.

These intimations are not scattered carelessly through the Gospels; they form part of their very substance. Our Lord’s own life and image are most clearly set before us in teaching the disciples that those who become as little children are greatest in the kingdom of heaven and that their rule was to be a service, and in the acts that went with that teaching. He never separates training them in their office from performing His own. And it is plainly an office He trains them to. He is not teaching them to be great saints and keep up a high tone of personal holiness as if that were the end of their lives. He is teaching them that they have a work to do as He has His. He is straitened until He accomplishes His; they must be straitened until they accomplish theirs. In trying to accomplish it they will most find that they are lights of the world and must draw their light continually from Him. So if we called the four Gospels “The Institution of a Christian Ministry,” we might not go far wrong or lose sight of many of their essential qualities. Above all, we would not lose sight of the different crises in our Lord’s life and their connexion with different discoveries of grace and truth to man. Before the resurrection there was only the general commission: “Go and preach the Kingdom of God. Heal the sick. Cast out devils. Freely ye have received, freely give.” Far deeper views of their office came out in the conversations the night before His Passion, all tied to the awful facts they were to witness and the mysterious service they had been performing. But only when He came back from the grave, with the witness and power of a new life for man, did He breathe on His disciples and say, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost. Whosesoever sins ye remit, they are remitted; whosesoever sins ye retain, they are retained.” Only as He was leaving them was the commission given, “Go ye into all nations,” with the promise, “Lo I am with you always, even unto the end of the world.” And only after He had ascended on high were the powers for fulfilling this commission confirmed. Then a sign was given of a union that distinctions of nation and language could not break, and they were declared the pillars of a universal Church.

These poor fishermen could not doubt for a moment that these powers belonged to them officially, not personally. So the chief question is this: did they suppose the kingdom was to die with them, or that they were to perpetuate it? Were they to perpetuate it in the manner in which our Lord Himself had established it, or in some other? The new circumstances of the Church necessarily changed the position of its ministers. Was that to be a change in the nature of their office and institution, or only in their numbers and the circumstances of their jurisdiction? If only the latter, were those who succeeded to the Apostolic office to reckon that they derived their powers less immediately from Christ, and were less witnesses of His permanent government, than those who got their first commission from Him while He lived on earth? If these questions are answered one way, the nations that have preserved the episcopal institution have a right to believe they have preserved one of the appointed and indispensable signs of a spiritual and universal society. If they are answered the other way, it seems hard to understand how such a universal society can exist at all.

The Quaker.

But we must consider the arguments of those who think otherwise. The Quaker tells us at once that we have described a formal ministry, not a spiritual one: a ministry of the Old Testament, not of the New. “A true minister is consecrated by an inward call. The voice of the Spirit, not of men, invites him into God’s vineyard. Older and more experienced men may judge whether his vocation is real, but they do not give him his appointment, and still less can they confer one on persons God has not chosen. Under the old dispensation the priesthood passed down in a certain family. That arrangement belonged to its time and is done away in Christ. Even under the first covenant there was a race of prophets who simply obeyed the divine voice and spoke and acted as the Holy Ghost moved them. The so-called Church of Christ has adopted the obsolete part of the system and rejected the living, spiritual part. Finally, the Christian teacher is fitted for his work by the Spirit’s teaching, not by human culture.” Each of these points deserves careful consideration.

1. It follows from the doctrine of Baptism, which lies at the threshold of our Churchmanship, that every Christian infant is taken under the guardianship and education of God’s Holy Spirit. The well-instructed parent raises his child in faith of this. His stern discipline against self-will, and his tenderness that draws out affection in return, have one end. The child, governed for a time by a visible parent, is to feel itself governed by an unseen Teacher. Loved imperfectly and waveringly, it is to perceive that an unchangeable, imperishable love tends it unceasingly. When this truth dawns on the child, blossoms and buds appear, and the parent watches them anxiously but with confidence. There are strange new thoughts and impulses, wonder at his own condition, hopes stretching into infinity, a deepening sense of ever-present evil and a brightening view of an ever-present Deliverer. The foster-father knows that these show his care has not been in vain, and that the boy is learning the secret of his other, royal parentage. Gradually he gives the child the conviction that not only his adoption and inheritance belong to another region, but that all his dim longings toward them were heavenly inspirations. This news is precious also because it explains feelings that will arise later. For now the boy asks not only What is he? and Where is he going? He asks what relations he has with other men, how he is to act on them, and what his vocation is. In this inquiry he will remember what his first instructors told him. As he nourishes gentle affections, keeps from sensual defilement, seeks a child’s temper, thinks on what is lovely and pure, keeps a cheerful heart, does good as he has opportunity, avoids noisy excitements of flesh or spirit, and is neither impatient of present perplexity nor greedy of distinction, he will be able in quiet meditation and prayer to learn the mind of the Spirit and to know where in the vineyard God means him to labour. Suppose his outward position shows he is meant to serve men’s bodily needs, in a calling whose apparent end is mercenary. He will be sure that in this station, esteemed or not, he is to glorify God and clear his calling of every deserved reproach, keeping a free, noble, heavenly spirit amid temptations to be sordid and worldly. Or suppose a secret impulse leads him to pursuits resting on science, which need scientific insight and diligence in experiments and facts. That impulse is no less to be heeded because outward influences and education may have helped to create it. Whether his thoughts turn to man’s physical structure, the powers of life, and disease and decay; or to the civil ordinances that uphold society and redress wrongs; or to the wider relations of men and the mysterious powers of sympathy, fear and awe that are the real bonds of human government, he will feel that a Divine Instructor is marking him out as physician, lawyer or statesman. He must look to the same guidance to direct his intellect, both in preparing and in the work itself. It must keep him in the fear of God and in honourable, affectionate thoughts of his fellows, so that he never treats them as fair game for quackery, chicanery or party-spirit. Nor will I yield so far to prejudices I respect, and would not wish to remove from any Quaker until I can show him the truth they distort, as to stop short of carrying the principle a step further. Every soldier of truly brave and gentle heart has been led to reflect on the preciousness of national life and the duty of upholding it even at the cost of individual life, awful as that is. He has been taught to give his energies to it, not by an evil spirit, but by the same Spirit of truth and love who, when laying the foundation of His new kingdom, chose as its first subject and witness a Centurion of the Italian band. But suppose a young man, studying in all things to approve himself to his great Taskmaster, finds none of these particular promptings, only a large, general desire to serve his race. He has a stronger sense than others of man’s highest destiny, perhaps with a less keen perception than would be desirable of those necessities which, though they may be baptized into religion, are of the earth, earthy. Spiritual forms that most men need to see in sensible mirrors rise before him in naked majesty. Good and evil appear to him not as means but as the great ends to which all tends. His inner conflict makes him feel his tie with his kind. He has a lively sense of temptation and of its peril to those whose objects are highest. He measures acts and events by spiritual proportions, not outward size. He has been taught to reverence poverty and helplessness, and to see that the highest truth is the most universal, not the most exclusive. The immediate vision of God and the full submission of heart to his loving will seem to him the great gifts intended for man. Then, unless outward circumstances oblige him to the perhaps more glorious, though more painful, task of yielding to a wisdom he adores without understanding, he may conclude that the Divine Voice is inviting him not to some partial service but to what we emphatically call the Ministry.

Thus far our opinion about inward calls agrees with the Quaker’s, except that we carry it farther. He holds that one inward call is needed for a Christian and another for a Christian preacher. We contend that every Christian should believe himself called to every work he undertakes. Unless he believes this, the work will be unholy and cheerless, pursued without confidence in God or hope of worthy fruit. I do not mean to explain away the express call of the minister, as if it meant whatever anyone pleases. My point is that the language we use about the highest pursuit sets the tone for how we speak, or at least think, about all pursuits. Other men may have honourable inspirations, obey them honestly, and silently credit them to their true source. But the minister of God, with fear and trembling yet without cowardly diffidence, is to declare to himself and others the real source of what is within him. He cannot teach others to believe themselves temples of the living God if he dare not admit the plain consequences of that doctrine for himself.

But this question remains. In every well-ordered community, the tradesman, lawyer, physician, soldier and statesman believe that the secret influences which drew them to their calling were not their title to enter it, but only the motives that led them to seek a title. Is the analogy violated here? Is the minister of God in a different position? Does the secret call in his spirit make him a minister, or only set him inquiring what the lawful way of becoming one is? I confess I do not see what should make the difference. I can see that the call is to a higher work, and that he whose very outward duty is spiritual needs to understand its principle and origin more clearly than others. But I cannot see that the difference is one of kind. I cannot see why, when the Spirit of God elsewhere moves a man to adapt himself to some rule or order, here it should teach him he needs no order and that the “motion” substitutes for it. I should have expected that the minister of God would receive his commission in a very different way from other professions. (That is, if his very name is no mere invention, if there is any communication between heaven and earth, and if any men are called to teach their brethren in matters directly concerning God.) I should have expected some scheme showing that he does not derive his authority from the king of his land, or any learned body, or any limited power. But I should never have expected the following. Elsewhere a man’s inward impulses, though needful to satisfy him that he walks the appointed road, are held wholly inadequate to give him authority or establish his position to others. Yet here, in an office instituted for mankind, for the poor and ignorant, an office in which the individual is to be hidden and forgotten and God’s majesty asserted, these motions are to be everything, and no token is given that all men can recognize of the extent and source of the influence he claims to obey.

2. Next comes the question, so often discussed in earlier sections, of the relation between the Old and New Testaments. In the Jewish Commonwealth, as the Quaker admits, we find at first a strict, definite organisation: a priesthood limited to one tribe, and sacrifices appointed in place and time. It is a rigid system, and its Author now and then, as in the case of Eli, asserts his own prerogative by breaking some of its rules, to show that it exists for an end and is not itself the end. Yet this precaution did not stop a dead sense of routine, harmful to the system itself, from creeping over the people. So the next contrivance in this divine machinery seems meant to counteract this without breaking uniformity. When the ecclesiastical constitution was well established and its principle explained by its working, a new order of men was raised up, evidently to prove that forms and institutions indicate our relation to God and are means of reaching him, but neither create that relation nor substitute for it. Several consequences follow. The prophet’s functions cannot be defined in a ritual. No formal law can fix which part of the community he comes from, as with the priest. Either limit would defeat his purpose. He lives as the witness of a continual presence and power in the nation, which it may forget but cannot get rid of. He must rise up as the emblem of the conscience he awakens and the law he testifies to. He must come like a thief in the night on the guilty soul. He must not let it lose itself in the whirl of events or the monotony of observances, but make it feel that both speak of a living person who is coming out of his place to judge, and whose day is at hand. To fix this on the people’s heart, he must often do strange things. He and his children are signs and wonders; he walks barefoot, acts out a siege, sees his wife die and does not weep, marries an adulteress. He must by all means break the yoke of familiarity and custom. And yet he is most orderly, a witness for order from first to last. His charges against kings, priests and people are neglect of institutions, indifference to divine precepts, and recklessness toward the everlasting covenant. If he reveals the inward law of God, he learns its nature in the outward law. If he longs for communion with God, he expects it in the temple. If he is stricken with his own and his people’s iniquity, it is a coal from the altar that cleanses his lips. The desolation of the beautiful city calls out his human feelings, and the loss of the Shechinah is the keynote of his saddest religious meditations.

Such are the obvious marks of the Jewish prophet, the appointed witness for a spiritual faculty and life in man. If the doctrine is true that outward appointment is the great cause of corruption and hypocrisy in the Church, the prophets should have been far purer and more exemplary than the priests, and the abominations charged on the one should have had no counterpart in the other. But Scripture says otherwise. There were as many false prophets as scandalous priests: as many who gave out their own heart’s vision as the word of the Lord as there were priests who could not tell clean from unclean or made the Lord’s offering abhorred. The spiritual call was abused as often as the outward ordination. But that is by the way. My main point is that the best prophets were still Old Testament ministers, not ministers of the Spirit. The Quakers will not deny this. Yet in what did the prophet fall short of a New Testament minister? He answers exactly to their definition of one. Take away the law, the priesthood and the sacrifices, and leave only the prophet, and we have the Quaker idea of the Christian ministry at its noblest. For the Jew is a witness to spiritual life, obeys a spiritual impulse, speaks of the Living Word in the heart, and speaks by the Spirit. If the Quakers are right, the prophet stands far above Peter and Paul, who witnessed to outward acts and preached Christ’s death and resurrection. The early Friends evidently thought so, since all the precedents for their proceedings come from the prophets’ records. Their sudden appearances, utterances and witnessings at city gates were copied from the Old Testament seer. They were copied imperfectly, I think, since the reverence for order, sacred places and national worship so conspicuous in the original was left out. They had no type or warrant in apostolic practice. For strange to say, the apostles of our Lord, appointed to introduce a new dispensation, speaking to a sense-hardened people and foretelling the most awful crisis of their history as near, nevertheless departed completely in their outward conduct from the prophetic model that was constantly before their eyes and which they studied diligently for other purposes. They dropped all the startling peculiarities of the prophetic character, though they proclaimed that Heaven had sent them to announce the fulfilment of the promises to the fathers, the accomplishment of law and prophecy. They did not say the function was obsolete, for its essential marks were present in themselves. Nor had they authority to declare even its accidental features lost, any more than to abolish sacrifices or circumcision. Agabus might still bind the girdle on his hands and feet, and the daughters of Philip the deacon might prophesy. But the special ministers of the New Covenant claimed a different function. They would not even submit to the voices of brothers or sisters whom they acknowledged to be truly inspired. Paul went up to Jerusalem in defiance of their warnings.

But why this difference? We say it is because in the days of the prophets the Spirit was not yet given, since Christ was not yet glorified. The priests and sacrifices of the Jewish Commonwealth testified to a divine constitution established in the Word. The order of prophets testified to a divine Spirit acting in man. But as the person of the Word was not yet manifested, neither was the person of the Spirit. The mystery was hid for ages and generations. Each new step prepared for its discovery, and faithful men were enabled to grasp it before it was fully made known. First came the undeviating regularity of the priesthood, like the settled succession of day and night. Then came the gusts of prophetic inspiration, like the wind blowing where it wills. Last came the inseparable union of the two. Alone, the one was a mere set of chords from which no music could come, and the other at best an Æolian harp, which might give a sweet note now and then but could never satisfy the soul with sustained harmony. When the Son of God came in human flesh and proclaimed himself the source of all the order of the universe, the outward organisation that had foretold him was bound to be turned into one that took him as its ground. Likewise, when the Spirit of Truth, who proceeds from the Father and the Son, was given by the ascended Lord to testify of both, the prophetic dispensation that had prepared the way was bound to change correspondingly. The occasional glimpses of a divine Lord of man were the vision in the plain of Mamre, the angel in the bush, and the one who did wondrously before Manoah. Bishop Bull calls these preludings of the incarnation, and they were lost in the full swell of “Lo, I am with you always.” The crucified man had been declared by the resurrection to be the Son of God with power, the ever-present King of man. Those foretastes of this revelation fell no further short of it than the sudden inspirations of the Old Testament fell short of the meaning in the words, “When he ascended up on high, he received gifts for men, that the Lord God might dwell among them.” So the apostles could not, without lowering their dignity as New Testament ministers, have given the prophetic office its old form. They believed themselves continually, not momentarily, inspired. They felt it was sin to doubt this, sin not to act on it, and sin to do anything that would weaken the perception of it in their hearers. So they delivered their message with perfect calmness and coherence. Their commission was a surer sign that the Spirit was with them to govern them than any impulses and emotions could have been. They were always ready to preach and always able to be silent. This was their idea of a New Testament ministry, and we say it ought to be ours. As ministers of the New Covenant we must draw our rule from apostles, not prophets. It is nothing to us that the holy men of old were sometimes called from the sheepfolds to witness to a spiritual presence, and that this inward call sufficed without any other designation. We know that the apostles received from Christ a formal, express designation before he gave them power to go and preach in the villages he would visit himself. After his resurrection he gave them a new and more awful commission, with the words “Receive ye the Holy Ghost.” After his ascension they completed their number by an external arrangement before the Holy Ghost gave them power to testify of the risen King. It is nothing to us that the prophets, in keeping with their office, waited for a conscious afflatus before uttering their oracles. We know that St. Paul said, “If I do this willingly, I have a reward; but if against my will, a dispensation of the Gospel is committed to me. . . . A necessity is laid upon me, yea, woe is me, if I preach not the Gospel.” We know that he presented it as the mark of a New Testament preacher that he put no veil over his face and used great plainness of speech. We know that his preaching is now a plain statement of facts, now a long historical argument, now turns on the habits, national character and circumstances of his hearers. It is not merely eloquent but at times most skilfully rhetorical. Yet it never shows a man overmastered by a power forbidding him to speak in the way best suited to his hearers’ spiritual needs. Was this because he lacked zeal? Or was it because his zeal was so intense that he never lost sight of the end of his apostleship, never sacrificed it for pleasant feelings, never thought about his own mind when his business was to go forward, and never overlooked any instrument God had put within his reach? Was it because he lacked the Spirit? Or was he under the habitual government of that Spirit who kept him from surrendering to his own tastes or emotions, projects or fears, and who turned every object in nature or art, all history and all passing events, to the service of the sanctuary? Once more, it is nothing to us that under the old economy there were prophetesses as well as prophets, and that between the founding of the Christian Church and the fall of the Jewish Commonwealth this part of the system may, like its other accidents, have been fading without yet having wholly ceased. If the prophetic call had had any restriction as to its subjects (apart from what is reserved in the depths of divine wisdom), it would not seem to have served as a balance to the rigid formality of the priesthood. But when the Apostle of the Gentiles announced that he would have women keep silence in the churches, we see that the principle asserted all along in the regular organisation of the Jewish Church was now to become a universal law, since the formal constitution had been joined to the spiritual power. If St. Paul had merely suggested the rule as expedient, to meet Jewish and heathen prejudices, we would readily allow that the Omnipotent Spirit might at different times practically annul it. But he was restraining a practice common among Jews and heathens, and doing so professedly on principles tied to the divinely appointed relation of the sexes. So we can have no doubt that the Spirit of Order, through his chosen witness, was announcing the law of his own commonwealth. And it is a fundamental maxim of Christian morality that the Holy Spirit does not break down eternal laws and ordinances merely to show his power. Power is the handmaid of love and order, and when it leaves their company and claims a separate existence, it is devilish, not godlike.

In this case, as in all the others we have considered, the Quaker is guilty of the very sin he charges on his brethren. He revives the old economy while professing to assert the glory of the Gospel. His complaints against a succession of ministers show this tendency well. He will not allow that in a universal, spiritual dispensation Baptism can take the place of Circumcision, or the Eucharist that of the Passover. In the same way he cannot see how in such a dispensation succession by the laying on of hands can take the place of the Levitical and Aaronic succession. I maintained that the difference between a national dispensation and a spiritual, universal one is realised in the difference between these two sets of institutions, and is lost when the old institutions are merely abolished. In the same way I maintain that the difference shows most clearly in the change from a ministry permanent in one tribe and family to one handed on by ecclesiastical tradition. Those who on Quaker grounds reject this last method as carnal and secular have no escape but slavishly imitating the most superficial and passing features of a bygone period.

3. The last point is that of ministerial gifts. Everyone knows there were schools of the prophets under the old dispensation. But we must not stress this so as to deny that in many cases the prophet’s call was itself what endowed him with the functions of his task. At any rate there seems to have been no outward act by which he was invested with the powers he afterwards used. (The case of Elisha may be an exception. But was not a prophet in Samaria in some sense a substitute for the priest, or at least a witness for the institution?) If what I have said about his office is true, it could not have been otherwise. It was easy to understand that the priest might be robed and invested with awful authority by a rite of consecration, since his endowments were a matter of formal, legal explanation. But no idea yet presented to the Jewish mind could have shown how gifts as subtle and inexplicable as the prophets’ could be passed on by any such method. The man of holy meditation, whose life witnesses to his continual sense of an unseen presence, comes forth and awakens in kings, priests or people the feeling that they are subject to an eternal law they have forgotten or resisted. By eloquence and song he rouses the spell-bound, death-stricken conscience of the nation, forces it to remember it is within an everlasting covenant, and shows what judgments must startle it out of its long sensual dream. The hearer’s acknowledgment of an operation from above, inspiring the prophet’s understanding, comes at the same moment as his awareness of a secret, wonderful operation on himself. He does not distinguish accurately between the power that has raised the teacher into a poet and himself into a man, and still less does he know from whom either impulse comes. He perceives only that some deep influence is at work, invisible, indefinite, incomprehensible.

But happily, for that age and all ages to come, the prophet’s duties were not limited to this. Beneath the consciousness of a living judge spying out his ways, other desires appear in the mind of the holy Israelite, and the teacher is to educate them by promises and glorious hopes. Through him man is to learn not only of that within him which fears and trembles before God, but of that which nothing but the vision of God can satisfy. The same event that is to free the conscience, so far as its fears come from dread of sin and not love of it, is also what is to give these longings their perfect object. That event is the coming of the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world, and who shows men the Father. It is the glorious consummation of all past history, to which the prophetic eye always turns, and in whose glories every true Israelite, whether still on earth or departed, is sure he will share. But another hope is bound up with this, equally exhilarating and still more mysterious. The spirit in man, striving after this perfect Object to be revealed, feels that when admitted to behold him it will need to be sustained by a life from himself, and that when admitted to converse with him it will need the assurance that he is conversing with it. Nothing less than an unspeakable communion, a Spirit witnessing with our spirit, can be the limit of the aspirations so wonderfully called out. The prophet meets this deepest cry of the inner man. The promise of the Spirit poured from above, and of the desert blossoming like the rose, is inseparably linked to the promise of a King who shall reign in righteousness, and in whom the glory of the Most High shall shine.

As the prophet’s own mind rose to the level of these anticipations, he must have seen a distinction between the spirit in him that longed for intercourse with the Everlasting Spirit and the faculties of thought and expression by which he passed on his desires and hopes to others. Both, he will have felt, are under divine impulse and government. But the one is in some sense a divine faculty, made for fellowship with what is divine and realising its own nature only in that fellowship. The other is made to obey a motion with which, strictly speaking, it cannot sympathize. The deeper, diviner faculty is the more universal. It belongs to me as a man and is the privilege of my race. The other is specially given me for wise and gracious purposes, to distinguish me from my fellows, to be received as an awful trust and used for their benefit. So in the great period of manifestation to which he looked forward, he will at times have anticipated two things. First, along with the revelation of Him who is the desire of all nations, there would be a revelation of Him who had been secretly moving the hearts and understandings of men. Second, this revelation would in some striking way both assert him as holding fellowship with men universally and as bestowing the special gifts that qualify some men to guide and teach their brethren.

Whether or not this anticipation took definite form in the prophets’ minds, we can tell how it was realised. The Son of God picks his chosen servants, the heralds of his kingdom, from the fishermen of Galilee. For three years he talks with them, teaching them mysteries kept hid from generations and telling them they were allowed to see what kings and prophets had not seen. In all his dealings he treats them as men destined for a work, giving them not merely a knowledge of truth but a method of communicating it. Yet after the call these disciples had received, after the wonderful discipline that had so long prepared them, and after he had reappeared in his risen form and breathed on them saying “Receive ye the Holy Ghost,” he still told them to wait in Jerusalem for the promise of the Father. Then, and only then, would they have power from on high for their work. As I have remarked before, they were met not as individuals but as a college, and had formally completed their number when the promise was fulfilled. And what was the fulfilment? Its deep mystery, I have contended before (following the Church, which has set Trinity Sunday next after Whitsunday), was the formal declaration of the Holy Spirit as a Person, the assertion in him of the divine unity of the Father and the Son, and the establishment of a universal Church. But Scripture inseparably connects this with the conferring of powers on a set of men already marked out as ministers of Christ. Christ’s call was not enough. Here was a formal endowment with the gifts Christ had designed for them when he told them to leave their nets, and which he had now received for them on high.

Signs, we know, accompanied the first great declaration of this divine presence in the Church. They were like the twig or clod of earth that in ancient feoffments attested the handing over of land to a person and his heirs for ever. We should no more expect those signs to be constantly repeated than expect the twig or clod to be presented again to the new owner each time he performed a fresh act of ownership. But we affirm the principle those signs asserted to be perpetual. By a wonderful demonstration the Spirit of God declares that he dwells among men; that an organised body of men has been provided for his habitation; that through this body his blessings pass to the world; and that through one portion of this body they pass to the rest. Everything on this great day of inspiration speaks of preparation, order, distinction and unity. The moment chosen is the period of an ancient festival, not a chance one. The place is an upper chamber in the temple, not a secret one. The persons are men who have been openly preparing for years, not chance individuals. Any system that teaches that a minister is not publicly, openly and once for all endowed with certain powers for his work, those powers being sustained in him by the constant presence of Him who gives them, we affirm to be essentially an Old Testament system. So is any system that supposes the minister, being such by his inward call, is either then given the gifts or receives them later from time to time by sudden movements and inspirations. The consequences of such a system must be these. The mere spiritual faculty awakened in the man by the voice of God’s Spirit will be confounded with that Spirit himself, and his personality forgotten in his operations. There will be a fearful confusion between the human speaker and the invisible power speaking in him, alternating with a continual attempt to separate them. The intellectual faculties will first be despised as having no connexion with the Spirit. Then they will be confounded with the truly divine and spiritual faculty in man, when both are found to come from the same source and the former to be the means of evoking the latter. I say that if we considered where the Old Testament system was defective and how the New fills the blanks, and then heard of a scheme that restored these blanks without restoring those other parts of the old system which kept its necessary imperfection from being evil, we should look for all these mischiefs as its fruits. And the actual history of the Quakers fulfils every one of these predictions. The belief in the Personality of the Spirit, and in his difference from the spiritual life he originates, is the truth they have found hardest to hold, and it has continually slipped away from them. Their ministers, even in the best age of their society, were almost idolized. They have swung continually between contempt for the intellectual powers in general and a vast over-appreciation of them when they seemed to be under spiritual guidance. All these contradictions are now coming to a head and threatening the extinction of their body.

The Presbyterian.

We now come to those whose objections are chiefly and expressly against Episcopacy. Some of these rest on the idea of the Church, some on the letter of Scripture, and some on experience. The first runs like this: ‘Christ is the only Bishop of His Church. Any attempt to put another overseership in place of His misunderstands our relation to Him. The words “Call no man your father upon earth, for one is your Father which is in heaven” do not forbid acknowledging spiritual fathers in some sense, since St. Paul constantly calls himself one. But they do condemn the claims to fatherhood that Bishops, by the very nature of their office, put forward. That office breeds assumption and domination, the opposite of what a ministry should show. Second, it has no warrant in revelation. No one disputes that the word for overseer, or bishop, occurs in the Acts of the Apostles. But it occurs in connexions showing that the officer it names was not distinguished from the Presbyter, and that the Apostles did not treat the distinction as bound up with the being of the Church. A Church on a scriptural platform, framed on Apostolic precedent, would therefore have no place for Bishops, or only a very unimportant one. Third, you may say we cannot return strictly to those precedents, that the Church has a principle of life and authority in itself, and that we must look at how institutions have actually developed. Very well: then look at the cruelty, usurpation, pride and worldliness of these spiritual fathers. Is not the Church’s history as conclusive a witness against them as the words of inspiration?’

The objection drawn from the constitution of the Church does not touch Episcopacy alone; its reach is very wide. Presbyterians in general have seen that it strikes at any human priesthood. If Christ’s being the universal Bishop rules out earthly Bishops, His being the Priest of His Church rules out any lesser person usurping that name. The second case will probably be felt to be the stronger, and many people denounce the Greek and Latin words for “priest” in the Divine economy who will still contend hard for Bishops. I ask them to consider that if these names are denied to the Church’s ministers on this ground, they must on the same ground be denied to its members. The words “We are made Kings and Priests unto God,” so often quoted against the claims of a particular caste or ministry, become profane and dangerous, since they give to the servants offices that, on this doctrine, belong only to the Lord. Nor can the argument stop there. Theological books, and above all the Book of Revelation, would have to be weeded of every phrase first used of the head of the body and then applied to the body or to any of its members. I beg anyone to reflect calmly on what this would do, not to the dialect of Christianity, but to its deepest principles. If it has any principle more essential than another, it is this: the language that makes Christ known to us is the only language that can fitly make the Church known to us. Catholic divinity and Puritan divinity alike treat the identification of offices in Christ and in His faithful members as part of the very idea of the Gospel. Where, I ask, is the line to be drawn? Why is it proper to teach that the state of the whole Church, and of each member, is the image of the state of Him who redeemed it, yet improper to teach that every office in the Church is the image of some office Christ performed in His own person, as the means by which that office is presented to men and made effectual for them through all time?

My earnest wish is that my proposition, ‘If the Incarnation mean anything, if the Church be not a dream, all offices exercised by her on behalf of humanity must be offices first exercised by Christ,’ be set beside the Presbyterian proposition, ‘It is profane and wicked to apply to ordinary human creatures the names which designate the works and offices of Christ.’ Let each be pushed to its furthest consequences and submitted to the holiest men among those whose upbringing leads them to reject Episcopacy. I have no fear of the result, and I am sure a great side benefit would follow. We are constantly asked how we dare put so much weight on an outward ordinance, as if it had anything to do with the great essential truths of the Gospel; is it not at best an outwork of Christianity? Our answer comes from this great Presbyterian argument. A matter that involves the most opposite conceptions of the whole order of the Church and of human society cannot be a trifle. If the objection is true, then the language that the most earnest men have used at their most religious moments, when striving to express the most spiritual and fundamental truths, is inconsistent and must be abandoned. If our principle is true, then it is a question of the highest practical moment whether the idea of Christ’s Episcopacy or His Priesthood can be kept among men once the Divine method for keeping them, on this hypothesis, has been rejected.

2. ‘But this view, whatever theories defend it, has no justification from Scripture.’ I admit at once that if I were given the Acts of the Apostles and told to work out from them alone what the Overseer’s office was, as distinct from the Presbyter’s, I would decline the task as hopeless. Adding what the Epistles say about these names would not bring me much nearer to satisfaction. My difficulty is to see how the Presbyterian draws, from these scattered notices, the clear conclusion that the whole Church for thirteen centuries, and most of it for sixteen, has been utterly wrong to believe that such an officer as a Bishop was meant to exist. On such evidence I would be sorry to condemn even the paltriest ceremony that could claim a similar prescription. So I would be equally sorry to offer this evidence as the original title-deed of the institution. Were there no other, I would hardly know how to state the question. The Presbyterian would not let me ask, ‘Was there in the Apostles’ time an order of priests distinct from Bishops?‘, for he admits no order of priests any more than of Bishops. Nor am I anxious to learn how soon the functions I attribute to the priest were separated from those I think belong to the Bishop. The only point that could be debated is whether the word for overseer always means in the New Testament the pastor of a number of congregations, and the word for presbyter always the pastor of one. I would be inclined to answer no. But turning again to these Acts and Epistles, I find a name that puzzles me far more, one that meets me at every turn and is implied in every sentence: Apostles. What manner of people are these, whose acts St. Luke records and whose letters are kept for the Church’s perpetual instruction? It may be answered, ’They were chosen by our Lord to be with Him in His temptations on earth, and to bear witness of His resurrection after He left the world.’ No doubt these were the functions of the first twelve. The Incarnation was to be the ground of the new kingdom, so there had to be persons who had seen and handled the Word of life. On that there is no dispute. The questions are, first, whether the fruits of the Incarnation ceased when our Lord left the world or only then began to show themselves; next, whether the form Christ Himself gave the infant kingdom was the form it was to keep through all its later development; and third, whether the Apostles’ office was to die out once the circumstances that made the name fitting had ceased. If the apostleship were inseparably tied to its first accidents, it would be strange that St. Paul should so eagerly claim the position and powers of an Apostle. His calling was of a wholly different kind from the twelve’s; he had not been with our Lord on earth; and he was a witness of the state of glory in which we believe Christ now is, as much as when He stopped the persecutor on the Damascus road. It would also be strange that the powers by which the other Apostles went out witnessing to their Master’s resurrection were not those they derived from Him on earth, nor even those they received immediately after the resurrection, but those that came upon them after He had gone out of men’s sight and ascended on high to fill all things. So the question this evidence raises is simply whether any office corresponding in its essential characteristics to the one we judge, from the New Testament, to be the distinguishing office of the Church at its foundation was or was not to continue in the Church. The common opinion is that by the continuance of this office the Church has been continued, the connexion of different ages realised, and the wholeness and unity of the body declared. We suppose the changes in the office to be changes of name, of the number of persons filling it, and of the limits of their government. Some of these are presupposed in the very existence of a body meant to expand without limit, and none touches the office’s nature or object. The Presbyterian says no such institution exists in the Church, that is, that the Church’s platform today is not the apostolic one. Yet in the same breath he protests against our departure from the simplicity of New Testament practice and calls on us to abandon all ecclesiastical precedent to conform to it.

3. One of his main reasons for this exhortation is the gross corruption and worldliness that have followed the Episcopal system wherever it has been established. I do not meet the charge by denying it, or by explaining away the instances offered, or by the seldom satisfactory commonplace that the abuse of an institution is no argument against its use. I could far more reasonably produce the facts showing that in nearly every case where the Church has enlarged her borders, where the commission “Go ye into all nations” has really been acted out, Bishops have been the instruments of obeying the command and fulfilling the promise. But I would rather argue on other ground. I would undertake to show, through all ecclesiastical history, that the worldliness of Bishops has always been the effect of their not believing in the dignity and divinity of their own ordination, and that any particular Bishop’s assumption has always been the effect of denying the dignity and effect of his brethren’s ordination. You show me a Bishop who is in every respect a splendid feudal lord, with his hounds and falcons, his sumptuous table, his armed retainers. I see a man who feels about his office just as you do: that it carries no Divine authority, and that he answers for it only to the class he moves in and the civil power that has added honours to it. I wish to cure him of the very habit of feeling you would fasten on him. But you may say, ‘Were he a Christian minister he would not be tempted to this worldliness.’ What do you mean? That he would not be tempted to any worldliness? That the parish clergyman, comfortably settled in his manse, is not tempted to sink into the habits of the ordinary middle class? That the mendicant friar or the itinerant Protestant preacher is not liable to be infected by the set he ministers to and lives by? You cannot say this without outraging Scripture and experience. Worldliness of some kind (its kind decided by the age, the man and his company) has assailed and always will assail every man in this world, and I believe the only deliverance from it is the belief that one has a vocation. Whether it accords with Providence that Christ’s ministers should also be ministers of the nation, and that each class of the nation should feel the influence of one of its classes, I shall consider in a later section. As to Episcopal assumption, I need only point to the history of popes and patriarchs, which shows that its occasion is always the exaltation of some advantage of place or circumstance attached to the order above the order itself.

Objections to an absolving power in ministers.

I have said that the main principle of the Presbyterian argument opposes the idea of a priesthood just as directly as it opposes Episcopacy, so in dealing with one question I have implicitly dealt with the other. The doctrine of a priesthood is also so bound up with the doctrine of sacrifice that my last section may stand as a sufficient statement of my views on it. Still, many minds feel such horror at the absolving power I have attributed to the Christian Bishop and those he endows with it, and their complaints have such practical consequences, that I should be wrong not to consider them separately. I do not class them as Presbyterian objections, because many Episcopalians seem to share them. They may be put like this:

‘According to St. Paul, those who believe in Christ are justified from all things from which the Law of Moses could not justify them. Ambassadors are sent to declare Christ’s Gospel so that men are not kept from believing by never having heard, and so that, if they believe, they may receive this justification and freedom of conscience. This is the minister’s true office, and all others are accidental and subordinate to it. He may recognise orders and forms of government in the Church, whether Presbyterian, Episcopal, Apostolical, or any other, but preaching Christ is his essential function, and his commission is to do this first and above all. When he claims some other way of relieving the conscience, when he says he has power to pardon and absolve and to pronounce men free from their sins, he not only commits a fearful usurpation of Christ’s rights but misunderstands and denies the true character of his own office. In his eagerness to grasp a power God never gave him, and which can never be of the least use to man, he deprives himself of his true power.’

One point here deserves special notice. In the Christian Church the person who has the function of absolving has always also had the function of administering the Eucharist. The two have never been separated, and they must be considered in relation to each other. In the Eucharist the worshipper is brought into direct communion with his Lord, and the human, visible agent is lost to view, or is seen only as a witness that the One he serves is a living, actual person. This must be a key to the whole character of the office, however it is exercised. The Eucharist also involves a confession of sins by the receivers. It involves a thankful acknowledgment of the fellowship and blessedness with their Lord into which they have been brought, though they have walked most unworthily of it. And it involves the acceptance of a pledge of forgiveness for the past, strength for the present (to be had only through union with the invisible Lord), and a promise of future blessings obtainable in the same way and no other. This would seem to settle the nature of the function the minister exercises when he pronounces absolution. His whole aim is to present Christ to men and men to Christ, really and practically. In the congregation, he represents its unity, offers it to God as one body, and confesses the sins its members have committed by separating themselves from that body. In doing so he witnesses to Christ’s continual intercession for the whole Church. Alone with one member, he is there to keep him in the unity of the Church and to present before God his tears and contrition for having lived unworthy of his place in it. Then he witnesses to Christ’s distinct intercession for each member of his flock. But this is only half his duty. The incarnation means very little, and the kingdom of God is a delusion, unless a voice speaks from heaven as well as one crying from earth, and the one answers the other. If the minister may not tell the congregation, ‘God has heard your petitions; rise up as pardoned men, with strength to offer praise and prayer and to do your work,’ then the confession is half real and the Gospel not real at all. If he may not likewise tell the sick and solitary penitent, ‘God accepts your tears and pardons your sin,’ I do not see what he means by claiming authority to preach forgiveness of sins. He preaches forgiveness to those who will accept it, understanding its nature and purpose, and receiving it as a deliverance of the conscience, not a licence for it. He delivers absolution under exactly the same conditions. How many of the congregation are in a state of mind to claim their fellowship with Christ and each other, and so take the mercy freely given, and whether any individual can do this, God only knows. The absolver has at least spoken the truth and acted out his commission, and he must leave the rest. His public preaching and private exhortations are all meant to remove stumbling-blocks from the way of those he is sent to, to explain the meaning of their confession and his absolution, and to keep them from offering the one or receiving the other in vain, or turning either to evil account.

If this is so, this doctrine seems no more perilous than the one that makes preaching the minister’s main office. As for misconstruction, it is no more likely with words that are not our own, spoken in God’s name on the most solemn occasion, than with words we have composed ourselves, which, whatever teaching we have from above, are mixed with our own modes of expression and habits of thought. Anyone who considers the matter without prejudice must admit this much. Such a person might then ask, ‘Where is the great difference? By your absolving power you mean just what others mean by preaching the Gospel. Make that clear, and no Christian would object to your statements any longer.’ I cannot make that explanation, because it would not be true. I do think there is a very great difference between two notions. One is that the act of absolution the minister pronounces in the Church’s name is the act that interprets the object of his preaching. The other is that he is sent to preach, and that because he preaches he may, in a certain sense, absolve. In the former case the minister presents Christ actually and personally to the congregation. His office witnesses to Christ’s presence among them and Christ’s relation to them, and it rests on the acknowledgment of an actual union between the body and its Head. In the other case there is much speech about Christ, his work, and his offices, which may be eloquent and true. But it is

a painted ship
Upon a painted ocean;

a description of what is good and beautiful and what man needs, but not the thing itself. I appeal to the history of modern preaching, and to the complaints of men on every side, whether this is not felt to be so. If it is, we must seriously ask how best to reach the ends which I fully believe my objectors sincerely desire: bringing men into more direct contact with the true and unseen Absolver, and making His ministers understand that they are nothing except as His representatives and do nothing except as they lead men to know Him. Consider patiently and calmly whether a priest who habitually believes that, as he may confess in the people’s name, so he may absolve in Christ’s name, must not have a humbler sense of his own insignificance, a greater confidence in an invisible kingdom, and a more serious conviction that all men are meant to be members of it, than one who believes he has gifts bestowed merely to enable him to announce the message of salvation. And do we not find that the best of those whose education and theories lead them to the latter opinion have been led, in practical life, in the conduct and discipline of their flocks, in their dealings with them as well as in their sermons to them, to act on the former?

The followers of Mr. Irving

Out of the heart of Presbyterianism has arisen a class of people whose objections to our Episcopacy take the opposite form from the one we were just considering. A few words about this modern development of church feeling will throw much light on the whole subject.

In answering the Quaker argument about the Old and New Testament ministry, I maintained that ministerial Succession is justified by the analogy of God’s dealings and has not been made obsolete by anything in the Gospel dispensation. The difference in how the idea is shown follows from the difference between a national and a universal kingdom. Hereditary tradition with solemn consecration expresses the first. Make consecration the substance of the tradition and abolish the family limit, and you have the true nature of the second. This is so obvious, and so much assumed in Christendom’s history, that it seems needless to dispute it except with those, like the Quakers, who reject outward ordination altogether. Admit ordination and you admit the principle of succession. You admit that it is unlikely to have been broken, in any important case, by people who habitually recognized it. At the least you admit that the burden of proof (onus probandi) lies on those who claim it was broken, to show when and how.

That would be the natural state of the argument if nothing else intervened. But the sons of those who abandoned Episcopacy came to feel that a great link with the older Church had been cut off. If my view of Episcopacy as the great bond of Christendom’s life is right, it was the great link. They were therefore far more concerned than their fathers, who felt the severance less, to prove two things: first, that there was some flaw in the idea of succession as Episcopalians held it; second, that they had an adequate substitute. The flaw was easily found. From the time of Hildebrand at least, and probably much earlier, down to the Reformation, the whole Church, apart from a few witnesses in the valleys of Piedmont, had been popish and corrupt. Any transmission of powers and gifts to later claimants must have come through the Bishops of this Church and no other. Second, they said the reformed bodies lost nothing by breaking formal ministerial connexion with the first ages, because they had kept the really important succession: they had inherited the pure apostolical doctrine.

As long as men could calmly accept that Christians are a sect professing certain sound opinions, more probable and useful than those of Mahomet or Confucius, and as long as this seemed to match what our Lord and the Apostles said they came to do, these two statements seemed reasonable. It was not strange that the sect should almost die out for a time, or survive in a few men whose names are confused and whose opinions and practices are more confused still. Nor was it strange that identity of opinions should be taken as the sign that the sect had reappeared, though it would be hard to prove in a court of law that the dogmas of the New Testament were the same as those prevailing in eighteenth-century Scotland and Germany. But the moment anyone woke to the fact that the Gospel speaks of a kingdom actually to be set up among men, they became very perplexed. At first it was easy to treat these words as belonging to the future, to what follows our Lord’s second appearing, not to the effect of his Incarnation and Ascension. But much could not bear that reading. If there is to be a Church in the world between the first and second advent, it must answer to these words and cannot be something wholly different in kind. A sect professing certain dogmas would be exactly that. The escape into a purely spiritual body was open only to the Quakers, and the Presbyterians had no sympathy with them. They had admitted an organisation. They had said the Church’s spiritual life ought to express itself through it, had treated the ministry as an indispensable part of it, and had constantly pointed to Scripture as showing the model of the Church’s constitution.

Pressed by these difficulties, the late Mr. Irving, in his commentary on Revelation, took refuge in the belief that the Kirk of Scotland had kept a succession of ministers in its Presbytery. At the same time he maintained, as stoutly as any countryman, that a human Episcopacy is incompatible with Christ’s universal lordship. But a man of so much reflection and honesty could not miss two things. Any ministerial succession foretold in Scripture or believed in any age of the Church was an Apostolical succession, a succession of persons holding the essential part of the Apostolical functions. And by his own argument he denied that any such function had descended to the Scotch ministers. His position could not stand long, and either he or, certainly, his followers soon left it. They admitted to some extent the truth and validity of the Episcopal succession hitherto in the Church, and at least the importance of the idea. But they said something had been missing since the first ages to make the Church complete: a distinct order of Apostles, who should either supersede the Bishops or be honored by them as the source of their authority.

Setting aside collateral questions, such as how these Apostles are to be identified, the reader will see how plausible this doctrine must be to men in the state of mind I have described. They have been taught that the Church was for many centuries in abeyance. They know from experience that no part of it is in a right condition now. They think the faults they see come from disorganisation. If a defect of organisation can be found that has existed from the very first ages, how easily the facts are explained. In the same way, two things are reconciled by this hypothesis: the habit, learned from their teachers, of looking to Scripture for every notion of the Church’s nature, and the conviction, gained in maturer years, that Scripture is explained by the Church’s history. They honor the plain letter of Scripture, yet it is a letter that means nothing except in reference to the progress of Christ’s kingdom. They can also connect a restoration of spiritual life and gifts with the strongest recognition of the Church’s organic and visible character. With such inward reasons for accepting this faith, there is little use attacking its outworks. Men are not likely to be laughed out of an opinion that brings all their other thoughts into harmony, by being told that they pay superstitious homage to the number twelve, or that the sudden recovery of an apostolic order after eighteen centuries is most improbable. To the first they will answer that, even if numbers carry no mystery, Scripture warrants attaching this number to this case. To the second they will answer that if God means to uphold His Church and heal its breaches, it is reasonable to believe He will by any means renew what seems indispensable to it.

Nor can I help seeing that some of the arguments used to defend Episcopacy and Episcopal succession tend to strengthen these convictions. Because Presbyterians have been the chief opponents, writers have in a way assumed two orders in the Church and tried to prove that a third was also needed. Resolving the ministry into deacons and presbyters plus an episcopate must leave any careful student of the New Testament with the impression that this episcopate has no close connexion with the apostolic order, which is plainly the root of all the others and the home in which they all originally dwelt. Again, certain ways of speaking about Episcopal succession have given many minds the feeling that it is necessary because the first Apostles were ordained by Christ Himself, and because the effect, or part of the effect, of that ordination has passed through a series of hands to their later representatives. I do not say any defender of the succession would put the reason this way. But its critics must suppose it is meant, or they would not joke about a virus losing its power through repeated inoculations, or about gifts spoiled by the unclean hands that passed them on. If such a feeling prevails at all, we can see how shocking it must be to men who believe that Christ is still present in the Church and would still give actual powers to His ministers if their faithlessness did not interfere.

But suppose it were calmly put to those who hold this theory, first, that according to the doctrine that has always prevailed in the Church, the episcopate does contain the administration of the sacraments, the giving of absolution, the preaching of the Gospel, and the care of the sick and poor, all the functions our Lord ever committed to His immediate disciples, and that the Bishops have, and ought to believe they have, all the powers needed for them. Second, suppose they were told that the Bishops’ connexion with earlier ages and the first Apostles is kept up expressly as a witness to the Church’s permanent constitution, and so to Christ’s continued abiding in it, each Bishop in each age being His servant and receiving gifts and powers directly from Him. They might then begin to see the whole subject differently. They might examine more carefully the grounds of their belief in an order above the bishops. Suppose they became convinced that any charm in the number of the Apostles belonged to their Jewish position and was broken by Christ Himself when He called Paul to the same dignity and through him broke down the middle wall between Jews and Gentiles. Suppose they became convinced that it is more likely, from the start, that an office established so solemnly would be upheld by Divine power than destroyed by human unbelief; that in every other case unbelief shows itself in doubting and denying the existence of powers and mistaking the character of a function actually held; and that such unbelief accounts for all the painful phenomena in the history of the episcopate. Then they might ask themselves, with some anxiety, whether there is any reason to expect that an order will be introduced by signs and wonders which seems already to exist. They might ask whether anything justifies their standing aloof from the body of Christ’s universal Church, and refusing to submit to those He Himself has placed over it.

The Philosophical Objector.

Between this class of reasoners and the one I must deal with next, there seems to be no point of mutual understanding. The modern rationalistic philosopher admits that the word “priest” carries much historical meaning, is not yet obsolete, and may have a good sense. He says: ‘Men generally err here, as elsewhere, by failing to separate the essential truth from the passing applications of the word. They do not see what the real and valuable power of the priest was, or who inherits it today. Churchmen boast that priests preserved letters, and led men to believe in a government that is inward, over the mind, not outward, over the body. That is true, and we should remember them gratefully for it. But those who now hold their name and claim a formal succession from them have no such influence. They do not preserve science or letters; they fear both, and care only to keep their religious system going. The real men of letters and science are the true priests: those who make us feel the bonds of spiritual fellowship between people of different nations, and who lead us to see fixed laws. They have the true insight to which men involuntarily pay homage. The more they are recognized, the less men regard outward ordination and the nominal priesthood. In the end only they will be honoured, and the counterfeit will be thrown out as withered and worthless.’

I cannot help sympathizing with the desire in this language: to find what is real and separate it from what is artificial, temporary and insincere. I will not ask whether it lies deep in everyone who uses these phrases, or whether they are learnt by rote and repeated glibly. Nor will I ask whether they offer some people a comfortable substitute for the hard work of freeing their own minds from the frivolity and falsehood they denounce so eloquently. Each person may ask himself such questions. I would rather take these words from the man who speaks them with the least self-deception, who most longs to be honest himself and to make his neighbours honest. To him I concede at once that the main proposition is right. The man of letters and the man of science are called by God to their work and are his ministers, and I wish they might feel themselves to be so. I will go further. Their function, especially that of anyone with a real poetic gift, answers in several important respects to that of the ancient prophet. They may fitly be said to do a similar work, with powers that match the circumstances of their own times. In what sense they do not correspond, and in what respect the Jewish prophet’s words have become a Scripture, taken out of the circle of ordinary words, I hope to consider in the next section. Here I am speaking of them as living, acting, speaking men, and from that standpoint it matters more to mark their essential likeness than their differences. I do not want the modern poet or philosopher to doubt that he has a high vocation and real inspiration; that is the feeling I would most wish to awaken in him. What I dread is that he should not feel it, that he should think his words are his own, glory in his powers, and so inevitably corrupt and abuse them. The misery of the last age, especially of its poets, was that they cast this belief away entirely. When they spoke of a Muse teaching them, they did not mean it. They used the phrase only because Homer had used it and Virgil copied it. They had no sense that the words were ever spoken honestly; the old singer to the maidens of the Greek isles had, they supposed, merely invented a clever ‘machinery.’ And critics who wrote edifying essays on “Paradise Lost” could suppose that Milton invoked the Spirit who prefers the upright heart and pure to all temples with the same profane insincerity. What could such an age produce but ingenious satires on the follies of the day, and laborious efforts to dress the ancients in its own laces and ruffles?

By all means, then, let those who feel their gifts strong train themselves to acknowledge them, and their source, with awe and humility. It should not alarm any religious man that our students and artists are inclined to make such claims for themselves. So far as they do, they have taken a great step out of the last generation’s infidelity. What is alarming is the pretension that goes with the claims: the loud talk of powers inherent, not derived, and the scorn these men show for others, which proves they rest on their own powers and recognize no sustaining, quickening inspiration. I cannot think that men who talk this way are likely to be prophets, or to recognize the prophetic gift in others. So for the sake of our men of letters and of the world, both need to be reminded, by clear and visible tokens, what they are, where their power comes from, on what conditions it must be used, and who makes it effective for men’s good. But no one wants these men themselves to receive a visible designation. It would not suit their office, and it would teach others little about their own position, since others regard the poet or man of letters as a person of a peculiar order, following a different impulse and pursuing different objects. But suppose there were a set of men occupied with the most obvious and interesting circumstances of humanity, those the tradesman and mechanic share with the man of genius: marriage and sickness, life and death. Suppose that in dealing with what belongs to all men, they dealt with it in relation to those awful inward feelings that carry us from the visible into the unseen world, and even connected these earthly accidents with fixed and eternal laws of that world. From the general, continuous and practical nature of their duties, such a class might well be chosen as a sign to all men of the meaning and source of all power, and to the sage of the meaning and source of the powers entrusted to him. It would be the natural link between those who tend to lose their sense of divine government in the monotony of daily events and those who tend to lose it in awareness of their own energies. Now suppose the chosen class itself forgot its position by falling into either temptation. It might court outward distinction and busy itself with a round of outward services, forgetting its mysterious meaning. Or, wanting to assert its difference from other vocations, it might become proud and self-exalting. I know of no better or more terrible witness against these sins, no more terrible prophecy of their effects, than the ordination that has marked the class out for the highest and lowest ministries. That ordination declares that we exist for the sake of men, that all our authority is from God, that our only safety is in forgetting ourselves, and that our highest privilege is to be instruments joining the members of our race to each other and to their Lord. Abolish our ordination, and you lose the strongest testimony against our sins. You lose too, I am well persuaded, one of the greatest safeguards against the degradation of our poets and men of learning, whether by sensual contamination or spiritual pride.

The Romish System.

It remains for me to explain how my views on the Christian ministry differ from those of the Romanists. On some very important points I must plainly agree with them. I have spoken of ministers as representing Christ to men; I have held that the power of absolution is real and not nominal; I have held that Apostolic functions and authority still exist in the Church; and I have admitted that the Jewish institutions have counterparts in the new dispensation. Let us ask, under each of these four heads, where the Catholic principle I have described resembles the Romish one.

First, I have spoken of ministers as representing Christ to men. Long before anyone claimed a supreme vicar of Christ on earth, men felt that the priest’s office was vicarial: ministers are deputed by our Lord to do now the work He did Himself while on earth. This notion has gone into the heart of the Romish system, and I believe it created it. Those who laugh at Athanasius contending to the death over an iota will be amused that I find an important difference between the words representative and vicarial. If the quarrel between the Nicene Fathers and the Arians was about a word and not a reality, both sides were weak and vain men. And I would be more foolishly at fault if I thought these two words had a charm, so that the one I reject could never be used well, or the one I adopt used ill. But I will try to show that the difference, whether or not these words express it rightly, is as essential and practical as any could be. By vicarial, the Romanist means that the priest does the work of one who is absent, and who presents himself to men only at certain times and under certain conditions. By representative, I mean that the minister sets Christ forth to men as present in His Church at all times, exercising Himself the functions He took up when He ascended on high. This is a radical difference, not about a word but about the most solemn question the human mind can face. Nor is it isolated; it is closely tied to all the differences we noticed in earlier sections. The Catholic principle I have tried to develop regarding Baptism, the Eucharist, the Creed, and the Forms of Worship is that of a direct, real, practical union between men and their Lord. The Romish doctrine, which we found in each of these cases, is that the veil between us and the invisible world has not yet been withdrawn, and that offices and ordinances are not the organs through which men and their Lord converse, but mere outward things that He has stamped with a certain authority and virtue, or mere pictures that show Him to the imagination. Happily this system has never fully realised itself; the nature of things seems to make that impossible. The moment it becomes, or nearly becomes, what it is always striving to be, it loses its meaning and turns into an oppressive phantom, and the judgments of God, together with the faith and the unbelief of man, seem to join in confounding it.

2. It follows that the absolving power I claim for the Catholic priest is entirely different from that claimed by the Romish priest. I do not say it is less; it seems to me much greater. He can declare in Christ’s name to a man that the union with Christ assured to him in baptism is still his; that he has done evil by living inconsistently with it; that this evil will not be imputed to him, though it may perhaps be sorely punished for his good, if he turns to God and claims the better life that is his in his Lord; that he will have strength from Christ to serve Him; and that he will know Him, and this knowledge will make him free. He can say these words confidently because they are true and because he has a commission to declare them to such and such men, and neither their truth nor his commission is affected by the unbelief and unrepentance of those he addresses. His is a power of absolution touching past, present and future, and he would be sorry to exchange it for any exercised by those who claim it vicarially as their own rather than representatively as their Lord’s. For anyone who knows history must see that the vicarial power did not absolve the human spirit but bound it in heavy chains, giving it no sense of the glorious liberty Christ bought for it, and teaching it to grovel when God has given it wings to soar. How could penances ever have been turned from their proper use — means of subduing the evil habits that make the spirit proud and unfree — into shackles and torments of conscience, checks on holy and thankful devotion, and instruments of pride and self-exaltation, if those who imposed them had really felt they acted for the great Redeemer and Absolver? Had they not said in their hearts, “The Lord delayeth His coming,” and so thought themselves entitled to beat the servants while they ate and drank and got drunk? How could the monstrous idea of indulgences enter the minds of men who had not lost the sense of their direct subjection to an invisible Lord, and who therefore became slaves of those they claimed to rule, forced to cater to their fleshly and worldly appetites in order to keep them in bondage? These may be old stories, but they are plainly written in the history of the world. They are not to be exaggerated to support Protestant conclusions, nor to be erased by any trickery of another kind, nor overlooked because we choose to think opposite facts concern us more. Facts cannot contradict each other. The records of the miseries the Romish system produced cannot contradict those which prove, even to the most thoughtful Liberals of our day, that the Catholic Church has conferred innumerable blessings on mankind. If we understand both, they must confirm and illustrate each other. What we need to find is the point where they connect.

3. I believe that point lies in the Romanist application of the vicarial doctrine to episcopal or apostolic authority. On the representative doctrine, all ministers exhibit Christ in the office to which they are called; the whole body of bishops, each in his own sphere, present Him to men as the bishop or overseer of the Church. Once make ministers vicarial, and we have the seed of an entirely new scheme. The oneness and universality of Christ’s office distinguish Him from each of His representatives and from all of them together. But if Christ is absent from His Church, that oneness and universality are lost to the world and are mere dreams. They must therefore be pictured somewhere, since they have lost their force as realities. Ministers must not only be vicars of Christ; there must be a vicar of Christ, who absorbs into himself and exhibits his one universal episcopacy. Here is the Popedom. It may have developed very gradually, and it could not appear openly as a Church idea while something so like it was openly realised as the idea of the World in the Roman emperors. But it was latent in the minds of all ministers who took on a vicarial character and of all laymen who acknowledged them in it. The conception of an apostolic primacy in St. Peter, on which it seems to rest, is a mere creature of this idea. Considered in itself it is a harmless, perhaps legitimate, historical theory; but once it has received the vicarial poison, it can support one of the greatest denials and contradictions in the annals of the universe. This view of the Popedom is not just the one we met when we considered how men were led to look for a great dogmatist to give them right and safe opinions; it is the other, kingly aspect of the office, in which it presents itself either as the true law of Christ’s kingdom or as its flagrant violation. If Christ is really in His Church, and all its offices declare Him to men, then a Pope is the most frightful of anomalies, and his existence is the key to all the other anomalies in Christian history. If Christ is not really in His Church, if there is no real connection between Him and those who speak in His name, or only an individual one, and there is no spiritual constitution among men, then I own I do not see how the popish system can fail to commend itself as the most comprehensive, effective and practical religious organisation ever conceived. Showing the evils it has produced will not materially weaken this conviction. They will be called excesses. We shall be asked what is so good or so Divine that it is not exposed to corruption by the corrupt human will, and how we account for the good that flowed in the Middle Ages, not from an idea of Christianity merely, but from that idea as expressed in the organisation of the Church. I think we can answer as we have before: ‘This papal system is itself, in its simplest and best form, the result of the corrupt human will you speak of. It is not the excess but the counterfeit of the Church constitution, a violation of an idea and also of the organisation in which that idea is embodied. Because it could not destroy that idea or organisation, the Church Catholic was able to spread some of the blessings God meant it to spread. Just so far as the system prevailed, and did not contradict itself by asserting the principles it sets at naught, it has hindered God’s mercies from reaching the world and turned them into curses.’ Such language asks to be tried by Scripture, by history, by the conscience of papists themselves, and by the truths they profess and some of them, I am convinced, hold most dear. The other language, which supposes there is no spiritual constitution of Christ’s ministry, has, I believe, done more, and is now doing more, to promote and establish Popery than all its own diligent efforts.

4. I must still briefly mention the connection I suppose, and the Romanist supposes, between the Jewish and the Christian economy. We agree thus far: we both believe the connection is real; that it concerns an ecclesiastical economy; and that the forms of the Jewish Commonwealth, so far as they were not merely national or oriental, were translated into corresponding forms and not merely into spiritual notions. Where do we differ? In this all-important point: we regard the Incarnation, Resurrection and Ascension of our Lord as declaring Him to be really and actually, not nominally or fantastically, head of the universal kingdom, as the mortal High Priest had been of the peculiar kingdom, all Jewish history being a preparation for substituting the one for the other. They believe that this High Priest has been succeeded in the new dispensation by one as mortal and sinful as himself; that he is to preserve the doctrines of the Creed to the Church while practically, in his own person, declaring that they do not mean what they seem to mean; that no real connection has been established between man and God in the person of the Mediator; and that the Church is not what her Creed affirms, united to Him in His victory as well as in His humiliation. Again, then, we contend, with more conviction and earnestness the nearer we come to the heart of the subject, that the Romish system and the Catholic Church are not identical and have no natural affinity, but are deadly opposites, one of which must perish if the other is to survive.
