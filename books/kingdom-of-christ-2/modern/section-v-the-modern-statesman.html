Section V.—The Modern Statesman

A statesman who claims sole authority over education, fearing the Church more than divided sects, cannot properly nurture family life, science, art, or morality, as a national Church can; and restoring the Jews would testify to the sanctity of nations without displacing the universal Church, as Christ’s return will unveil rather than replace the kingdom already here.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Such statements, however unwelcome to many Churchmen, will not win over modern politicians or ease their fear of ecclesiastical influence. They may even say that the power I claim for the spiritual body is more dangerous than the one I give up. ‘A statesman has to admit that a dominant hierarchy exists, and has little right to complain, since in ordinary times it helps keep other sects quiet and clergymen indifferent. But when you say this hierarchy is the proper educator of the land, you are not content to humour it with a toy. You want it to become practically and politically mischievous. A power that can educate a land must rule over its families, arts, literature, science, ethics and philosophy, and, as head of the professional classes, in one sense over its law and medicine. No government today can tolerate such claims except where circumstances compel it. Religious bodies must affect a country’s education for good or ill, but they are happily broken into fragments, and the statesman’s business is to see that each acts only as a religious body. The general education of the land he must gradually take under his own direction, after first removing the old prejudices and institutions in the way. If he lets any ecclesiastical body superintend it, that body becomes as dangerous as the Jesuits ever were. For their real power lay not in governing courts but in the schools, and less in the poison they poured into monarchs’ ears than in the maxims of ecclesiastical submission and craft they gave children along with their primers and grammars.’

In the first division of this work I traced the political and philosophical movements that have led men today to see education as the only adequate means of preserving government and society. I showed that the line of thought leading to this opinion contradicts the practical results it seems to promise. The statesman wants education as a power to act on men’s spirits, yet the statesman of our day has formally repudiated any claim to deal with men’s spirits and has blamed his forefathers for assuming it. So when he claims to be the educator of his land, he is in a strange contradiction, though a natural one. He found that warring religious bodies could not exercise the influence over his subjects that he feels they need. He wants them united and harmonised, and a sect-educator sets them at strife. So he leaves the religious bodies the right to teach their own dogmas and takes under his own cognizance whatever else education includes. This course is awkward and inconvenient, even destructive of the very idea of education. Yet it seemed the only one left to him, unless some spiritual body existed in the heart of his nation, as organic as the civil body and able to do what the civil body confesses it cannot. Our later inquiries have led us to believe that there is such a body: it has established itself in every European nation, has taught every nation, and has incorporated itself with civil society. The statesman finds society everywhere bearing witness to it, and must depose it from its functions before he can become schoolmaster himself. That is where the question stands, and since it has been discussed elsewhere I shall not treat it at large. But I need to indicate the difference between the education given by a national Church that understands its own powers and responsibilities and that given, first, by the State, second, by a set of sects, third, by an extra-national ecclesiastical order like the Jesuits. Following the order of the supposed objections, I will speak briefly of how each affects family life, science, art and literature, popular ethics, and philosophy.

I. 1. I have spoken more than once about the first of these. I have shown that a mere religious body, such as the Quakers or Calvinists, may reverence family life and at certain stages may even preserve it in great purity, but cannot connect the institution of the family, as such, with its religion. The religious man is one who chooses for himself, who at a certain time has been led to seek a new life and a new fellowship expressing it. That is how a sect begins. The notion is greatly modified once the sect is established: hereditary feelings develop, and deserting the faith of one’s forefathers begins to be called an evil. Still, the society subsists on the same principle, and those admitted to its privileges do not grow into them. The religious body is regarded as different in kind from the family. So every sect complains that where hereditary habit prevails, religion becomes a matter of course and loses its power, and violent efforts are needed to revive it. I maintain that such influences cannot cultivate a nation’s family life, because they do not bring the spiritual life into direct relation with natural kinsmanship. Many influences now threaten household sanctities, and many schemes of universal society cast them aside altogether. A statesman who has no better protection for them than the teaching of sects must expect to see them perish. Where there are no political motives, no trade-temper, no grand philosophical generalisations, religious men in the sects may hope to keep respect and attachment alive in their children. Where these are abroad, they must tremble, and so must anyone who believes the nation’s existence depends on the domestic relations if sects are their only guardians.

2. But what can the statesman himself do by his education to protect these relations? Nothing. Must he not rather do much to shake confidence in them and impair their sacredness? It is no fault of his; it is the necessity of his position that he aims at making men citizens. He cannot teach them to be sons and brothers, and he has to interfere with the duties that belong to those roles. He must found his schools on the express principle that parents are not competent to teach or to choose teachers, and he must treat the father’s authority as if its sacredness depended on the authority of the law. All wise statesmen of antiquity felt this difficulty and were glad of any means of escaping it. Modern statesmen should ask themselves earnestly whether any such help is within their reach.

3. Most of those I am arguing with will acknowledge that the Jesuit is not the person to whom family life can safely be entrusted. They may also agree, and may wonder that I concede it, that the evil of his influence comes not solely, or perhaps chiefly, from the particular opinions he teaches, and that a Protestant order of the same kind would be almost equally mischievous. Where, then, on my principle, does the evil lie? Here: I believe God has established a universal Church in the world, which grew out of a family and embodies the idea of family life in its highest expansion. That idea stays fresh and real only so long as a strict, unbroken connexion is kept between its highest form and its lowest, so long as calling Him who was, who is, and who is to come “Father” is felt to be no figurative abuse but the only possible explanation of the word’s most ordinary use. And I believe Ignatius Loyola founded a universal order on a principle altogether different from this divine one, indeed subversive of it. Such an order cannot preserve any part of the true constitution of society; it must keep interfering with it and substituting something else; and family order and this pseudo-ecclesiastical order must be perpetual, irreconcilable enemies.

4. If so, I need not argue at length that the spiritual and universal society whose signs we have found should be the great instrument for building up and sanctifying each nation’s domestic society, which divided sects, the civil power, and a merely seeming universal fellowship cannot do. It takes the family by baptism into God’s family, supposes all civil duties and relations to grow out of these first ones, and connects the highest ecclesiastical duties with ordinary social duties.

II. 1. How far religious bodies or sects can be trusted with a nation’s scientific education can be judged from how they have felt about science at different stages of their history. Almost without exception, a sect at the height of its faith and sympathy has looked on science as wholly alien to faith and irreconcilable with it. The cause may have been a Manichæan horror of the outward world, or dread of it as too holy to touch, or dislike of the slow, cold methods by which its secrets are learned, or, as with the Quakers, a dim intuition of a link between the laws of physical and spiritual investigation that ordinary philosophers had overlooked. The result was the same. A generation or two later, outwardly at least, everything changes. Persons with a genius for physical speculation arise in these sects and devote themselves to it; members generally feel that such pursuits can no longer be discouraged as they were in their fathers’ days. A few sturdy Protestants remain to warn the young against perils that a sure instinct tells them are real, while others, who think themselves wiser and more enlightened yet very religious, explain what lessons about the Divine wisdom and goodness may be drawn from natural discoveries. Some find this last change promising; to me the old state was far better. The old teachers acted on a principle: man’s business is to know his Creator and do His will, they saw no connexion between these studies and that end, and so they rejected them. Their descendants, when they first take up these pursuits, complain not that the application of the maxim was narrow but that the maxim itself was, since a man who attends properly to his religious duties may give a fair share of time to pursuits with a different aim. Soon the scientific pursuits feel genuine and the religious duties artificial and traditional; if the former are not wholly followed and the latter neglected, there is at least no sympathy between them. Then if a religious revival comes, and the feelings embodied in the sects shape public opinion and create what is called a religious world, science is made to tell a tale about the truth of Scripture and the being and attributes of God. A moral is wrung from its facts by fair means or foul, often by dishonest construction of evidence or suppression of what has been proved. If a fact contradicts a current theological notion or interpretation of Scripture, it is not looked at fairly; the question is raised whether there ought to be such a fact, and so whether we may recognise it if it is one. Scientific men are rightly disgusted. They see that the cause of honesty as well as of science is at stake, and they begin to suspect any hypothesis the more for its gratifying religious feelings.

2. Here the civil power steps in and says, ‘We cannot permit this; our subjects must be taught science fairly and truly. We must have railways and steam-engines. In the present state of society even our handicraftsmen must understand something of the machinery they work, and they need knowledge of many subjects unknown to their ancestors. Your religious men may make what corrections or draw what inferences they please, but we must teach the things; we must give our countrymen a scientific culture.’

I have only this to say in answer: I believe this determination will defeat itself. Teaching a multitude of things is not scientific culture but fatal to it. Those who defend this education like to name Bacon. Would that they studied him with real pains! They would find him denouncing, as one of the main hindrances to scientific knowledge and progress, the desire for facts that are “fructiferous” and not “luciferous,” that lead to mere results and not to the search for higher principles. His whole aim was to teach how to seek laws in facts, not how to make from a heap of observations first a theory and then a machine. He blamed the passion for mere effects and so-called practical results for most of the delusions and crimes of the alchymists. If he reappeared today and heard himself praised as the man who taught that making shoes is nobler than seeking principles, he would believe the very mischiefs he delivered his countrymen from were returning through the abuse of his own wisdom. Yet this is the doctrine a merely political statesman inevitably adopts, and it has ever been, and must be, the maxim of State education.

3. The Jesuits cannot be accused of neglecting to give their scholars information on physical subjects. Nor does it appear that they tried to restore old theories or teach opinions other than those generally sanctioned by the philosophers of their day. As the Dominicans and Franciscans reversed the papal decree against Aristotle, so the Jesuits seem practically to have reversed the decree against Galileo, eagerly using the direction men’s minds were taking toward physical inquiries to turn them away from inquiries more immediately concerning themselves. Here, as everywhere, their instruction followed one principle in one coherent system: teach everything, whether physics, history or philosophy, so that the student feels he is not apprehending a truth but receiving a maxim on trust or studying a set of probabilities. On this rule they could publish an edition of the “Principia,” noting that its main doctrine had been denounced by the Pope and was therefore to be rejected, while recommending the book for its ingenious arguments and apparent demonstrations. Strange as it seems, there was no curl of the lip in this, nor any dishonesty in the usual sense. The editors did not believe Newton had proved his point; they lacked the feeling of certainty needed to think anything could be proved. All is one sea of doubts, perplexities and possibilities, and the great necessity is to feel that we cannot reach truth and must therefore submit to an infallible authority. That was the habit of their minds. The religious man can judge whether it was true when he considers its effect in producing the scepticism of the eighteenth century; the scientific man, when he thinks how hopeless of progress those who hold it must be.

4. A national Church believes it exists to cultivate the inward man, as the civil power exists for the outward man, and that it has a commission for this. It must therefore witness continually against all these notions of education. It cannot tolerate for an instant the sectarian notion that studying the laws by which God framed this universe is not a solemn, religious work, to be done reverently alongside the study of the laws on which He built the moral universe. As she believes there is a method for knowing the one constitution, so she believes there is a method for knowing the other. The two methods may be connected but cannot be the same. The spiritual method is not honoured when you force physical facts into obedience to it; you are certain they cannot contradict it, and sure they will illustrate it ten thousandfold more than all your moralities laid over them. A national Church must believe, in the highest sense, that what is is right. This is the pillar of her own existence, and she sets it against the world’s maxim that things are right which we make so by our rules and conventions. So she must teach her children to ask boldly, “What is?”, encourage them to expect an answer, and teach them in what frame of mind to wait for it, receive it, and give thanks for it.

This lesson is very unlike the one the civil power tries to teach in its education. The spiritual teacher in his own sphere leads men into the secret heart of things, teaching them the laws of their own being and their direct relation to the Creator. In physical science he must act on the same principle. He cannot merely teach facts and opinions; he must guide his pupil to a knowledge of laws. He will follow this method with the higher or professional classes under his discipline. He cannot change it, though he may entirely alter his scheme of instruction with the lowest classes. They too are men, who want to know and have a right to know the order in which they are placed and the meaning of what they do, and who must not merely be taught what to do or be given rules for doing it.

So it is almost needless to say that such a teacher regards authority not as the Jesuit does, as a substitute for truth, but as what puts us on the right way of searching for it. A national church believes she is set in the midst of a nation by Him who “for this end was born, and for this end came into the world, that He might bear witness to the truth.” She is there to bear witness of it, to rebuke the slavish and godless tempers that hinder men from seeking truth in any direction, and by leading them to know it to make them free.

III. How literature and art are likely to be treated by these different classes can be guessed from the foregoing. Sects in their infancy reject both as worldly and heathenish; in their manhood and decline they tolerate them as necessary indulgences, or try to make them religious by sugaring them over with Christian phrases. The civil power encourages both, because they supply diversion and entertainment to different classes, but judges their value by how far they serve immediate utility. The Jesuit favours the literary diligence of laborious compilations, annals and chronologies, and the art that helps tie devotion more closely to the senses. So in each of these forms of education, from different causes, there is the same tendency to give human utterances, whether in books, pictures, sculpture, music or architecture, an artificial, outward, fictitious character. Either they become insincere expressions of what is actually in men’s hearts, or the hearts themselves are made insincere by constantly aiming at some effect to which the names “moral, useful, religious” are, by great abuse of language, applied. But a body that really believes it has a commission to cultivate the mind and spirit of a nation, to call forth what is truest and noblest, awaken reason, understanding and affections, give them their key-note and bring out their harmonies, will feel that the men God has given power to express their own minds and the mind of their age, in words or in sensible forms, have a high vocation and mighty responsibility. It will feel that the influences of the world are likely to choke their powers and keep them from expanding freely and happily, and that the spiritual mother must brood over them with tender care. She will cheer them amid outward and inward discouragement, give them the soothing food and medicine of peaceful devotions and outward images of serenity, stir them with heroic examples, and make them conscious of their relation to past and future. She will hold up high and distant ends, so that they are not crushed by their age or tempted to court its approval. She will humble them that they may be exalted, and teach them to discover the invisible in the visible instead of confounding them and bringing the higher under the conditions of the lower. In training the more illustrious citizens of the commonwealth she is in fact marking out the course by which all should be trained who are to be citizens indeed, for God has committed some trust to each, to be fulfilled for His glory and the good of the land.

IV. 1. I will close this subject with a few words on ethics. Strictly speaking, the sectarian does not recognise such a study. He thinks the religious man has been taken into a position altogether different from other men’s, and that he and they are not under the same law. A set of rules and maxims must be observed by those who would be members of the family and citizens of the community, and the religious man submits to these, but he is also subject to Gospel influences that the ordinary man has nothing to do with. Christian ethics mean the religion of the heart according to the Bible and apply only to the converted; worldly ethics mean regulating conduct by the maxims received among worldly men and apply to the unconverted. On this showing, a morality for man as man does not exist.

2. So the statesman interferes, as in the other cases, and says, ‘Then you shall teach the morality that belongs to your position, and I will teach that which belongs to mine. Men must acknowledge some rule of life. These subjects of mine, converted or unconverted, must be trained to some sense of their relations to each other. Legal penalties alone are not enough; they must be taught some reason for their conduct, some method of self-government.’ Of course these reasons and methods must turn on maxims of self-interest. How could they turn on any other? The statesman has been warned off religious ground, and this is all that remains to him.

3. I need not speak at length of Jesuit ethics, which are in bad odour among us and probably in most Continental nations. I wish only to remark that all the evil in them flows from the first principle of founding a universal order on a human calculation of what is expedient for preserving the Church and religion. Once you build a society as powerful and coherent as the Jesuit society, the preservation of the order inevitably becomes the one great end to which all others are subordinate. To keep this great machine moving and effective, everything must be sacrificed. I do not think there is one pernicious maxim in the Constitutions that cannot be legitimately deduced from this primary assumption. The Jesuits feel about morality, as about science, not that it is but that it has been made, and so may be remade for a higher object. The world has framed its maxims to keep itself alive; the Jesuit may frame his to keep the holy religious order alive. The object is surely better, but in both cases the ways are determined by arrangement and convention.

4. Once again, a national Church exists to protest against these outrages upon the very ground of a nation’s existence. It affirms that morality is universal: in its highest form it is meant and attainable for all men, since the covenant of Baptism takes all who will receive it into the highest state a man on earth can enjoy, with all help for resisting the powers of the flesh, the world and the devil that seek to rob him of his human privilege. It affirms that morality is in direct opposition to selfishness, in its lowest forms as well as its highest, since the lawgiver’s penalties and the Church’s prayers are both aimed at this sin, one denouncing its outward effects and the other seeking to root out the inward disease. It declares that morality, in its lowest forms as well as its highest, is grounded on the character and will of God, subjection to that will being the lesson of the law and conformity to that character the effect of the power of the Gospel. So it must of necessity hate and curse such schemes of morality as the Jesuits have sanctioned. These schemes pervert a truth, that each case has peculiar points and delicate complications requiring wisdom, refinement and freedom from rash judgment in the one who deals with it, into the confounding doctrine that there is no common law of right and wrong, or that the creatures to whom it is addressed have no conscience to perceive it.

I have two remarks before I conclude this head. First, I believe all the defects of national Churches, and of the education they have given, can be traced to a notion far too common among their members and ministers: either that their position is sectarian, that they are merely civil bodies built for civil ends, or that they are merely parts of a religious society or order existing for purposes wholly foreign to those of the civil power. If an opponent produces facts showing the weakness and inefficiency of these Churches, or any one of them, I will gladly consider them. I am satisfied they will all confirm my view, prove the inherent viciousness of the schemes of education that have at various times seemed most plausible, and give another reason why every national Church should understand its high position and zealously assert it. My other remark is addressed to the statesman. He has felt in most European countries, and still feels, the peril of Jesuit influence and the need to guard against it. But what can he do? If he tries to get rid of the idea that there is one Catholic Church in the world, and treats religion as merely private sectarian opinion, he will not stop the Jesuits from entering his dominions and becoming masters of his schools. His tolerant maxims will ease their settlement, and the earnest cry that will rise in a land left to sectarian influence for some united body, some organic fellowship, will make their arrival welcome. Will he then, in despair, resort to his own peculiar powers, proscribe and banish the intruders, or put them to death? He knows these methods have been tried and tried in vain: the crushed order has risen again, its other influences strengthened by the credit of persecution and martyrdom. This subtle, Protean society knows one barrier, and only one, that it cannot break through: a national Church, strong in the conviction of its own distinct powers, paying respectful homage to those of the State, educating all classes to be citizens by making them men. The Jesuit regards that spectacle with wonder and despair. Where such a Church exists he may safely be allowed to walk up and down in the land; the sting of his order is taken away, and he may become a worthy and respectable member of the commonwealth. If the statesman is convinced that maiming and finally suppressing such a Church is the true object of his policy, he must invent some new charm for laying this enemy. None has yet been discovered.

Section VI.—The Modern Interpreters of Prophecy.

There is one class of people I sincerely respect who may be offended by these observations: the modern interpreters of Prophecy. First, they will think that I professed great reverence for the Old Testament as the national history of the Jews, yet overlooked one of its most remarkable features, its promise of permanence and restoration to God’s ancient people. In doing so, they will say, I showed indifference to inspired words and a preference for my own theories. Second, they will say that my notion of a Divine constitution already established, one that is national as well as spiritual and universal, in practice sets aside the doctrine of the second coming of Him who will make all things new. Third, they will say that when I spoke of the Romish system as distinct from the Latin Church, I overlooked the Divine word’s clear declarations about judgments on the apostasy and the final cutting off of every body that belongs to it.

1. I agree with those who look for a national restoration of the Jews that much of the language commonly used against their views comes from prejudice and misunderstanding. I do not understand, for instance, what is meant by “carnal” when it is used against the doctrine that there are other privileges besides those of the Church as a spiritual body, which the Jews once had and will have again. If I am right, these privileges are as necessary a witness against carnality as those that belong to us as children of God and heirs of the kingdom of heaven. By carnality I mean the inclination of the flesh, which is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be. In one sense these privileges are a stronger witness, because they touch more directly the acts of our earthly life, which tend to call carnality forth. And far from this witness belonging less to our age than to earlier ones, I believe our characteristic weakness is to put religion in a middle region. We will not understand it either in its most transcendent character or in its bearing on common, daily doings. So I suspect these phrases about carnality. They show a tendency that the sight of a national commonwealth like the Jewish one would counteract more effectively than anything seen now, or perhaps ever seen in the Catholic Church. Nor do I see how the reappearance of such a commonwealth on its original soil could be anything but a marvellous and glorious testimony to the great scheme that God, to whom all his works are known from the beginning of the world, has been carrying on, and which no human self-will can frustrate. Everything seems to me to point this way. A people as strange as the Israelites could not have been allowed to exist for so many generations without country or polity unless this destiny were in reserve for them. It is a strange and painful effort even to imagine national distinctness lost in men who, in glory and in depression, have witnessed to it for nearly three thousand years. That would be a far more startling miracle than a Hebrew commonwealth in Palestine; but the first miracle would violate all the analogy of God’s dealings, while the other would be their natural consummation.

These conclusions seem to me so reasonable that I ask myself why I found them so hard to reach, and why so many people, less hindered than I may be by prejudice or want of faith, still find the same difficulty. I cannot help feeling that the usual way of stating the Jews’ claims has been a great obstacle. At one time the modern interpreters seem to expect the Jewish nation to take the place of the universal Church. At another, they seem to expect Jerusalem to be the centre of that Church in the next age, as Rome has tried to be in this one. At another, they seem to believe that in the restoration of all things the Jews will be the one specimen of a true and godly nation. Those who use language that seems to imply these views may not really hold them. But even coming near such notions may rightly alarm good men. If we are to relapse into a national dispensation, and the idea of the universal Church is to be absorbed into that of an exclusive society, then all the promises made to the Fathers are set at naught. The truth with which Old Testament history was pregnant has come to nothing, St. Paul’s mighty conflicts to prove it had been brought into the light were in vain, and the last eighteen centuries have been a blank in human history. If the principle is admitted that anywhere, in profane land or holy land, the spiritual Church can find a visible capital for herself, the principle of Romanism is confirmed, and all the sad experiments that showed it to be ungodly are made worthless. If the Jewish nation or Church is to exalt itself in solitary greatness over the ruins of a fallen universe, then Isaiah and the prophets were wrong and the Pharisees of our Lord’s day were strictly right. I do not mean only that Isaiah and the prophets looked forward to a universal dispensation, a Church developing out of one nation. I mean that they always speak of Judea, even in their own time, as the centre of a group of countries, each of which was (or was trying to be) a nation. The burden of the “Valley of Vision” is joined to the burdens of Egypt, Moab, Tyre and Damascus. The Jewish nation interprets to each of these what it ought to be. Each is seen as standing in some relation to the Lord God of the Hebrews, as part of His scheme of judgment and mercy, and as threatened by the same Babylonian power. The Pharisees had wholly lost this feeling. They wanted only that Judea should be supreme as Rome was supreme, that it should in fact be the Babylonian monarchy it had testified against for so many generations. But suppose all these views of Jewish restoration were given up. Then what I have said of the state in this chapter may strike earnest, thoughtful men as the true explanation and justification of an idea they cherish so devoutly, and which I hope they will not be forced to abandon, whether by their opponents’ arguments or by their own inconsistencies.

I look on the Jewish nation as an abiding sign to the Christian Church of the honour God has put on national life, and of His will that the Church should never try to set itself up apart from the nations. I look on it as the sign to every nation, East or West, of the law under which it is constituted and by which it will be judged. Because I believe this to be a true and unchangeable law, I believe it will in the end make itself good in each case. One great central manifestation of it may be, and I trust will be, the restoration of the Jewish Commonwealth. I hope that restoration will be followed by the restoration to national life, joined with Christian and Catholic life, of the countries now united under the prophet’s sceptre. They are separated from each other by the most violent sectarian controversies and cannot understand how they might be distinct and yet one. In a Christian Jew a Mahometan sees what he was meant to be, and sees embodied the truth he has been twisting into a denial and a falsehood. So I cannot quarrel with those who dream that Jews will convert the Mahometans, and that a Hebrew nation will be the sun and centre of the Eastern world. But no one pretends that such a result will come without great conflicts and heavy judgments. Why may I not suppose, then, that the West will reach a like blessing by the like process? Why may I not suppose that the principle of Judaism will be asserted, and the exclusiveness of Pharisaism confounded, by the full development of the European nations and their colonies elsewhere in the world, with the universal Church still the life-giving power and uniting principle for them all?

2. But all these hopes are decisively refuted if they set at naught the truth of our Lord’s second appearing, on which the Church has rested in her greatest troubles and to which the Scriptures so constantly urge us to look forward. I would be more sorry than most to weaken this faith in any mind. It seems to me that its revival in our day has been a great means of clearing away the clouds that kept us from seeing Christ’s Church as a Kingdom, and from connecting all individual blessings and rewards with its existence and establishment as such. The wretched notion of a private, selfish Heaven, where troubles are compensated and prizes given for duties done in this lower sphere, had spread through our popular teaching and our theological books. It spoke in the language of Scripture and of earlier days of the Church, but cut that language off from the idea it had always carried and tied it to our low, grovelling, mercantile habits of feeling. Those who merely preached justification by faith could not drive it out. In their eagerness to be rid of human merit, they seemed to take away the hope of reward altogether, and while giving the conscience present relief, they left the heart and spirit with no future object to aspire to. Suppose that, after the feeling either of these teachings was likely to produce, some country in Europe has come to a vague and indistinct but perhaps real acknowledgment of another end for men than the selfish individual one: the end of beholding Him in whom is no selfishness and no darkness at all, of sharing the light of a common sun, and feeling a common warmth and life from its rays. I believe that change must be ascribed largely to those who steadfastly asserted, against much opposition and despite the refutation of their own favourite schemes of interpretation, that the Church is to live in expectation of the appearing and triumph of her Head.

But the more strongly I feel our debt to these teachers, the more I regret one thing. I do not mean particular crude opinions that have mixed with this faith, for those we must always expect. I mean any great central confusion that has weakened the grounds it rests on, made it unacceptable to wise and thoughtful men, and given almost unlimited licence to the speculations of those who are neither. Such a confusion seems to me to lurk in the notion that the Advent, or, as St. Paul far more often calls it, the Epiphany of our Lord, will begin a new order and constitution of things. The phrases of Scripture about this event seem to me to refute that idea outright. A light that shows things as they are and before which the darkness flees, a day of judgment and distinction, the gathering of all into one, the restoration of all things: this is the language in which we are taught to express our expectations of the future. We are obliged to use it even when our own theories would suggest other language as more suitable. What do such words imply but the full evidence and demonstration of that which is now, and the dispersal of all the shadows and appearances that have counterfeited it or hidden it from view? What do they imply but the existence of a kingdom, order or constitution that men have tried to set at naught and deny, though they have been living under it all along, and which in the clear sunlight of that day is shown to be the only one they can live under? This is exactly in line with those who speak most about the second Advent when they say it will come at a time of great darkness and almost universal denial. Denial of what? Surely of a truth always recognized in our institutions and ordinary habits, which men have only just found courage to reject outright as inconsistent with their conduct and other professions. It will come at the moment that shows that conduct and those professions to be false, and conscience’s witness against them, in favour of what they resisted, to be true. Is there any shelter from this conclusion in the distinction between the spiritual dispensation or kingdom that has existed since our Lord’s first Advent, and the outward, visible kingdom to be set up after His second? If “outward and visible” means that something less spiritual is in reserve than now exists, that now we live by faith but then shall live by sense, that now we see the highest glory in what “eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, nor it hath entered into the heart of man to conceive,” but then shall see all glory in the visible and comprehensible, I cannot conceive a darker or more dreadful vision of millennial perfection. But if it means that Christ’s dominion will reach beyond the heart and spirit, beyond what directly connects man with God and the unseen world, to all his human relations and earthly associations, to the policy of rulers, to nature and to art, then I say this is as true now as it ever can be. This dominion has been asserting itself and making itself felt for eighteen centuries. The Son of Man claimed it when He did not abhor the Virgin’s womb, and when He mingled in men’s ordinary transactions, blessing their food, their wine and their marriage feasts. The claim may have been denied at all times, and may be especially denied in the time we look forward to. But that time must assert it, not as something new but as something old: a government actually in exercise, whose easing even for a moment would have brought dreariness and death through the universe. If this is so, then the principle I have tried to defend in this book interferes with no sound or true understanding of our Lord’s second coming. It interferes only with a system that has kept men from acknowledging it. That system makes them think lightly of their present responsibilities, and gives them a fantastic habit of speaking of God’s providence as if it meant nothing now and only led to something hereafter. And it is very likely to suggest that when He has taken the power whose right it is, the Cross will no longer be the symbol of glory and victory.
