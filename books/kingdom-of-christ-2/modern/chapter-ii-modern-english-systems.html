Part III. The English Church and the Systems Which Divide it

Chapter II. Modern English Systems

Liberal, Evangelical, and Catholic parties each hold a truth that collapses into its opposite once made a system, so a national Church free of party spirit can better serve dissenters, the poor, and the colonies.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Section I.—The Liberal System: The Evangelical System: The High Church or Catholic System.

1. “See!” says the Liberal, who views English orthodoxy from the side where it stands against change and improvement. “See what a hopeless set these old pillars of the Church are! How can the Church stand on such maxims? Everything around us is moving, and can we choose to stand still? Opinions on every subject are being overturned, and we think our Articles and Formularies can stay as they are? Can’t you see that the Dissenters will keep growing and finally overwhelm us unless we find some scheme to include them? And what of the Romanists among us? Why treat them as if they belonged to the days of Gregory the Seventh or Innocent the Third? Why not make them friends by assuming they are friends? Why not throw away your prejudices and enter heartily into the spirit of the age?”

2. The Evangelical complains of the old school’s spirit in quite different language. “They have lost sight of all spiritual influences and realities; a dry notion of human merit lies under all their thoughts and teaching. They expect men to get to heaven by being baptized and leading good, respectable lives, and they forget the principle of faith altogether. The Gospel’s power as a message of peace to man is neither felt nor regarded. They set up a bond of union other than spiritual fellowship with Christ, so holy Dissenters are denounced while ungodly Churchmen are befriended. Restore the doctrines of our Articles; preach the Gospel in season and out of season. This is the only way to improve our condition and to remedy the harm the indifference of the last age has done.”

3. “Alas!” cry those members of the English Church who wish to be called Catholics, “miserable comforters are you all. Our English orthodoxy is indeed very bad, but you Liberals and Evangelicals will bring in something a thousand times worse. The error of those you attack is that they thought themselves members of a nation rather than of a Church. They followed the maxims of their own day instead of recalling those of better days. They looked to the State as guide and authority, when the State has an aim altogether different from ours. At certain happy moments, under godly princes, it may conform to our teaching, but habitually, and now above all, it is our jealous foe and aspires to be our tyrant. The Church may combine with a State, or rather submit to it, but has no natural connexion with it. She has divine sacraments, an apostolic order, and a power of binding and loosing. The practice and rules of the age of the Fathers are her model, and she must always be seeking to conform to them. She must refuse communion with the Dissenters here, not because they want the State’s privileges, but because they have cut themselves off from the universal Church, renouncing her orders and counterfeiting her sacraments. In the same way she must repudiate those Protestants abroad who have separated from their succession and abandoned it. She must aspire to union with the orthodox Greeks and Latins, but wait until we or they are ready for it. At home we must work to assert the worth of sacraments. We must introduce discipline, to preserve baptismal purity in our children and give repentance to those who have lost it, and to cut off those who hold schismatical or heretical notions under the garb of Churchmen. We must stir men to a more exact and religious life and encourage them to do good works and expect heavenly rewards for them. We must urge our disciples toward retirement from the world, penances and mortifications. We must preach repentance as the only way of recovering the privileges of Churchmen, which were given once but which most men lose through sin. We must discourage every exercise of private judgment except in choosing teachers. And we must advise our disciples to be content with probable conclusions, as all that faith requires, and leave certainties to men of science.”

Section II.—Reflections on these Systems, and on our Position generally.

These are the main outlines of the three systems that now present themselves to the young English theologian. The supporters of each tell him he must embrace one of them. All his attempts to blend them have failed. Inventing a scheme of his own seems enormous arrogance, and he feels he cannot fall back on the old State Churchmanship.

This fear of arrogance is one we should encourage in ourselves and in everyone else. With more humility we would probably have far fewer difficulties. So, if I had any chance of being heard, I would say: let us try to be humble. To stay humble, let us not hastily condemn any of these systems or those who put them forward. Our consciences have told us from time to time that each contains something we ought not to reject, and we should not reject it. We may find a divine harmony in which the living principle of each system is one note, while the systems themselves are a disturbance and violation of it. That seemed to be the case in our earlier inquiries into the Protestant bodies and the Catholic Church. Let us see whether our own national Church is an exception to the rule or an illustration of it.

1. Every true heart responds to the Liberal’s claim that a living Church cannot be tied to the system of one age. It must expand, breathe and move, throw off the results of partial experience, and profit by new experience. We sympathize when he says the Church is meant to comprehend and not exclude, and that neither Protestant nor Romish Dissenters should be beyond its sympathies or shut out from any of its benefits. But how would he realize this? He proposes that we give up the prayers we have received from past ages and the Articles that came down from the Reformation, or else adapt them to the maxims of our own time. What if those Prayers are the very means that kept us from bondage to particular habits of feeling when those threatened to hold us fast? What if those Articles kept us from sinking into one theological system and forced us to feel that truth has two sides, neither to be asserted to the exclusion of the other? What if giving up the Prayers or Articles, or reducing them to our present standards of thought, brought the Church into flat and hopeless monotony, leveled her to the superstitions of the nineteenth century and divorced her from past and future, so that expansion became forever impossible? And how would he achieve comprehension? He would strip away one point after another on which we and the Dissenters differ, until he found a few common principles on which all could agree. But what if the peculiar doctrines and practices of each class of Dissenters are exactly where their most living feelings are expressed? What if every plan of comprehension has failed because the best and most earnest men saw most clearly the importance of what was to be given up? If these suppositions are true, we must look somewhere other than a liberal system for what Liberals have dreamed of.

2. The words of the Evangelicals also come home to us with truth and power. Loss of faith was the great misery of the last age. Outward acts took the place of life-giving principles, and so became poor and dead. Whatever vital glow was restored to any part of the Church at the close of the last century came from the feeling that God had interfered for His creatures and was interfering still. There is a real relation between creature and Creator, and a real power coming from the Creator to help His creatures and enable them to do His will. These are mighty words, and the Evangelical is right that all men should hear them and act on their truth.

And how is this hope to be realized? Go and tell men that their baptism is not an admission to the privileges of God’s spiritual Church, and that they are not to take it as warrant for calling themselves members of Christ and praying to God as their Father in Him. Tell them they are in a nominal relation with God, not a real one, and that if they are ever to enter it they must bring themselves in by an act of faith, or wait until an angel comes down and troubles the waters. Tell them the Eucharist is not a real bond between Christ and His members but only a picture, which a violent act of will may turn into a reality. Make these comfortable declarations, mix them well with denunciations of other men for not preaching the Gospel, and you will have fulfilled God’s commission and reformed a corrupt and sinful land.

3. There is a charm in what the propounders of the Catholic system say. There is a Church in the world which God Himself established and did not leave to the faith, feelings and notions of men. He gave us permanent signs of its existence. He did not leave us to find our way in; He took us in Himself. Being in it, we are under His own guidance and discipline. We need not prove ourselves members by tests that exclude others who share our privileges. We need not form circles, parties and coteries. We belong to the Communion of Saints and need ask for no other. Amid the confusions of political parties, what good news to hear that we are slaves of none of them, that we can do without the State’s money or the State’s sword, and that we have powers the State neither gave nor can take away.

On practical matters, too, how true it seems that men ought to be called to repentance, good works, self-restraint and sacrifice to God. And how clear it seems that the Evangelicals, however much they wish to press these duties on their flocks, cannot actually do so. They cannot bid their congregations generally “Arise and go to your Father,” because they allow only some of them to call God their Father, and by their doctrine that portion has already repented and turned to God. They cannot call their congregations generally to holy acts from holy principles, because they do not believe most of them have received the Spirit from whom every holy desire and just work must come.

But how great is our confusion and dismay when we find that preaching repentance and good works is just as impossible on the Catholic system. One congregation is treated in practice as if it had never obtained its baptismal rights, the other as if it had lost them. Repentance and moral discipline are held out as possible means of recovering a lost treasure, not as the fruit of shame for the past and caution against abusing it in future. So exhortations to good works must take a selfish form and rest on selfish sanctions. After the splendid assurances that the Church really exists and has mighty powers, how grievous to find the strangest uncertainty about the terms of her existence. Is she only a splendid dream, recorded in the Fathers and perhaps to be realized some day? Or a potentiality that became fact in the Middle Ages through the Pope’s supremacy? Or, finally, an invisible equatorial line between Romanism and Protestantism, whose dim traces can occasionally be found in English history with powerful glasses, but which has gradually been lost in the darkness on one side of it? And for men who have left behind the intolerable pride and real slavery of recent notions about private judgment, it is painful to find that these Catholic denouncers of it in fact justify the most extravagant, self-conceited and unreasonable use ever made of it, and condemn it only when it has lost its evil character and is exercised under moral discipline and government. For what is more subversive of order and government, or more an outrage on fact, than the assertion that men in general are left to choose their teachers? And what is more subversive of the very idea of a teacher than the notion that he is not to cultivate his pupil’s mind and judgment but only pour his own notions into him? The very arrogance we wish to deliver men from is the notion that they need not receive the teachers, parents, judges and pastors God has set over them. The very hope we wish to encourage is that if they humbly receive the light given them, it will be increased more and more until they are brought into the perfect day.

Lastly, the dogma of probable evidence, which the Catholic school makes the foundation of its intellectual teaching as baptismal purity is of its moral teaching, seems to contain the very poison of the scepticism they denounce in the Liberal. The Liberal says: “Nothing is certain in morals; one opinion may be less mischievous or more plausible than another, but sensible men who know history have discarded the dream of what dogmatists call truth.” What says our English Catholic? “We admit nothing is certain in morals, but we do not want certainty. We are so faithful and submissive that we are content with appearances and likelihoods; we receive what the authority we have judged on the whole best tells us. God has not willed that we should have more light.” I appeal to the conscience of mankind against this language. Do we not mean, when we use the awful name of God, “The Being, He who is”? If there is no certainty, how dare we take that name on our lips? Are not the words “I believe in God the Father Almighty” an assertion that something fixed and eternal holds up the pillars of the universe? Do not the next words mean that He who Is has revealed Himself to us, that we are not to live on probabilities and plausibilities, and that He who is Truth wishes us to know the truth and be made free by it? So I say that this system, so far as it rests on the doctrine of probabilities, begins in scepticism and must end in it.

You will notice that I have not charged the authors of these systems with the tendencies they usually impute to one another. I have not said that the Liberal wishes to put Rationalism in place of Orthodoxy, that the Evangelical wishes to establish Dissent, or that the Catholic systematiser wishes to introduce Popery. My charge against each is that he defeats his own object. On how far these mutual accusations are true, I would often have to give a double answer to be understood. I can see that each party believes a clear and strong assertion of its own principle to be the best safeguard against the very evil it is accused of favouring, and I think that a true and reasonable opinion. The Liberal may rightly say, “Recognize the idea of Rationalism in the Church, and it will not assert itself outside the Church as Infidelity.” The Evangelical may say, “Recognize personal faith as the condition of Christian fellowship in the Church, and it will not assert itself outside as Dissent.” The Catholic may say, “Recognize the idea of Catholicism in your Church, and it will not assert itself outside as Romanism.” I acknowledge this, and can understand the disappointment and indignation each feels on finding his purpose misunderstood. Yet I must say that facts and reason both show that each principle, once worked into a system, becomes fairly open to the complaint of its fiercest critics. Church Liberalism reduced to a system is, I can only see, the denial that anything is given to men, whether as Tradition or Revelation. Church Evangelicism reduced to a system is the denial of the very idea of Church fellowship or Unity, replaced by a combination of individual units. Catholicism reduced to a system is Romanism, that is, the direct denial of the distinction of National Churches and the implicit denial of the Church as a spiritual body holding a spiritual Head. It seems to me a false way of speaking to say each system is good in moderation but bad when pushed to extremes. The system is not the extension of the principle but its limitation and contradiction. I do not see how the principle can be carried too far, or how anything can be done towards forming the system without introducing a seed of evil that must germinate until it bears all its natural fruits.

I have written very much in vain if I have not explained why I think this must be so. These systems, Protestant, Romish and English, each bear witness to a Divine Order and each is a miserable, partial, human substitute for it. In every country I would want to see men freed from the chains they have made for themselves and entering the freedom of God’s Church. But in England we have a clearer witness than anywhere of our right to this freedom and of how it may be gained. System-building is not natural to us. We have evils that are natural to us, against which we must always be on guard, but this is an exotic product. One charge the Liberal, Evangelical and Catholic systematisers all bring against our native English divines is that they understand little of systems and go on in a blind mechanical course, caring only to keep their places and do their work. Yet when not engaged in actual controversy, the members of all these parties keep showing that they feel this maxim of “keeping their places and doing their work” to be neither low nor grovelling. Their consciences testify for it, and they would conform to it if they could. As they grow older and holier, more disciplined by affliction and more aware of God’s will and of the ends they should seek, this old-fashioned notion, which struck them in youth as vulgar and earthly, is more and more acknowledged to be high in its origin and hard to realize. An old systematiser in England is a very rare sight. Either he gravitates to some lower region or ascends to a higher: he falls out of the middle air of speculation to our mother clods, or he sees clearly that heavenly things are substantial and that their images are to be found in the solid earth, not in the clouds. I would be presumptuous to speak to such men except with deference and humility, beseeching them not to make us what they have ceased to be themselves, and not to let us think from their words that they belong to schools and parties when we know that in their closets and in their lives they must be renouncing them all. These parties are recruited from the young. Young men want, as they say, principles and ideas. They cannot go on in the line of mere practical business and exertion; they must know why they act and what the end of action is, or they will not act at all. I am as sensible of this need as they are, and sensible how little their elders can sympathize with it or satisfy it. I can see further that unless we who are younger earnestly seek principles and grounds of action, we shall sink into the monotony of the last century or into something far worse. I believe the great principles each system has developed were made known to us for the wisest purposes. But they are the sap meant to invigorate and restore the oak trunk that has stood so many ages on our soil, and the seedlings they have sent forth themselves are poor, weak and twisted, unable to withstand any tempest. I do not urge the young English student to make light of these principles; he cannot safely make light of any of them. All belong to him and he needs them all. But I beseech him to consider solemnly, as in the presence of God, whether he may lawfully do any act implying that he adopts one of the systems in which these principles are buried, and whether he dares to fraternize with any parties, as parties, that profess them.

He will of course be told that to stand aloof is practically impossible, that to attempt it is self-conceit and self-will, that he is an Eclectic or a Syncretist, and that if he persists he will soon throw off his faith altogether. To the first charge he may reply that it cannot be impossible for an Englishman to be what an Englishman, not under some peculiar influence, is naturally inclined to be. To the second he will answer that he is not rejecting the instruction of his parents and teachers but trying to hold it fast. They may belong to a particular school. On beginning to think, his first impulse is to free himself from their notions, choose new teachers, and adopt the system most opposite to his education. Those who beg him not to join a party say instead: “By no means do this. The notions you have learnt must not be abandoned. There is a truth in them you must have; do not let them go till you have mastered it. When you have, do what you like with the system. You will love those who taught it you more than ever, and you will only refuse to let their teachings keep you apart from men you ought also to love.” The accusation of Eclecticism or Syncretism is better left unnoticed. Nine out of ten who use the words do not know what they mean; they are bugbears to frighten children. The tenth, who does know, will understand that a man who tries to put a Church in place of systems must regard with most dread and suspicion the attempt at a complete, all-comprehending system. Hating all systems, he hates the most perfect most, because they have the fewest crannies through which the light and air of heaven can enter. He hates the Romish system more than all Protestant ones, because they are inconsistent and fragmentary while it is all-embracing and satisfying, and therefore more lifeless, inhuman and godless. As for the fear of losing his faith once he has thrown down the party walls raised to defend it, he may venture the risk. If his faith is in the doctrines of men and not in the wisdom of God, the sooner it falls the better. If it is in Him whose name is Truth, let it be committed to His care. We believe His sentence has gone out against systems and parties, and we do not believe He has recalled the words, “None who trusteth in Me shall be confounded.”

I am sure our responsibility in this matter grows heavier every day. I have said these systems are not natural to us, but I do not mean that they cannot join with our most characteristic tendencies. Elsewhere the defenders of a system may merely form a school. In England, because we are by constitution politicians and not systematisers, they must form a party. The moment we adopt a peculiar theory we begin to organise, with flags and watchwords, chiefs and subordinates. Generous feelings of sympathy, courage, readiness to support a friend and unwillingness to desert him in an unpopular act bind us to maxims our leaders or allies have put forth, even when nothing in our own minds answers to them. We throw the feelings of men of action and soldiers into defending propositions worked out by dry school logic. So personality necessarily enters into all our solemnest discussions. It is a noble symptom of what we ought to be, and a miserable effect when we strive to be something else. The respectable champions on each side ask again and again why they should be treated with harshness and malignity for maintaining principles they believe in their hearts to be charitable and true. Immediately afterwards, their Newspapers and Reviews are seen generously striving that no other party shall be more unfair and libellous than their own. Worse and more grievous still, all, whether capable of understanding systems or not, are expected to enlist in one of these parties and bear its name. The poor must be taught in penny tracts to call one man a Papist and another a Low Churchman. Our children must become polemics before they can repeat their catechism. Women, whose place is to pacify and harmonise society, to witness against our cold logical habits and to teach us the worth of things above words, must talk about opinions and imitate our discords. They must pollute their minds, if not their lips, with the ribaldry we think it part of our Christian duty to indulge against those who bear the same name and share the same sacraments. Surely such a state of things must bring heavy judgments on our Church and land, and everyone should consider whether he will make himself an accessory to the sin, and whether he can do nothing to avert the punishment.

I know how much pains the defenders of party have taken to enlist the practical feelings of Englishmen. They say: “Let theorists talk as they will, the moment we begin to act we must associate with some men or others, and that association will take a party character. To bid us abandon parties and systems is only to bid us hang down our hands in stupid indolence. Those who wish to do anything must take things as they find them.” Yes, this is certainly the right test, and I rejoice that we are brought to it. I leave it to the defenders of systems and parties to explain what they are doing with them. They cannot complain that their machinery is not in active operation. It may occasionally meet some obstruction from a vague impression in men’s minds that they have been commanded to love their neighbours as themselves. Still, they cannot be so ungrateful as to deny that it has been brought to very tolerable perfection, and so to great efficiency, in our English towns and villages in this nineteenth century. An opponent’s description of its results could not be fair. So I will confine myself to a short statement of certain modes of action that I believe are open to someone who does not use this machinery, but is content with the powers he believes God has given him as a minister of His Kingdom.

1. Suppose such a Churchman finds himself among sects and parties: a Quaker here, a Baptist there, a Unitarian on his right, a Plymouth Christian on his left. He believes he belongs to a polity that recognizes the truth in each of these systems. But each has made a system out of some principle torn away from the rest, and has destroyed the principle by separating it. He believes that earnest men in these sects feel this. They catch at every scheme of union because they feel it, and they are angry with us for not sharing their sense of the need and not fraternizing with them. They are proclaiming the very principle on which the Catholic Church stands: that all unity is in Christ, and that intellectual notions and opinions should not divide men from Him. So the sect principle is in practice renounced as untenable, and yet there is a furious desire to maintain it against the national Church. He will see two reasons for this. First, the Church has presented itself merely as an English Church, and its character as a Catholic body, a kingdom set up for all nations, has been kept out of sight. Second, in reaction against this, it has taken a negative, that is, a sectarian form. The idea of the Church as a united body has been put forward chiefly to show the wickedness of those who separated from it, and its episcopacy and sacraments have been seen chiefly as shutting out those who lack them. Above all, the Church’s spiritual character, its life drawn from its head, which Dissenters by profession are especially disposed to recognize, has been cut off from the institutions that embody it. Men have been asked to accept the institutions as such and then hope for spiritual life through them. Little has been done to prove that the institutions are themselves living parts of the divine kingdom. So a person who holds these convictions will not despair of seeing all the true, hearty Dissenters gradually receive them. He will not be impatient to force his own notions on them. He will want to enter into their feelings, to destroy nothing they have received or learnt, to confirm their affection and reverence for their fathers, and to strengthen the hereditary affections which their doctrine of private judgment so much weakens. He wants to preserve all the faith they have from the destruction threatening it, to unite it with the faith of those they are separated from, and to make them integral members of the body from which they fancy we, in pride and selfishness, mean to exclude them. What comes of such a method is in God’s hands, not ours. Other methods have been tried and have failed; this one has not been tried.

2. Or suppose the Churchman finds himself in one of our awful manufacturing districts. The first thing to seize him, growing stronger every day, will be his own utter inadequacy to the mass of evil there. Yet he is there, and he knows that One cares for these multitudes infinitely more than he does. His own coldness will remind him that if he is to care for them at all, the feeling must be given him by Him who often seems to these unhappy people heedless of their sorrows. And he has the consolation the Athenian orator found when he thought of his countrymen’s reverses and Philip’s advance: if we had done the right things and failed, we might despair; we have not done them, so let us hope. A Church that was regarded, and almost regarded itself, as a tool of the aristocracy, that compared its own orders with ranks in civil society and forgot it existed to testify that man as man is the object of his Creator’s sympathy, had no voice that could reach these multitudes. The Liberal proclamation, “Teach them; give them a few of the things we know,” was more genial and humane. But these Englishmen have thoughts, quickened by hunger and suffering, which such instruction could not appease. Far more impressive was the Methodist and Evangelical speech: “You have immortal souls, they are perishing; ask how they may be saved.” Spoken with true earnestness, such words are very mighty. But they are not enough. Men feel they are not merely lost creatures. They look up and ask whether this can be the whole account of their condition, and whether their sense of right and wrong, their craving for fellowship, and their consciousness of powers no other creatures have are all nothing. If religion cannot explain these feelings, they say, and can only tell of a fall for the whole race and an escape for a few individuals, then our wants must be met without religion. Then begin Chartism and Socialism and whatever schemes make rich men tremble. What modern champions of a Church system say about active charity to these sufferers, about showing that we regard them not as pensioners on the nation’s bounty but as fellow-men for whom we make sacrifices, is far more to the purpose, and must produce happy effects if acted on even imperfectly. But how could it help to tell Chartists and Socialists that they once had baptismal purity and have lost it; that they must recover ground by repentance, prayer and fasting; that they must submit to discipline and be deprived of privileges they never exercised or cared for? How could that meet or set right the confused, disorderly notions stirring in their minds?

On the other hand, suppose a new and unwonted proclamation went forth: “God has cared for you; you are indeed His children; His Son has redeemed you, His Spirit is striving with you; there is a fellowship larger, more indifferent to outward distinctions, more democratical than any you can create, a fellowship of mutual love, not mutual selfishness, in which the chief of all is the servant of all.” May we not think the result would be as great as that of any Franciscan friar’s preaching in the twelfth century or any Methodist preacher’s in the eighteenth? These are true, everlasting words, and yet they belong especially to our time. They interpret, and must be interpreted by, the regular charity, ministerial holiness, sacraments, prayers and discipline of which the Catholic speaks. They connect his words with the Evangelical’s about repentance, making it plain that only a cursed nature and a depraved will could have robbed anyone of the blessings God gave to all. They turn all the Liberal plans for educating adults and children into meaning and life. And they fulfil the statesmen’s notion that the Church is to support the existing orders, by making her a teacher and example to those orders of their duties and responsibilities, and by removing the hatred which their forgetfulness of those duties is threatening to create among the lower classes.

3. A Churchman like this would be compelled by his circumstances and urged by his principles to turn these convictions into action, enlisting the wealthier people of his parish in services and occupations for the benefit of their inferiors. I am unwilling to say more, first because my practical ignorance makes me unfit to suggest anything, and second because I am sure English political wisdom, guided by Catholic feeling, is already doing much of this in many parts of the land. But I must mention it to remark how the notion that party organisation is necessary is at once explained and refuted when we aim at an ecclesiastical organisation. It is explained, when the truth implied in that strange maxim, that no man is meant to work alone, becomes the principle of our action. It is refuted, because a society held together by sacramental bonds and moving under an appointed pastor has far less friction than any society built on a party model or a party motive ever has or can have. The one seeks to preserve all existing ranks and relations; the other sets them all aside. The one is always trying to understand how the middle classes can act on the lower so as to be their guides and not their tyrants; how the upper classes can act on the middle so as not to be their fawning slaves and betrayers of their consciences at elections, yet cold, distant objects of servile imitation at other times; and how each part of the community can keep its proper place and be fused together by the spiritual power that exists for each, the minister of all, the creature of none. The other confounds all orders without lessening their mutual repulsion or making them feel a common object. Above all, the Churchman longs to find how the Church’s handmaidens can do her the services only they can do without leaving their own true estate as wives, sisters and mothers, so that the whole sex may be an order of Sisters of Charity, working in each neighbourhood in lowliness and meekness to soften and heal the world’s sorrows. The partisan acknowledges no difference of vocation between man and woman; all are to be equally feverish and restless, careful about many things, and unfit alike for quiet contemplation or regular activity.

4. Suppose again our Churchman in Ireland, among a population mostly acknowledging no relation to the body he belongs to. How would he feel and want to act? Would he not think this? When Anselm came from his Norman convent to be Archbishop of Canterbury, his victorious countrymen expected him to treat the old Saxons as they did. He told them plainly that a Churchman acknowledged no distinctions of race and that his vocation was to be the friend of the poor and distressed wherever he found them. These principles, with great exceptions and deviations, were acted on by a large part of the Norman bishops and clergy. The effect was that we grew into an English nation. The Saxon serf felt he had a share and a right in the soil; he remembered the sounds of his native language and began to speak it; in time conqueror and conquered became one. If our Churchmen in Ireland had acted on this principle, and had told the English settlers, “We will have nothing to do with your Orange lodges and hell-fire clubs except to discipline and restrain their members; we have come as protectors of these Celts, to raise them out of barbarism, to speak to their Church feelings and national feelings and call forth both together” — how many problems that perplex the statesman today might have been solved long ago! But the phrase The English interest was always in the minds of the statesmen who sent out our Bishops. They might stumble on a noble rebel to their commands, but they diligently sought men who would forward their own narrow policy. The consequence is that Irish national feeling has strangely and unnaturally attached itself to Romanism, the foe of all national feeling. The Irish see our Church as a Saxon Church, and they actually fly to Rome to give them an Irish one. Even now, at the eleventh hour, if truer feelings about our position are rising among statesmen, may not the Church be the means of carrying them out? We have tried the mere preaching of Protestantism in Ireland; so far as it was earnest and sincere it has not been in vain, yet it has not touched Irish hearts, and it has met resistance not only from their bad feelings but from their good. State liberality has been tried; so far as it was a token of kindness and sympathy, perhaps this too was not in vain, but all must admit it has done very little, and most think a fair share of evil was mixed with the good. But if the Catholic and National principles sympathize, and cannot really exist apart, why may we not begin to speak to the national sympathies of Irishmen, as members of an Irish Catholic Church; declare that every Irishman ought to see himself as a member of such a Church and not of any other, Saxon or Romish; and show by acts that we hold our revenues for the good of the whole land, which would gain only misery by their confiscation or by the extirpation of those who hold them? Such thoughts, I say, are likely to arise in an Irish Churchman who enters into the principles I have tried to develop. They may be crude, but they may be the germs of acts which neither State nor Church will have cause to regret.

5. To one who feels the importance of the Protestant principle and sees that its true home is the Catholic Church, it must seem a strange providence that England has on one side a nation where Protestantism has tried to exist nakedly and exclusively, and on the other a nation that wishes to be Catholic by being Romanist. Each experiment is, I think, very decisive, but each is tied to sins we need to confess and deplore. Scotland has surely proved that Presbyterianism cannot support a national life. But we began by setting up our episcopacy as if it were an English thing. We gave the Scots the notion that their own kings were coming back to reduce them to an ecclesiastical province of England, and the religious as well as the national spirit rose against that pretension. Now the episcopalians in Scotland seem to have the opportunity to show they are neither a religious sect nor tools of England. They have existed for many years without State patronage. Their chief fault has been that they have not sympathized with the people’s feelings, have stood too much on ecclesiastical dignity, and have seemed too much mere anti-presbyterians. But if in the present crisis they will consider earnestly that they exist as witnesses not of a system but of a Church, not of notions about episcopacy but of episcopacy as part of the constitution of Christ’s spiritual kingdom, I think they may have a quiet, soothing influence on the ferment of Scottish feeling which all State contrivances have failed to allay. I hope they will not look with proud aristocratic contempt on the people’s earnest cry to be freed from civil dominion, nor indulge in mocking allusions to Presbyterianism’s proud claim to have been free of it. They will acknowledge that spiritual freedom is essential to a nation’s life. They will labour to show that the Church, rightly constituted, can humble the lofty and exalt the lowly; that the tyranny Presbyterianism granted its aristocracy at the Reformation is the tyranny its sons groan under now; and that its boast of being a Church for the poor has ended in a sadder separation of poor and rich than has almost ever existed in any country. Here again I am proposing no projects to Scottish Churchmen. I am urging them to consider seriously the indications of God’s will, and to desire to act in accordance with it.

6. The lessons from our connexion with Scotland and Ireland (I have spoken before of those from our old colonies in North America) cannot be lost on us when we plant new settlements on the other side of the globe, or decide how to deal with those we already have. Every circumstance seems to say: do not merely establish an English kingdom in those soils, or it will be a blessing to neither colonists, natives nor the mother country. Do not merely send preachers in your ships to tell the people that all they have believed, if they believed anything, is false, and that we hold a doctrine that sets it all aside. Raise up among them what they will feel to be as real a kingdom as the one they see in governors and judges: a kingdom that not only deals equal justice to natives and settlers, but claims both as citizens and gives both its highest privileges; a kingdom that comes to subvert nothing but to restore what is decayed and fallen; to adopt every fragment of existing faith and feeling, purify and exalt it, and cut off only what the native’s own conscience admits to be inconsistent with it; and to testify that wherever there is a creature with human limbs and features, there is one of the race for which Christ died, one whom He is not ashamed to call a brother.

In countries like New Zealand and Australia, the testimony of a Christian kingdom of peace and righteousness is everything, for there only the rudest and most incoherent spiritual theories will be found. In India the case is altogether different, yet there more than anywhere the signs of a spiritual kingdom are needed, so that Christianity may be seen as more than a fine theory. For how did the simple tenets of Mahomedanism prevail over the complicated creeds and philosophies of the Hindoos, except that the former came as an organic society and the latter were only forms of thought connected mainly with the physical universe? The Christian Church ought to understand the position of both Hindoo and Mussulman: the strange mass of feelings and opinions in the one’s traditions, and the struggle after consistency and unity visible in the other’s actual history. I wish the supporters of Indian missions had taken this ground when the cowardice and indifference of the merchant-emperors assailed them thirty years ago. I wish they had answered those who accused them of disturbing the natives’ faith and endangering English dominion: “No; it is your godlessness and rapacity that endanger their faith. You are making them infidels while you pretend to indulge their superstitions. We go to save their faith by delivering them from their superstitions and from your example; we go that England may not perish on the day she must account for your crimes.” But at that time it was not fully understood that Christianity was anything but a sect, or a collection of sects, sent to displace Pagan and Mahomedan sects. So the years since have had their natural effect, and we now mostly face a generation of open or disguised infidels. Still, the good men of that day, guided by a wisdom higher than their own, strenuously asked the English legislature to send out a Bishop. They felt they needed a Church. A heart was put into a country hitherto directed only by wise heads and skilful hands. It is still beating, and we trust it may yet send life-blood into every part of that vast empire. The issue is with God, but He has shown us clearly enough how He wills us to do our part.

7. I have not yet spoken of the spirit in which we should act toward members of foreign Churches, Romish or Protestant. But enough has been said earlier in this work to show the course an Englishman not tied to systems must try to follow. What I chiefly wanted to show is that here we have the means of acting on principles all men everywhere ought to act on if they could, and that herein lies the blessing for which we must give thanks. Our Church has no right to call herself better than other Churches in any respect, and in many she must admit she is worse. But our position, as a confession and not a boast, is one of singular advantage. If what I have said is true, our faith is not formed by uniting the Protestant systems with the Romish system, or by taking elements from each. So far as it is represented in our liturgy and articles, it is the faith of a Church and has nothing to do with any system. This peculiar character God has given us enables us, if we do not slight the mercy, to understand the difference between a Church and a System better perhaps than our neighbours can, and so, rightly used, our position lets us help them realise the blessings of their own. By refusing to unite with them on the ground of any one of their systems, and seeking to unite on the ground of the universal Church, we teach them where their strength and weakness lie. By determining to be a nation distinct from all others, we encourage each of them to be a nation distinct from us and from all others. By showing how our Church life and national life are interwoven, we teach them that the bonds which make them one with us are needed to support the peculiar character that makes them independent of us.

But for such tasks as these — reconciling the sects in our own land, dealing with the wild feelings about government and society abroad in it, bringing the classes into co-operation, entering into the strong passions of Scotch Calvinists and Irish Romanists, taming the savages of the antipodes, restoring the strange relics of ancient civilisation among the natives of British India, giving any practical hint or help to our brethren on the Continent — what need we have of a different discipline and spirit from what we seem to possess! Shall we get either by sitting still, by saying these tasks are too great for creatures so infirm and fallen, by waiting for some sudden inspiration? That cannot be. These works are set before us; one way or another we are trying to carry them on, and must. The necessity is laid on us; the only question is how to bear it. Do we tremble at the great efforts of thought these undertakings presuppose: careful study of ecclesiastical and civil history, acquaintance with the powers and distinctions of words as signs of thought, intimacy with the symbols nature and art have given the mythologist, and the patient toil of weighing all this in our minds before we can enter into other men’s feelings without losing our own? This is certainly required of us, not the whole of it from each student, for the Church is one body with many members, but something from every one, and the habit and disposition from all. Yet nothing here should daunt the countrymen of Bacon and Newton. Study is painful to Englishmen if they cannot connect it with action; they cannot pursue it for pleasure or beauty. Make them feel it is necessary for their business and they will be as diligent slaves to books as to road-making. Our systems and parties have confused us in every direction. They lead us to fancy that all things move round in a weary circle or are imprisoned in lifeless notions. At the same time they tempt every man to think he must be everything, know everything and do everything, since he feels that if he lacks the whole of his system, each part becomes mischievous and false. He cannot trust others to do their work while he does his, because he belongs to a party rather than a Church and so has no security that each person has his orders and duties assigned. Thus we are at once indolent and over-diligent, ignorant and encyclopædic. Break this spell, and we shall again connect our specific studies with a general humanity, and so preserve their limits and make them universal.

But there is another and more serious subject. I have spoken of a different discipline, but we need a different spirit to make it possible. Not a different spirit from the one we received in baptism, but one altogether different from the spirit of party and selfishness which we have let enter and possess us in manhood. Our first object should be to exorcise this, so that the other may really inform and rule us. And we cannot drive it out of others until we have striven to banish it from ourselves. If we who form the clergy believe we are the heart of the land, we must suppose the purification of the whole body depends on ours. We must feel that every evil we call others to repent of has its root in us, and that we must repent of it first. I fear that the habit of apologising for our institution when ignorantly attacked by those who know nothing of its meaning or blessing may have harmed our lives. We have defended the arrangements of Providence and the order of the Church until, unawares, we began to defend ourselves, who have so grievously sinned against them and hindered men from seeing what they are. If we have done this, we must wish to make amends. There can be no national confession or reformation if we, who should be foremost in both because we have most to answer for, are making excuses for ourselves, hiding the evil we are inwardly conscious of, or blaming circumstances, most of which favour us and none of which ought to be our masters.

But if shame and humiliation are needed by English clergymen generally, they are especially needed by those who have presumed to speak of our sins and suggest how to amend them. It is too probable that they would have known nothing of the evil of systems and parties in others if they had not felt it in themselves. The irritation of the beam in their own eye has made them more eager to spot the mote in their brother’s. In this book I have attacked no wrong tendency that I do not know myself liable to. I hope I am aware, though very insufficiently, of the danger of substituting denunciation of it for practical correction in the only sphere I control. I also know that the hints I have offered against systems may be turned, by me or by others, into a system, and that neither its weakness and inconsistency nor its originator’s insignificance may keep it from attaching itself to some new party. I believe that some of those I have spoken of in this chapter began their mission with as sincere a desire as I feel now that their words might never become the symbols of a faction. So I do not trust myself. But a school formed to oppose all schools must be more mischievous than any of them, and a school pretending to amalgamate the doctrines of all others would, I think, be more mischievous still. I therefore pray earnestly that if any such schools should arise they may come to nothing, and that if anything I have written in this book should in the least favour their establishment, it may come to nothing. On the other hand, if anything here may help raise men above their own narrow conceptions and mine, and lead them to believe there is a way to truth that is living and universal and above us all, and that He who is Truth will guide them in it, then I pray that He will bless this, which is from Him and not from me. “Let all thine enemies perish, O Lord”: all systems, schools and parties that have hindered men from seeing the largeness, freedom and glory of Thy kingdom; “but let them that love thee,” in whatever earthly mists they are now involved, “be as the sun when he goeth forth in his strength.”
