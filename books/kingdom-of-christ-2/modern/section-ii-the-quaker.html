Section II.—The Quaker

The Sermon on the Mount fulfils rather than abolishes judicial oaths and just retribution, answering the Quaker’s case against war, oaths, and a paid clergy.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Sermon on the Mount—Different passages in it considered—Provision for Ministers.

The Quaker objects to this whole statement. Formally, he objects to the part about war, oaths, punishment, and a national provision for the spiritual body. Practically, he objects to all the principles on which these institutions have been defended. He says: “Our arguments may sound plausible to worldly men. But those who take the Gospel simply, and try to shape themselves by its precepts, find them directly contradicted by the Sermon on the Mount. The highest of all authorities has said ‘Swear not at all,’ ‘resist not evil,’ ‘love your enemies.’ We choose to call the practices of statesmen who set these precepts aside reasonable and religious. He has plainly annulled the old maxims on these points, and it pleases us to say they still bind. But we cannot make bitter sweet or evil good by calling them so. Men will be judged by Christ’s commands, not by our glosses on them. Our theory, however, is consistent with our own practice, if not with our Master’s words. We want worldly men to take us into their houses. So when they owe their Lord a hundred measures of wheat, it suits us to tell them to write fourscore. If we become pensioners on the nation’s bounty, we must make the best of the nation’s sins. The Apostles followed a different rule. They lived on the love of their flocks and took what was cheerfully given by those they served. We have become hirelings instead of shepherds, and we boast of the change and think ill of those who will not accept it.”

I. The first class of these charges turns mainly on the fifth chapter of St. Matthew, so I will examine that passage at once. We are accused of violating its spirit, perverting its letter, and lowering its standard of duty. My case will not be established unless I bring each of these charges home to the Quaker himself.

1. The opening sentences of the Sermon on the Mount must in some measure be the key to its whole purpose. The blessings pronounced on certain states of mind make us feel that One has come to establish a kingdom in the inner man. He deals with the principles of things, lays the axe to the roots, baptizes with the Spirit and with fire, and reforms the fruit by reforming the tree. The idea of a parallel between the mountain where the trumpet sounded and the one where Jesus opened His mouth may be only a notion or superstition. Even so, the principle implied in it is exactly true.

2. “Ye are the salt of the earth, ye are the light of the world” exhorts us to see that the salt keeps the quality that hinders corruption in other things, and does not take on their tendency to decay. It exhorts us to see that the light is not quenched by the inner darkness it is meant to penetrate. These exhortations agree exactly with what came before. They show the nature of the authority with which the new Teacher spoke, which distinguished Him from the letter-hunting Scribe. I also notice that the effects promised to these states of mind are of the same kind as the states themselves, and are distinctly spiritual. The poor in spirit will understand what it is to be brought into a kingdom. The mourner will be comforted. The meek will govern the earth, that is, will have the greatest joy he can know, making other men happy. The merciful will obtain mercy. Those who hunger after righteousness will obtain it. The peace-makers will be called children of the great Peace-maker. The pure in heart will see God. He who keeps the salt in himself from corruption will preserve the earth from decay. He who keeps the light alive within him will cause men to glorify their Father in heaven. I make this point because I want to show how carefully our Lord keeps the characteristics and rewards of His kingdom free of all secular mixture. He carries men into a region entirely unlike the one they usually live in, and yet it is their own native region, the region of their true being.

3. But how is this distinctness kept? Is it by denying that a lower, outward region exists? Is it by setting that region aside as evil and impure in itself? Is it by absorbing every influence into the one transcendent influence? Or is it by the opposite course: recognizing the reality of the outward world, showing how God’s economy provides for it, and showing its relation to the invisible, heavenly atmosphere that informs and surrounds it? Our Lord’s next words answer: “Think not that I am come to destroy the law and the prophets; I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. Verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in nowise pass from the law till all be fulfilled.” Every word here matters, so I remind the reader that “fulfil” is not the same word in the two verses. The last clause of the eighteenth verse would be better translated “until all things have been done, or have come to pass.” I suppose our inconvenient version has given rise to the notion that our Lord speaks of His own personal obedience to the law as practically abrogating it. On that reading He would be saying, “I am come to fulfil the law, and when I have, it may pass away, but not before.” That reading destroys the connexion of the passage with what precedes and follows. The sentence “Whosoever, therefore, shall destroy one of the least of these commandments, and teach men so, shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven” could not have been added if the law were spoken of as destroyed by Christ’s work. For the kingdom of heaven is the kingdom that His work was to establish. So our Lord must use “fulfil” in its strict, ordinary sense. He means that He has come to give the outward law its kernel and substance, filling up the husk. He must also mean that this kernel will not destroy the husk. The husk will remain in all its dryness and literalness, with no jot or tittle of its enactments abolished and none of its authority diminished. This holds until all things are done and have come to their perfect condition, until formal law has lost its application to the universe because its meaning and spirit are accomplished in every human creature. Until heaven and earth pass away, as long as there is any evil to prevent and any flesh in any man not subject to God’s will, law in its outward character must exist. And he is least in the kingdom of heaven, with the least spiritual insight, who tries to cut down its precepts or its terrors.

4. So far, everything in the sermon seems to deny that Christ came to repeal one set of rules and establish another. Everything seems to show that He came to confirm rules that already existed, to show the inward righteousness that grounds them, and to lead His willing disciples into possessing it. The next words strengthen this: “Except your righteousness exceed the righteousness of the Scribes and Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter into the kingdom of heaven.” There are only two ways to read this. The righteousness required may differ from that of the Pharisees in degree, or in kind. If in degree, it follows that our Lord sets aside the Mosaic decrees and puts stricter, harder ones in their place. There is a feeling behind this reading that should not be despised. It is so common to believe that Christianity is a system of mitigations and allowances, valued mainly for freeing us from troublesome limits on self-indulgence, that honest men are naturally startled into a violent reaction. They are eager to press all Scripture into proof that the perfect dispensation asks more and is severer than the imperfect. But after giving full honour to the truth in this view, we must say its fruits have been most pernicious. It has turned the Christian race into a selfish contest over who can gain most of the rewards of a future state, and has blinded men to the nature of those rewards. It has destroyed all high, pure, disinterested morality. It has depraved the principles of those who seemed to aim highest. It has also done infinite injury to the practice of those content with less, by making them think there is no universal standard for all, only a peculiar one each may choose. It has bred useless superstition and ecclesiastical oppression on one side, and utterly lax and reckless habits on the other. In short, it has contradicted the whole scope of the Sermon on the Mount more than any other opinion in the Christian world. So we are driven to the other reading, which all good commentators hold. The righteousness that exceeds that of the Scribes and Pharisees is spiritual and not literal. It is the conformity of life and character to the original mould after which all outward laws are fashioned, the pattern on the Mount, and not the mere conformity of conduct to precepts. The Pharisees notoriously held the idea of supererogation, and their sect was founded on professing a righteousness higher than the multitude’s. So we might expect our Lord to show how this superlative morality differs from His own. We might expect Him to show that the one set the law aside in trying to refine it, while the other sustained the law and filled it with new life. We might expect Him to show that every attempt to adapt the law in order to exalt the inner life was hopeless, because the law existed for another purpose. The principle it pointed to was something quite different from the outward command and could not be extracted from it by twisting its formulas. But after so many cautions and so careful a statement of His object, we should certainly not expect Him to annul any precept He was not to destroy but to fulfil. Nor should we expect Him to introduce new legal dogmas in their place, when He came to bring a righteousness above all dogmas.

5. Let us see whether He disappoints this expectation, or whether every following passage clearly and beautifully illustrates the preface. “Ye have heard that it has been said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill, and whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment; but I say unto you, that whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment; and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council; and whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire.” Suppose “It has been said of old time, but I say” meant here “I am about to tell you something that annuls what was said of old.” Then “Raca” and “Thou fool” would have to be taken in the same formal, legal sense as “Thou shalt not kill.” Words meant to supersede other words must be construed as those words are. If “Thou shalt not kill” points to a definite, specific act, then “Thou shalt not give this name to thy brother” must point to one too. Who does not see what shameful limiting of these sublime precepts, what low superstition and vile hypocrisy, would result? Men have actually fallen into these evils trying to work out how much slander in their own language matched the Greek μωρέ and the Syriac Raca, and so how near they stood to the judgment, the council, and hell fire. Such doubts have only now and then taken this monstrous form. But they have clearly haunted sin-darkened minds, and would have haunted them more if teachers had not said: “These words are addressed to your inward ear, not your outward. ‘Raca’ and ‘Thou fool’ are only signs of certain states of mind. Causeless anger is the beginning of the disease. It has become chronic when it breaks out in words of fury, and radical, infecting the vitals, when it breaks out in words of settled scorn. The first state subjects you to a judgment: you experience separation from God and man, you cannot feel with the congregation, you cannot pray to your Father. This may pass if ‘thou agree with thine adversary quickly whilst thou art in the way with him.’ That means settling the accounts between you and your conscience, your adversary, which you know cannot be done until the outward account with your brother is settled too. But take care it does not harden into the second state, which subjects you to the council, a more complete alienation from heavenly feelings, peaceful hopes, and communion with God. Even this God’s discipline may cure. But there comes a time when all discipline has been tried in vain, and the sentence on the soul is ‘Let it alone.’ Then it must be left to raging, consuming fires that the love of God could not quench. The only fitting emblem of them is the fires outside Jerusalem, burning up its rubbish and offal.”

This is the meaning, so spiritual and so awful, that we may put on our Lord’s words. We feel that it belongs to us in our highest, most responsible, and most perilous condition, as immortal beings taken into covenant with God, brought into fellowship with our brethren, and placed under the government and education of His Holy Spirit. We must give this meaning up for some barren, hungry interpretation, fretting to the conscience and profitless to the heart, the moment we forget that He came not to destroy the law but to fulfil it. We give it up the moment we imagine His work was to substitute one formal precept for another, and not to stanch the fountain of evil in the heart, from which came all the crimes the outward law was the true and permanent witness against.

6. The next two passages concern the marriage bond: the law’s prohibition of adultery, and its toleration of divorce. In the first the meaning is evident. Our Lord comes not to destroy but to fulfil. He leaves the precept against adultery as He found it and gives it new authority. Still it belongs to the “old time,” and He has a message for the inner man. He aims not at the crime but at the sin: “Look not upon a woman to lust after her”; “If thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out”; “If thy right hand offend thee, cut it off.” Be willing to give up the use of powers God meant you to use, and the pleasures of sense God has given, if you find they feed your inward corruption and keep you from the higher joys of the spiritual kingdom. It is better to enter into life halt and maimed, with the spirit itself pure and free at the cost of some of its tools. The alternative is to have two hands and two feet, with all your powers and senses at liberty, and be cast into hell-fire, consumed by tyrant lusts and ever-renewing remorse. This is the very principle and illustration of inward spiritual discipline.

The next clause is at first more puzzling. Our Lord seems to repeal the Mosaic law of divorce, though He expressly disclaimed repealing any jot of the law. But on a moment’s thought there is no inconsistency. The law of Moses was not meant to encourage divorce but to put obstacles in its way. The Jew could not send away his wife whenever he took a dislike to her. He had to resort to a legal instrument, a bill of divorcement. That much the law could do to witness for the sanctity of marriage. It could do no more, because of the hardness of heart of those it dealt with. Prohibitions and penalties could not by themselves preserve the primal law of creation, established when God made them male and female. To accomplish what they aim at, the new dispensation shows marriage as a lower form and image of its greatest mystery, giving it a sacred dignity that is only just short of sacramental.

7. Now we come to one of the points in dispute between us and the Quaker. “It has been said by them of old time, Ye shall not forswear yourselves, but ye shall perform unto the Lord your oaths; but I say unto you, Swear not at all, neither by heaven, for it is God’s throne, neither by the earth, for it is His footstool, neither by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the Great King, nor by your head, for you cannot make one hair of it white or black.”

I do not say the Quaker may not be able to prove that this passage forbids all oaths, judicial and religious as well as vituperative and conversational. But I do say this is not the view that would occur to a literal interpreter who reads the whole sermon and interprets one part by another. A literal interpreter could not ignore the repeated formula “It hath been said by them of old time, but I say unto you.” Without the strongest reason he could not give it an opposite meaning here from the one it plainly has in three other places. He could not overlook that none of the oaths our Lord cites is a judicial oath. Every one is the kind that, by analogy with other nations, we would expect in familiar talk. He could not overlook that the “communication” (λόγος) which is to be yea, yea, nay, nay, cannot without very strange use of language mean a formal, legal procedure. So I am not assuming the Quakers cannot overcome all these à priori arguments. But I maintain they have not the shadow of a plea for presenting themselves as strict interpreters and denouncing our laxness. They must show why our Lord in this one case broke the method and coherence of His discourse. They must show why He brought in this old law, apparently to illustrate that He has not come to destroy but to fulfil, when it is a direct exception to that principle. They must show why He tied it by a common phrase to two other cases that strongly enforce and explain the principle.

The only defence they make, so far as I know, against these presumptions runs like this: “‘Swear not at all’ are clear and obvious words, spoken for poor and ignorant people by One who came to preach the Gospel to the poor. Whatever commentators say, an ordinary man finding such a command in the Bible would suppose it governs all cases, not a few. There is no moral line between an oath in private and an oath in a court of justice. And a ban on private, conversational swearing would be superfluous, since no one ever thought it lawful.” On the first argument I will not stop to ask how it comes from those who have prided themselves on finding meanings in Scripture that the ordinary careless reader misses but the spiritual man sees. I will not ask whether they are the people to complain that we do not take the most superficial view. I take the difficulty head on. Take an ordinary English peasant with just enough intelligence and religious feeling to attach any meaning to the passage. He would be far less likely to think “Swear not at all” forbids judicial oaths than to think our Lord wants him to injure his members when He says “If thy right hand offend thee, cut it off.” That is because in the one case he has a practical test of the meaning and in the other he does not. The peasant knows perfectly well that the feeling with which he enters a solemn court, among men in official dress, and calls God to witness his words are true, is as different as any two feelings in one person from the feeling with which he uses the same holy name to back a chance word over his cups in the tap-room. He would never compare the two acts, except in this way: he has seen that those most given to oaths and to trifling with God’s name in common talk are the ones on whom a judicial oath has least effect and who are most likely to forswear themselves. So if the claim is that the plain wayfaring man will be particularly likely to find perplexity or contradiction here, I believe it is a mistake. I do not say he may not be perplexed, since I do not say he may not be perplexed by any passage of Scripture. I have already argued that a personal ministry is as necessary to him, and as much appointed for him, as the Written Word, and that one is not generally meant to profit him without the other. All I need maintain is that my view, once set before him, will seem not more difficult or more contrary to his expectations but more intelligible, and will better interpret his own experience. For example, to a congregation of English countrymen I would speak, in full confidence that it would approve itself to their hearts and understandings, words something like these:

“My friends, the assizes are to be held tomorrow in the county town, three miles from our village. Several of you are summoned as witnesses. So that you may understand what to do and in what spirit, I shall explain this morning a passage from our Lord’s Sermon on the Mount. I have often told you that the Bible is not a show-book about things that have nothing to do with you. It concerns all your common business and tells you how to go about it rightly. The passage begins: ‘It hath been said by them of old time, Thou shalt not forswear thyself, but thou shalt perform unto the Lord thine oaths.’ First, you would like to know who said this in old time. Look back a few verses in this chapter. There it is written, ‘It has been said of old time, Thou shalt not kill,’ and a little lower, ‘Thou shalt not commit adultery.’ These commands were given of old to the Jews, and you know who gave them. I was reading them to you just now from the altar, and before them all I read, ‘God spake these words and said.’ God declared Himself the King of the Jews, and told them amid thunders and lightnings that these were His laws, that breaking them was disobeying Him, and that misery and destruction would follow. You know the history of the Jews. They tried whether this was true, and found that it was. Misery did follow, until at last they were sent away into a strange land. Now as God commanded the Jews not to kill and not to commit adultery, so He commanded them not to forswear themselves but to perform their oaths to Him. Our Lord puts the same honour on all these commands. He calls each ‘what was spoken in old time,’ that is, what God spoke of old to His people and what they kept as His commands. I tell you, my friends, that if the Jews had forgotten this command they would have been as badly off as if they had forgotten either of the others. They were held together as a people by having God’s name put upon them. So when they came together for any solemn dealings with each other, they were bound to remember they were in God’s presence, that He watched their words and intentions, and that He would avenge Himself on them if they dealt dishonestly. An oath in a court of justice says this: ‘We are in God’s presence, and we know He is a witness whether we speak truth or lies. He is a God of truth, and will punish us if we lie.’ If the Jews had forgotten to perform their oaths to the Lord, or if the rulers had not enforced oaths, they would have been guilty of a great sin. They would have refused to bear witness for God as He told them to. It would have gone as ill with them, and the nation would as surely have fallen to pieces, because there would have been no fear of God and no sense that He was near, as if they had committed murder or adultery. Indeed they would have committed murder and adultery, because the thought of God, which keeps men from these courses, would have left them. Now our nation of England acknowledges God as its King just as the Jewish nation did. In our prayer for Queen Victoria we call Him ‘King of kings and Lord of lords.’ As He was King over King David, so He is King over our Queen. Our laws that say ‘Thou shalt not kill, thou shalt not commit adultery’ are God’s laws, not the Queen’s. She did not make them, nor did any king or queen before her. She and her servants carry them out, and are answerable to God for how they do it. They were made of old; God made them and God enforces them. We are to do all our business as in His presence, knowing He is the witness of what we do. Our rulers are bound to tell us this, and on all solemn occasions, when we meet as in a court, to urge us to say boldly that we know the eye of the unseen God is on what we do, say, and think. Remember this when the book is put into your hands to swear. You are declaring in that court that God’s eye is upon you, that you believe He will teach you to speak truth, and that you believe He will punish you if you lie. But I have more to say. I have often told you that all the laws in the world will never make us good men. They are ‘a terror to evil-doers, and a praise and protection to them that do well.’ But they will never put one right thought into your heart or make it pure and holy. The laws cannot, but He who gave the laws can, and that is what the second part of our Lord’s words is about. He says ‘It has been said by them of old time.’ In other words, I, the Lord Jesus Christ, said it in old time, for it was He who gave the law. He was King then, and He is now. ‘Thou shalt not forswear thyself, but thou shalt perform unto the Lord thine oaths.’ He is not going to say this again. It was said once and lasts always. He has a new message. Some of you think you must be careful about using the word God in your familiar talk, because God has set that apart for solemn purposes. But you do not think much of swearing by heaven, or earth, or Jerusalem, or your head, and care little how lightly you use these oaths. Whether you know it or not, this comes from want of reverence for God. You think only the name is sacred. No! Everything is sacred. Look up at the wide heaven: the sun, the moon, the firmament all speak of Him. Look at the earth: every tree, plant, and flower speaks of Him. Go into Jerusalem: there is the Temple where God promised to dwell. Think of your own head: it is a witness for God, for He preserves every hair of it. So I say, Swear not at all. If you trifle with an oath, you trifle with God, in whose presence you live, and move, and have your being.

“My friends, these are the words of Christ’s new covenant. They were not spoken only to the disciples; they are spoken to you who are under this covenant. You are baptized men, children of God, members of Christ, heirs of heaven. Christ has other, better, higher words for you than those He spoke in old time. He tells you He has brought you into God’s immediate presence, adopted you into His family, and sealed you with His Spirit. He begs you to remember this honour and glory, and so He says to the heart of each of you, ‘Swear not at all.’ When you go out to your work in the morning, look cheerfully and reverently up to heaven and say: ‘The sky I am to labour under today is my Father’s throne. It is holy, and I must not trifle with it. This earth I am to till with the sweat of my brow is my Father’s footstool. He cares for it and makes it bud and bear. This, too, is holy.’ When you go to market in town, look up at the churches and say: ‘This too is my Father’s dwelling-place. These are witnesses that I am a citizen of the New Jerusalem, that I belong to an innumerable company of saints and angels, and that all the men about me are of the same family. The city and its men are holy.’ When you come home at night and lie down with no one near, think: ‘In this body of mine God has said He will dwell and make it His temple. This too is holy.’

“It is your privilege as Christians to have these calm, happy thoughts wherever you go and whatever you do. They are the thoughts of new men who believe themselves redeemed to God by the blood of Christ. Think how contrary to them every idle oath is! What pride it has in it, what contempt of God, what setting up of ourselves, and generally what cruelty to our brethren! Depend on it, every such oath makes men feel God’s oaths less sacred, and makes them more likely to forswear themselves when sworn in court. If you think such a crime would be horrible, and wish to obey Christ’s first command, ‘Perform unto the Lord thy oaths,’ then attend to His second command and do not swear at all in your talk with one another. Take the spirit of these commands into you by remembering always and everywhere that you are in the presence of a Father who loves you, and that all things testify of Him. Then you may go to court tomorrow with free, clear spirits. You will not think as slaves and cowards think, ‘I must not lie, or I shall be prosecuted for perjury, or lose my place, or be pointed at.’ You will speak the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, because God is truth and you know that liars cannot stand in His sight. You will know that He helps and blesses every true-hearted servant and child of His, and brings them on farther in the knowledge of Himself.”

I think there is nothing in this that a plain man would reject as unintelligible or an honest man as sophistical. It marks a great moral distinction between two acts that the Quakers have hastily confounded merely because they share a name. It shows there was a reason for putting the command against conversational swearing in this place. The Jews had not argued that they might trifle with oaths because oaths were enjoined on the most solemn occasions. That subtlety never occurred to them, ingenious as they were at finding pleas for evil practices in a tortured use of the law’s letter. But they had persuaded themselves that if they kept away from the dreadful name of Jehovah, they did not violate the awfulness of oaths by using them familiarly. In striking at this hypocritical notion, our Lord threw a new and brilliant light on His own dispensation. He showed how He came to bring men into God’s very presence and to hallow every place with it. He bound together two things: the Hebrew’s awed sense of a distinct personality with an unutterable name, and the sense of a God everywhere, which had been struggling to find expression in the Greek Pantheist. So this prohibition was not superfluous for that time or any time. It ties a rule of daily life to the deepest principles of our being. Adopt the Quaker notion of the passage, and our Lord says, in a sermon addressed especially to men’s hearts and consciences, “Once, without a reason, I told you to do a certain act; now, without a reason, I tell you not to do it.” Adopt ours, and we see the eternal grounds of these precepts in two principles, both involved in the constitution of man. Each was developed in its due season, one by the teacher who had the veil over his face, the other by Him who came to show the express image of God in human flesh. Each is to be upheld for the sake of the other, and each loses its own stability when the other is forgotten.

8. The Quakers, and those who share their theory of the Sermon on the Mount, hardly distinguish between two sayings: “An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth,” and “Thou shalt love thy neighbour and hate thine enemy.” They take both as much the same kind of proverb, describing the savage character of the old dispensation, which Christ replaces with the mild spirit of the new. An interpretation that confuses two maxims so different in object and application must be vicious in itself. The first plainly concerns the judicial proceedings of the commonwealth, the second its external relations. Good or bad, harsh or gentle, they do not mean the same thing, and anyone who wants to read our Lord’s words strictly will take them one at a time. I begin with “An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth.”

The sense of retribution and compensation, that every injury deserves its recompense and the evil suffered should match the evil done, works in the heart of every savage and secretly justifies his blind acts of fury. He constantly overshoots. He exacts far more than the wrong committed, and his conscience reproaches him for that excess. Confused as he is, you can make him admit that an evil spirit drove him to take more than the equivalent. But it is useless to press him further, and I believe it is not right to. It sets his conscience a task it is not ready for and makes him doubt what it does testify. Why is a man who approaches his brother’s soul with fear and trembling, aware of how wrong it is to displace one true conviction there, and of how tender one must be where the good is weakest and least guarded, so careful not to destroy this idea of adjustment and retribution in the savage? Because he believes every transgression does and must receive its just recompense. Because the absence of this sense in the savage would mean the absence of any feeling of law, and its presence may lead him to acknowledge that he himself is under law. So it is the essence of a lawgiver’s duty to take this principle of God’s government, which each man is groping after and which he uses to excuse acts of violence (acts that feed the inward hatred which joined with this right feeling to produce them, and harden it into habit). The lawgiver must make it the rule of his own proceedings and enforce it strictly, so that men feel a power ruling over them which redresses the wrong each had so impotently and harmfully tried to redress for himself. The lawgiver says: “You are all members of one body. The law cares for each of you separately and feels every wrong done to any of you as a wrong to itself. It will require of everyone who injures another that he make compensation and satisfaction. It aims, as far as it can, to make every wrongdoer suffer in the same kind and degree in which he offended.”

I maintain, then, that “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth” lies at the foundation of a State and, more than any other principle, explains what a State is. It is a righteous principle; I had almost called it the righteous principle, because it gives the most complete picture of the order and moral government of the world and shows the rights of each person in connection with that order. Vengeance must be somewhere: “It is mine, saith the Lord.” The State is what teaches each man that there is a Lord, an invisible ruler, judge and governor over him, whose authority he must acknowledge and against whose authority every act of private vengeance offends.

This much the law can do, and nothing but a law can do it. No spiritual principle acting on the life of man, reforming and regenerating heart and will, can bear this witness to a God who punishes wrongdoing or bring men to grasp the system of retribution established in the universe. Unless a power stands out and says, “An eye for an eye,” “As you have done so shall it be rendered to you,” and embodies that principle in regular, expected, proportionate punishment, nothing in the secret work of shaping a man into the Divine image suggests that man is actually under such a government. If we look only at that inward work, all our feelings about God are absorbed into one, that He renews and sanctifies our lives, and the belief in Him as judge, which is the belief in His personality, is in danger of being lost. On the other hand, the law with its scale of retributions cannot reach any man’s heart or spirit, or produce the tone of feeling that it shows to be necessary. It may tell a man that vengeance is the Lord’s and that private vengeance will itself be counted a crime. It may even convince him that his private fury is inconvenient to himself as well as his neighbour and an outrage on society. But it can do nothing to take the principle and desire of vengeance out of him. It cannot make cheerful citizens who recognize its righteousness and delight in it, nor guard against the outbreaks of a will that rises above the rules of the understanding. To check these, it must resort to some spiritual influence, and that can be of three kinds. It may be mere discipline, as in Sparta, which excludes education, drives all the faculties in one direction and leaves them consciously feeble when they try any other. It may be an education of the calculating faculty alone, a skilful experiment to destroy every thought and feeling that interferes with the hope of tangible advantage, possible only in a thoroughly enervated age and then only briefly. Or it may be the kind of influence that acts directly on the will, strips it of selfishness and conforms it to the perfect will of God. To use this, the lawgiver must seek the aid of Him who said, “Resist not the evil,” and “if any man will take thy cloak, let him have thy coat also,” and who in saying so showed that He “was not come to destroy but to fulfil the law,” including “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth.”

So we come back to our old doctrine. The law protests against the selfish, individual principle and raises a standard against it, and the Gospel comes to root that same selfish principle out of the heart. On the very ground that I hold tribunals for righting social wrongs to be a godlike institution carrying Divine authority, I hold that the principle of turning the other cheek is godlike and carries Divine authority. Patient endurance of private wrongs and indignities, and complete willingness to refrain from recrimination or even self-justification, are parts of the Christian character. Every Christian should seek them, and without them he should feel his profession maimed and imperfect, and that he is not living out his baptism. He will plead for no toleration of selfishness in himself, but will want every sign of it detected and exposed so that it may be exterminated. He has no fear of pushing our Lord’s commands too far and sacrificing his civil duties to his Christian ones. The law will not thank him for being litigious, and he does not confirm its authority by turning it to private advantage. But as he communicates his spirit to the world (and the higher his standard and practice, the more he communicates), the law will be better observed and all its subjects will regard it with more cheerful reverence. Note, however, that he binds himself to a principle, not a rule. By God’s help he resolves never to resist evil for a selfish purpose, because he knows that is our Lord’s meaning. He does not say he will not resist evil, for that would be to say he would not be like his Lord, whose whole life on earth, and whose whole life in His members, is a constant resistance to evil. He resolves not to go to law to avenge himself or make a profit. He does not resolve never to go to law if the dignity of law is attacked by some illegal power. It is most harmful to think there can be the least departure from a great moral principle, and most absurd to claim that quite opposite methods may not at times be needed to uphold it. When Hampden resisted ship-money, I think he obeyed our Lord’s precept better than if he had paid the tax, for he was sacrificing his own interest for the dignity of law, not using the law to advance his interest. Where private interests are not in play, the conscience of mankind sees this distinction as broad and plain. And each man’s conscience, guided by the Spirit of God and seeking light from His Word, will be able to carry it into daily practice when private interests are involved. So we offer this case too as an illustration of our Lord’s basic proposition, that He does not destroy but fulfils.

9. One remark by way of introduction to the next passage, the last that concerns our present subject: the whole argument against war, so far as it comes from this sermon or from the New Testament, must turn on that passage. After what I have just said, it is clear that no attempt to draw a condemnation of war, or any allusion to it, from “Resist not evil,” “He that smiteth you on one cheek,” and the like can succeed. The application of the new doctrine must be settled by the application of the old. If “an eye for an eye” refers to judicial proceedings, the words that follow, whether they repeal or confirm it, must point the same way. It is plain torturing of their letter and gross perversion of their spirit to connect them with another class of cases, which our Lord treats separately. So let it be clear that the words on which the question turns are these. It was said of old, “Love your neighbour and hate your enemy.” Christ says, love your enemies, bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse and persecute you, so that you may be children of your Father in heaven, who makes His sun rise on the evil and the good and sends rain on the just and the unjust. If you love only those who love you, what reward have you? Do not even the publicans do the same?

One more observation is needed for a fair examination. The words our Lord attributes to the men of old do not appear in exactly those terms in the Old Testament. So it has been argued that in this passage He may be denouncing a traditional maxim of the Scribes, probably a gloss on some text in Moses, and not a dogma carrying the authority of inspiration. I must think that a commentator who can deliberately take refuge in this has not learned that honesty and plain dealing are as much required in criticism as in everyday life. He does not find the words in any text of the Bible; does he not find the meaning in a hundred? Can he read the five books of Moses, Kings and Chronicles, and above all the Psalms, and say that if a scribe invented the phrase he did not capture in it very happily the feeling of the old time, or that our Lord does not plainly endorse his picture of it?

But leaving the phrase for now, let us notice the ground on which our Lord bases his new precept: “that ye may be the children of your Father in heaven,” because He makes His sun shine on the good and the evil and sends His rain on the just and the unjust. Here we see the whole principle of God’s dispensations. Their end is to restore the Divine image in man, and so every precept or command to man is joined to a revelation of something in God’s character. Man is never required to show any quality in himself that has not first been shown to him in its original and archetype. From this we can draw several corollaries. First, a precept of universal love, especially love for men as sinners, could be given only as hint and preparation until God had been completely manifested in His incarnate and dying Son, who is bound to all men, feels for them as they now are and cares for the vilest and most abject. Second, every earlier duty laid on men as a test of faithfulness and obedience must have corresponded to something in God’s character that had been made known to them. Third, since these two revelations of God’s character cannot contradict each other, and the qualities attributed to Him must in some sense be compatible, the corresponding duties in men cannot contradict each other either but must in some sense be compatible. Fourth, unless one revelation can be shown to merge into the other, so that all the qualities attributed to Him in the first are actually, if not apparently, contained in the second, the duties founded on them cannot merge either but must remain distinct obligations. A few words will illustrate each and show their bearing on our subject. On the first: it is plainly admitted that Christ came to establish a universal dispensation which did not exist before. It rests on a manifestation of God as absolute, universal love, and on the fact that in the person of His Son He has entered into relations with man as he is. To men so united to His Son He gives His Spirit, that they may be endowed with the same universal love which is His own essential nature and which was displayed in the acts and sufferings of a real man. This revelation and this command lie at the foundation of the Christian Church and are expressed in our baptism “into the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.” Those who enter this state are bound to love their enemies, indeed all men, because they see that God loves all. They love those who hate and persecute them because Christ died for those enemies and persecutors. They love even the enemies of God, because they regard them as creatures still wearing the flesh Christ wore, not yet finally separated from Him or deserted by His Spirit. They keep the command of the Sermon on the Mount strictly, fully and spiritually, for the reason the Lawgiver Himself gives: they “are the children of their Father in heaven.”

Our second corollary says that every duty laid on the Jews as a proof of their zeal and faithfulness was grounded on some declaration of God’s character. God is declared to be King over the Israelites, watching over them and caring for them as a father cares for his children. They are His chosen nation and appointed witnesses, and therefore they are to care for each other. They are to feel themselves members of a nation distinct from others, and to feel for each other as they do not feel for other men, since they are called to a distinct office and hold a distinct position which it is sin not to maintain: “they are to love their neighbours.” Again, God is revealed to them as waging war against evil in the world and upholding the law and order He established. In this work He sends judgments on nations, sweeping away multitudes by pestilence and famine, and vindicating the truth and order that are mankind’s only happiness at the cost of individual lives. He requires His chosen people to feel as He feels: to hate violations of law and order as He hates them, to be ready executors of His purposes, to maintain order and truth, to be avenged on those who violate them, and not to shrink from sacrificing individual life, sacred and awful as it is, for the sake of that without which life is a miserable lie. They are to regard their nation as established for this very end, to be a witness against evil, carrying on warfare within and without against those who remove landmarks, set might against right, turn the world into a wilderness and deny righteousness and God. It was not always to be fighting, and its witness was often silent. But there were occasions to draw the sword and throw away the scabbard, and at all times it was to hold its God-given position and resist invaders to the death. The exception was an awful time, such as came in the days of the prophet Jeremiah, when it was no longer a witness for God, when resisting the invader merely asserted a self-willed power that had thrown off the Divine yoke, when allegiance was dissolved and society at an end, and each man’s duty was to surrender and keep his life as spoil. But so long as the nation was a nation, owned God and was owned by Him, the maxim “Thou shalt hate thine enemy” expressed a duty as real and binding as the one it was joined to, “Thou shalt love thy neighbour.”

Our third corollary says that the revelation of God as universal love does not contradict the earlier revelation of Him as carrying on continual strife with whatever in our world resists law and order. So the duty of loving our enemies, grounded on the one, must somehow be compatible with the duty of hating our enemies, grounded on the other. Think what the idea of a Being of perfect love comes to, and has actually come to, when men treat these two attributes as opposed. Love becomes a weak, contemptible tolerance of the unlovely, a merciless mercy that can allow its creatures to be as sinful, that is as miserable, as they please, now and forever. Does not every man’s conscience strongly resist the verdict of his carnal understanding that there is strife between a law of love and a Being determined to carry that law out? Does he not feel that if he gave up either side of the character, the other would at once become unintelligible? And is it not the same, must it not be the same, in our judgments of ourselves and others? Is that love of good not paltry which is not joined with hatred of evil? Is hatred of evil not a mere name if it will not go out in acts to resist and extinguish evil? Has that man not a poor love of mankind, and of each man as one of them, who does not count as enemies those who hinder the good and advance the evil, and does not, as such, hate them? These are no sophistical refinements. They are the common, honest judgments of mankind, triumphing over sophistry, getting past the likeness of names and words to the real likeness of things, and so, even unconsciously, justifying the ways of God and asserting the harmony of His dispensations.

Our fourth position is that unless the view of God’s character given in the Jewish economy is contained in the view Christ gives in the Sermon on the Mount, the duties required of the Jew remain binding on the Christian and are not swallowed up in the duties that belong peculiarly to him as heir of the New Covenant. Our Lord says, “Love your enemies, for your Father in heaven causes His sun to shine on the good and the evil and sends His rain on the just and the unjust.” Here a fact of His providence is used to illustrate His character. But other facts of His providence exist alongside it without interfering with it. He who gives rain and sunshine also sends plagues and pestilences. These do not come to separate the good individuals from the evil, but they certainly come for discipline: to teach nations the effects of idleness, intemperance and sensuality, and gradually to lead them to tell truth from falsehood and good from evil. If these facts exist together as distinct signs of distinct though perfectly harmonious purposes in the Divine mind, then men, who are meant to be God’s images, should have some way of expressing each distinctly in their own minds and conduct. Christ comes to bring men closer to God, to give them power to fulfil His will completely, and to make them full vicegerents in carrying out His purposes toward the world. Surely He does not come to remove them from executing any part of the work to which He once called them. If He does not cease to judge and punish because He admits all into His kingdom of love, neither can it be meant that they should cease to judge and punish under Him because He has appointed them to publish His Gospel and open the doors of His kingdom. The only remaining question is how both forms of character can be kept at once, and how two sets of duties so apparently opposite can be fulfilled. There is plainly great danger in leaving out either and great danger in confusing them. I have already hinted how weak and ineffectual lovers we are when love is parted from law, and how monstrously we pervert the Divine law when we set it against love needs no explaining. But do we fare any better if we try to keep a balance in our minds, “not too much love, lest you grow lax; not too much law, lest you grow cruel”? Will this see-saw satisfy any man who wants to be honest with himself and others? Not too much love! How can there be too much, if dying for love was not too much? Not too much law! How can there be too much, if the destruction of cities and empires, indeed of a world, for its sake was not too much? Then, I say, there must be some distinct Divine scheme for asserting the dignity and glory of each: upholding love in its fulness lest law perish, and law in its fulness lest love perish. By acting in concert with each, a man will find that the feeling of God’s universal love in him does not clash with the feeling of God’s eternal, unchangeable law. He will see more and more clearly his own duties, inward and outward, and the perfect compatibility of those that seem most opposed. By refusing to act in concert with each scheme, he will find one set of duties continually interfering with another, with the peculiar temper of his mind deciding which he prefers and which he neglects. The words “Thou shalt love thy neighbour and hate thine enemy” are therefore not destroyed but fulfilled by “Love your enemies, bless them that curse you.”

I hope the reasons for my difference with the Quaker are now explained. I do not ask him to relax the strictness of his views, only to make them less narrow. I do not want him to have a less spiritual idea of Christian morality, but not to drain it of its spirit in practice by depriving it of its body. Above all, I want him to see that the scheme he has set up in the world to establish peace and charity is far less effective than the scheme God has set up for the same end. The Quaker society has had self-denying, brave men who bore witness to the truth that we are not to resist evil but, when smitten on one cheek, to turn the other. Such men must have done good, as everyone does who shows the world that selfishness is not his law, that he can obey a principle, and that God’s arm is more to be trusted than the arm of flesh. But nothing prevents the Catholic Church from bearing such testimonies, and many in every age have borne them. It is the other kind of Quaker testimony, his negative testimony, that seems to me worth nothing except where it gains some worth from mixing with the acts just mentioned, and in itself seriously harmful. Much in the worst feelings of men, especially in our day, sympathizes with Quaker language about war and punishments. There is a cowardly shrinking from physical suffering. There is a readiness to talk of the expense of national honour, because money is visible and honour invisible. There is an unreasonable, uncharitable and superstitious notion that a soldier, as far as his profession goes, belongs to this world, and that a man who dies on the battlefield is necessarily less prepared for death than one who dies in bed. All these feelings have sadly degraded and impoverished the mind of modern Europe. They have fostered the trade temper and made armies what they are told they must be, and so made them dangerous by depriving them of any high restraining principle. They have been greatly encouraged by the tone that religious men in our day have taken from the Quakers. What is such language doing for lasting, universal peace? It is the greatest obstacle to any high understanding of the word and to any hopeful expectation of the thing. Whoever translates the holy name “Peace” as carnal security or luxurious ease desecrates it and makes every scriptural use of it meaningless. Whoever teaches civilians to love their money above all, or soldiers to believe they have no vocation but a murderous one, helps make the first so weak that they must quail before any physical force, and the second so wicked that they must be ready to use it. The loss of all national spirit will lead, as it always has, not to a golden age of Christian brotherhood but to military despotism. The Church of Christ, as we have seen, has worked very differently in the world. It has been the instrument for putting down military despotism and calling forth national feeling. The sins of its ministers, who exalted their own position and put it outside the nation, and the sins of rulers who tried to suppress the spiritual power within their nations because it seemed to cross their projects, have thwarted the gracious design of Providence. But they have only made the character of that design clearer. They have shown that it is His will to establish peace. First, He creates in the heart of every nation a witness of what the true order and universal fellowship of the world is. Next, He uses the society that embodies this fellowship to cultivate the spirit of each nation, to awaken each to the purpose of its existence, and to destroy in each the motives to strife and division (the worst and strongest being the trade temper). Finally, He puts into the national ruler’s hand a sword for punishing those who love war rather than peace, a sword not to be sheathed or left to rust until all things are fulfilled.

II. It remains to say a few words about the Quaker’s objections to a national provision for Christian ministers. The remarks already made in this section will help greatly. We have found two societies, both organic, both part of the same constitution, both related to man under different aspects of his life and both bearing witness for God according to different aspects of His character. One is expressed in institutions like the Sacraments, which concern man as a spiritual being; the other in institutions like Property, which concern him as a creature of this earth. We have seen that under our dispensation the first and higher society has called the lower into life. Nothing is truer than the Quaker’s assertion that all the provisions made for ministers of the Gospel in the first ages were made by the love of their flocks. He is quite right, because the principle of the Gospel is love and its power acts directly on the spirit and will of man. He is right too that, since we live under the same dispensation of love as the Apostles, the principles that governed the Church then govern it now. But he has overlooked one thing: love does not look only at today or tomorrow but reaches into the future and embraces all ages together. The early Church’s love for St. Peter and St. Paul was not shown chiefly for their own sakes but for their sake as stewards of the mysteries of God and representatives of a kingdom that would never end. So it was inevitable that this charity should provide for the needs of the time to come, for the service of God and the benefit of men in generations unborn. I ask whether this far-looking charity does not mark a dispensation more than the charity that sees only present objects and thinks only of those to whom it is personally obliged. Yet it is this charity that underlies all the Quakers’ complaints against ecclesiastical property. That property is expressly a fund of which particular men in particular ages are only stewards, a fund for all ages.

I grant that whatever its origin, such a fund is a most dangerous possession for anybody unless the ends for which it exists are very clearly defined. The effect of the Church’s union with the nation is precisely to define these ends and make the property given to the ecclesiastical society as such available for clear, intelligible purposes. The Church establishes itself in a district, sets up its buildings and carries on its services by the processes I have already described. The people among whom it comes acquire national consistency and begin to have clearer notions of their relation to one another and of property. With regard to ecclesiastical property, what they do is give it the security that belongs to individual property and arrange for it to serve the education of the nation from age to age. They also require the owners of the soil to acknowledge directly that their own property, though secured to them individually, is a trust from God for the nation’s good and must contribute part of its produce to the common fund. That is the simple history of Church property and its connection with national property. It was increased later by voluntary gifts and bequests, often the fruit of superstitious influences, but influences acting on the will, and so not destroying the character of the gift. On the other hand, as everyone knows, the nation’s influence in the Middle Ages was used to prevent this accumulation of wealth and to make such gifts and bequests difficult or ineffective. Nothing is a greater distortion of history than to speak of ecclesiastical revenues as if they were the fruit of union with the State. The State has been a great instrument in God’s hands for preventing the mischiefs that might have come to the Church, and at times did come, from its influence over men’s minds. It has also been a great instrument in showing the Church the purposes for which its wealth was given and may be most profitably used. If we are asked what tokens there are that it is God’s will that a fund should exist for the teaching of the nation, exempt from the attacks of individual greed and not diverted to selfish uses, I will mention three.

First, there is the shameful inclination the clergy have often shown to turn this wealth to selfishness and luxury. There is the witness it has borne against that sin, the strong feeling and conscience in men that because the clergy were stewards of this property, selfishness was a greater sin in them than in others, and the heavy judgments that followed its exhibition. Second, there is the greedy readiness of the nobles and gentry to plunder this property. They have shown that this was part of the same disposition that made them careless of their tenants and dependants, lovers of self-indulgence, and ready to say, “The soil is our own; who is Lord over us?” They cultivated miserable habits in themselves and passed them on to their descendants, wherever there was not such a fixed and continual testimony against these wicked imaginings as Church property provides. Lastly, there is the complete inability of the Quakers, with all their charitable tendencies, acts and protests, to convince the world that they do not value wealth far above its worth, or that they do not regard gaining it as the main end of life. I do not say the impression is true. I do say that a society which exists to bear testimony against what is secular has utterly failed to make mankind understand its testimony. It has failed precisely because it has treated property as necessary yet evil, something to be toiled and watched for, yet not to be redeemed from temporary and servile uses for the continual service of God and man.
