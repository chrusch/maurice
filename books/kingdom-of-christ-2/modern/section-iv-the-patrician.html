Part II.— (Continued.) of the Catholic Church and the Romish System › Chapter V. On the Relations of the Church with National Bodies

Section IV.—The Patrician

Those who idealize the early Church’s first centuries as a golden age of discipline and unity forget its persecutions, heresies, and corruption, so national churches mark not decline but further providence.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Idea of a Golden Age.—Allegorical Interpretations of the Old Testament.—Church Discipline.—Extrusion of Heretics.—Catholic Unity amidst National Peculiarities.

There is another class of objectors to my statement, whose opinions deserve great respect. They do not see the union of the Church with the civil power in different nations as a positive evil. It was God’s ordinance, and like everything else He appoints, it is to be submitted to. But they say it is false and dangerous to claim that the Church is better for this state of things, or that its circumstances in modern Europe are better than in the first five or six centuries. The age of the Fathers is the pattern shown on the Mount, the true model of a Catholic Church. It had fellowship in faith and worship, discipline for moral offenders, and separation from wilful heretics. Since then the Eastern Church has split from the Western, and Protestants have divided from Romanists. Heresies have been tolerated and discipline made light of. The idea of national Churches has replaced the idea of a universal Church, and in each nation the Church is treated as part of the civil establishment. This condition, they say, is to be borne with humiliation and patience, not spoken of with triumph.

1. The feeling that the Church once had a golden age, which we may look back to and pray to see restored, is so deeply rooted in men of every circumstance and opinion that only a very thoughtless Christian could treat it lightly. Those who confine that age to the time before Constantine, or to the lives of the Apostles, cannot fairly complain against those who praise the first six centuries. Even the most limited period cannot be called pure. While the Epistles to the Corinthians and Galatians stay in the Canon, indeed until every epistle is stripped of its sharpest warnings, we must admit, as everyone in practice has, that even then there were spots in the feasts of charity. Every form of corruption and every habit that threatens apostasy could be found in Christ’s infant family. Shall we then say the glory of the apostolic period lay in the Church’s ministers, though not its members? The epistles to the angels of the seven churches get in the way of that. Some of those churches had lost their first love, and some did not exercise discipline. Some tolerated a heresy, which a fairly well supported tradition traces to one of the seven Deacons. Some had a name to live and were dead, and some were neither hot nor cold. Are we ready, then, to give up the feeling that the apostolic periods differed from others? If not, I think we must adopt some such hypothesis as this, which will perhaps satisfy most who give it leisure: that in the age of the Apostles there was a more distinct and evident conflict with different forms of evil, both outside and inside the Church, than there ever has been since. It was a critical period, one might say the critical period in the history of mankind. It therefore brought all dark images to light beside the perfect light they opposed. This, it may be, is the true idea of the golden age, and the only one the Church’s past offers us. It was not an age of innocence but of conflict. It was not holding itself up as a model to the world. It was bringing out the idea of the Church as a body belonging to no age, the permanent witness against the secular spirit that always makes some period of time, rather than the principles shown in it, the object of its admiration.

Surely the history of the first five or six centuries shows the Church in the same kind of struggle. The battle changed its character after Constantine’s accession, but who will say it grew less severe? Who will say the Divine image was less brought out through these strifes in men like Athanasius and Chrysostom than in Ignatius or Justin? The whole history of this period is a book of the wars of the Lord. It is precious because it shows strength made perfect in weakness, working itself out in individual men and in all of society. It shows the shouts of the soldiers of the Cross laying low the highest Babel tower ever raised in this world. It shows that God’s scheme was to prevail and nothing could withstand it. But the picture is not one of still, beautiful, pastoral life. It is a picture of great crimes and great virtues, often in the same men, always illustrating each other, and always letting us see what man is with God and without Him. God has shown us this age not in particular specimens of excellence but as a whole, and as a whole it is precious. We lose its blessing, and the idea of the Church it represents, if we leave out any of its darker features. So consider what we do when we wish this age restored in fact. If we ask for the age of St. Paul’s preaching to return, we ask for Nero to return. If we ask to be taken back to the glorious time of Athanasius, we ask to live under the tyrant Constantius, with the world almost wholly pagan and the Church almost wholly Arian. If we long to sit at Chrysostom’s feet, we long for the infamous corruption of Antioch and Constantinople, for the government of Eutropius, and for the villainies of the palace eunuchs. If we think it a blessing to have lived under Augustine’s teaching, we must accept Rome sacked by one set of barbarians, the African Church threatened by another, our learning taken from worn-out rhetoricians, and life amid all the seductions and abominations of Manicheism. These considerations are commonplace, but they are true, and for the honour of the Church and its Head we should dwell on them now and then. They also help remove a fantastic habit of mind quite foreign to the temper of the early ages. They cannot make us despise the Fathers, or adopt the notions about them recently spread in this country with more self-conceit than learning, which could only have caught on through some weakness in the theory they opposed. If we are reasonable, the more we see the mass of evil in society that the Church was sent to break down, the more we shall admire the power that broke it down and be thankful for the instruments He used.

2. But can we say all this and see nothing more than a permission in the national societies which the Church called into being, and with which in the Western world she became identified? Must we not believe it was a mighty step in the development of the Divine scheme, in establishing the Divine kingdom on earth? The Church had been brought out as one body existing in different places to test its strength against the Roman world, and it prevailed. It made good its principle against terrible odds. What is more reasonable than to believe it was to carry on its work of creation along the very line on which the world’s work of destruction had run? The Roman empire had swallowed all nations into itself. Its mighty antagonist was to make them breathe again and show each its proper place and function in God’s kingdom and commonwealth. Are we told this savours of those godless and intolerable modern theories of progress? I do not care what bad name it is given, provided it justifies God’s ways to man and shows the Church has not existed for nothing. But if the charge means we hold a notion of progress inconsistent with the permanence of God’s order and truth, no charge was ever so ill supported. They are the innovators and the deniers of the permanence of God’s order. They would make the Church merely a growth out of past and exhausted states of society, who maintain that national life, so honoured in the old world, has been discarded as worthless in the new. We say the Divine mind’s counsels did not change in this way, and that the history of modern Europe proves it. We say the Church’s constitution, as shown in the conflicts with the Roman empire, was simply a universal constitution. We rejoice that it was, since this showed such a constitution to exist and made plain the meaning of all the past history that had been leading to its discovery. But we say it was necessarily imperfect. It left unaccounted for all the relations that hold men together as neighbours and distinguish them by race and language, and did not bring them under Church influence. Since the Old Testament especially develops these relations, one aspect of that Testament was necessarily lost to the first age of the Church. I know this language will shock some good people, so I will try to explain myself.

3. It is commonly said that we must look to the Fathers as interpreters of Scripture because they were close to the Apostles and might have heard their very words. I have never understood this doctrine of proximity. It would prove that Athanasius, Ambrose or Augustine must be far less valuable to us than Hermas or Ignatius, and I do not think the Church in any age has felt that. It seems to me rather that the Fathers’ great worth came from God placing them in circumstances that especially enabled them to grasp certain great truths, above all the foundation truths about His Being and the order of the Universal Society. On that view I can believe each had his own special merits. The Alexandrian might see what the Latin could not. One principle would come out in power through him who struggled with Arians, another through him who had fought Manicheism in his own heart and in the world. On the other view I think I would risk making an age into a Church, and exalting some individuals of that age above others who perhaps had a more important work.

Whichever view is adopted, it will be hard to prove the Fathers were better placed than other men to understand the circumstances of the Jewish nation. They had no proximity to that nation’s fathers. The Jews they could talk with were of three kinds. Some had let their national feelings dissolve into wider Catholic sympathies. Some were trying to set their old national distinctions against the Church. Some regarded their whole past as little more than a collection of allegories or the development of a Mosaic philosophy. Nor could the Fathers’ circumstances give them any sympathy with the old national life of the Jews. The temple was gone and the city laid waste. These events had been necessary to establish the universal Church, and that Church stood in the midst of a great empire in which no nation “moved the wing, or opened the mouth, or peeped.” Would it not be reasonable to expect, in such circumstances, the following? In polemic, they could prove clearly that the Jewish Commonwealth was meant as the seed of a great tree. In experience, they could show that the Jewish saints struggled with the same inner enemies that assailed themselves. In mysticism, they could show an invisible guide and teacher training man to know Him through all past ages. But whatever belonged to the common, daily, human life of the Jews would puzzle them utterly, seem out of place in a Divine book, and so of necessity be turned into cabalistic lore. Would anyone not expect this from the Fathers’ position? And suppose the facts fit these expectations exactly. Every Christian today who looks into them is puzzled by subtle spiritualisations of facts which delighted him as a child and, as he grew up, seemed to connect with what is going on in the world around him. The Church even of that age condemned one use of this spiritualising method, yet an extravagant use of it was justified in the practice of the most learned and laborious of the Fathers, and it is hard to find where he crossed the legitimate rule. In that case, which is wiser, more pious and more respectful to these holy men? One option is to say they could not take in the literal meaning of the old Scriptures so as to give it any life, and that it was not intended they should. The other is to make out a case for them by renouncing our own advantages and resolutely praising a system of interpretation our consciences and hearts keep rejecting, and then, after all, to give up defending it once it is clearly and consistently worked out.

I will not stop to remark, though it must be obvious, that the Fathers’ ideas about marriage, property and every institution belonging first to our earthly condition must have been strongly affected by their views of the Old Testament. They would always have looked for the highest, most transcendental ground on which to defend such ordinances. Since the ordinances must exist, they would readily treat them as types of something higher. But they could not see how to connect the type with the actual fact, or how to avoid concluding that whatever is not directly of heaven belongs in some sense to human depravity. It will be impossible for us too, I believe, if we feel no more than a grumbling acquiescence in God’s scheme for educating our race. We must acknowledge that by restoring national life through the Church He was carrying out the law of redemption. He was showing that everything belonging to man and his position here, everything that does not violate that position, is comprehended under it.

4. But it is affirmed that the early Church recognized the discipline of moral offenders as essential to its existence, and that this has almost been lost sight of since the Church entered into fellowship with the nations. I admit at once that the principle of spiritual discipline is implied in the constitution of the spiritual body. This is a discipline for rectifying habits and principles, far deeper and subtler than any lawgiver can exercise, and like the Church’s other principles it was first shown to the world in its first ages. I admit further that, alongside this highest power, there is one implied in the existence of every society, and especially needed in the Church. That is the power of noticing the more outward and flagrant transgressions of its members and deciding how far they exclude the offender from its privileges. I think the early Church exercised this power. It was almost the only witness to the degraded, sensualized slaves of a military despotism that any authority in heaven or earth could punish the offences of the high as well as the low. One example like St. Ambrose’s sentence on Theodosius was in practical effect worth all the volumes of the Roman jurists. Yet the history of that transaction and the others of that period proves the Church was quite unable to deal with the accumulated profligacy among those admitted to its fellowship. Councils might decide that certain crimes excluded the offender from the Eucharist for five, six or twenty years, yet we know the blackest crimes were committed by clergymen as well as laymen. And everyone must sometimes ask whether the Church, while claiming such lofty, heavenly powers, was not often forced by her circumstances to degrade them and become too much a mere spiritual police.

I readily allow that restoring fixed, positive national law and formal tribunals for punishing crime has made men less aware of the meaning and need of spiritual discipline. The stricter and more definite this law has become, and the more men have accepted it as a fixed, necessary element of their lives, the less the sense of another kind of government, one reaching feelings and character, has prevailed. There has even been a desire to be rid of it altogether. But none of this convinces me that establishing outward law and forming national societies were not part of God’s great scheme for developing more fully the nature and character of Christ’s kingdom. Looking at it the plain way, I compare any modern European nation, even Russia, with the state of the Roman and Byzantine empires in the first seven centuries. I should tremble at my own ingratitude if I did not wonder at the change God has wrought. I affirm this change is not merely one of outward, material happiness, but is connected with the very ends for which the Church exists. First, it seems to me a positive good that the Church should not be seen as a police, and that another body should perform that function and leave her to find out her own. Second, I believe every circumstance of later European history has been enabling the Church in each nation to discover, if she will, what her powers are. They are far greater than the statesman’s, and indispensable to him. In the first age spiritual discipline tried to be everything. Since then outward law has tried to be everything, and spiritual discipline has been forgotten. I showed in my first part how clearly and awfully it has been demonstrated that law cannot do what it wishes and pretends to do. So I think we are coming to a time when the spiritual side of Christ’s kingdom must come forward as it has not yet done, and each nation will demand the Church’s education and discipline to preserve its own existence. But the Church can profit by this crisis only if she acknowledges that, by the will of her Author and Lord, she is not meant to have an independent existence or to be extranational. She has no commission or powers that dispense with positive, formal law and outward government. Her highest honour is to be the life-giving energy in every body in whose midst she dwells.

5. The objection runs: “In old times the Church was able to cast heretics out of her bosom. Once she is connected with the nation, the nation either punishes them by its own crude methods, or else tolerates them and pays no heed to the Church’s sentence.” I think the same observations apply here. The Church’s character and constitution in the early ages showed themselves in her contests with heretics. One partial theory after another was put forward, and one article of the Creeds after another was set aside or pared away. Often against terrible odds, the Church asserted the integrity of her constitution, the spiritual unity that is the ground of her existence. To prevent divisions she was driven to draw subtle distinctions. She often accepted the charge of word-fighting so that she could keep hold of the realities that the word-fighters were emptying out. She often seemed to puzzle the traveller so that she could preserve his inheritance for him. For this struggle she needed her individual champions and her general councils. I have no doubt this was the early Church’s work, and that, despite the sins of even her best and bravest members, she fulfilled it as it was meant to be fulfilled.

But does not everyone feel, reading ecclesiastical history, that this position was glorious in some respects and unfortunate in many? In these struggles against those who would divide her, the Church was maintaining her character as a kingdom, held together by its relation to an awful name that she was bound to assert and defend. Yet the strife she entered for this purpose seriously weakened the impression that she was such a kingdom. It led men more and more to imagine that the battle was over a set of dogmas or opinions, which one party of doctors, called Catholics, had agreed to maintain against the world but could not settle among themselves. If Pagans or Jews had taken up this notion, it would have been regrettable. When Christians and their teachers fell into it themselves, the very existence of the Church was endangered. And that is what happened. The Greeks especially, proud of the gift of subtlety that has always been theirs, seem to have lost without noticing the whole substance, though they could distinguish every part of it so accurately. Their zeal and decrees against old heresies called up new ones. They felt less and less that the Church was anything but a school, even while they were using, without scruple, measures that could be justified to their consciences only by the belief that she was the one human fellowship. I think we all feel relief at escaping from this Greek world of controversy into the Latin world of business and enterprise. There may be much evil there too, but practical work is going on, societies are growing, and the Church is felt to be a society governed by certain laws and informed by a certain principle. Who can fail to see that, amid all the contradictions of the Middle Ages, the sense of a common bond, of one spirit and one object, was far more fully realised than it had been, except by a very few of the highest minds, in the age of the Œcumenical Councils? This is what we should expect if we really believed that the Church was under God’s government, and that He was making her meaning and power plain amid the perplexities and distractions of human self-will. However strange it may seem, the connexion of Church polity with national polity was what kept this feeling alive. The Greeks could not see the Church as a kingdom because nothing had taught them what a kingdom was. They lived under a despot with whom they had no sympathy, who ruled through creatures and tools rather than as the centre of a social order.

A fact that seems at first to contradict this, and to give a better reason for the Western Church’s character, actually confirms it. The Pope, it will be said, made the Middle Ages conscious of their Church unity, and yet the Pope was the great enemy of national societies. I answer that it was the growth of national life in the West that made it possible for a Bishop to take the position the Pope took. Once national feeling was strongly developed, men could no longer see the Church as a mere learned school. They had to see her as a kingdom, since she was exercising a kingdom’s powers, and in no other character could they have paid her homage. So the Bishop of Rome could give this kingdom an outward, formal character. He could put himself at its head and act, amid continual reluctance and displays of independence, as feudal superior of the societies into which Christendom was divided. But note the condition: the Pope himself becomes the sovereign of a state, and so honours the very principle he is setting at naught. I feel the anomaly of this. Still, it explains how truly it was God’s will that the Nations should come into being, and how necessary this was for the Church’s development, not for her chastisement. Returning to the point before us, this also explains the new position of those who differ from the Church’s doctrine or order. In the first age they are pronounced guilty of violating the tradition and creed of the universal society. In the second they are treated as invaders of the order and unity of the particular state where they are found, or of the states generally so far as these feel united in a common object. In the third the state judges that it best consults its own peace by leaving them alone, giving them a settled position, and treating them like its other subjects. I have already said why I believe each state will go on doing this only while it keeps its relation to a spiritual society, and why, the moment it becomes a mere civil body, it will resort to force again to put down opinion. But here I am looking at the question in another light, and I ask whether this is not a most important step in God’s plans. First, the spiritual society in her general councils took cognizance of schisms and heresies, but she ran the risk of seeming only the maintainer of a system of opinions, not the pillar and ground of truth. She inadvertently cultivated the very spirit of disputation she wished to check. Next, the particular states showed that they were interested in repressing schisms and heresies, thereby testifying that these are practical matters that really interfere with government and order. But in giving this testimony the states also did the Church a great injury. They put themselves in her place, using crude visible weapons for an invisible, spiritual end.

Lastly, the states have held back, or are inclined to hold back, from these experiments, having found them ineffective. But now they are inclined to regard all spiritual matters as dealings between quarrelling schools and sects, with which the national government has little to do. At most, when the quarrels grow violent, it keeps the peace between them, and otherwise leaves them to destroy one another. Now, I say, if there is a Church in the world, these are circumstances in which she can give evidence of her reality that she could not give in either earlier period. She cannot cut off heretics from her communion, for they have cut themselves off and do not care for it. But she can show that she has a secret power within her that may unite them. She can show that the fine distinctions of the Fathers and councils were meant not to cause separation but to prevent it. They were meant to preserve truth in its fullness until a time when men, having tried their different plans and methods of thought, would begin to desire something that could reconcile them. They would then accept no higher evidence of a Divine mission in any body than that it satisfies this aspiration. Nor can the Church make civil rulers see that she has a power of the same kind as theirs, for when they have fought her with her own weapons they have prevailed and she has been foiled. But she may prove to them that she has another power, wholly distinct from theirs and far higher, to which they must turn or perish in their feebleness.

6. I will close this head with some remarks on the claim that one Catholic Church cannot be recognized under the limits of national bodies. The objection runs: “How can a Church be one if it has no visible tokens of unity? Those who dream with the Quakers that the Church is a purely spiritual, unseen body may think ceremonial uniformity worthless. But defenders of National Churches want a strict, formal ecclesiastical organisation. Yet on their view it must differ in every country. There can be no general councils, because everything is subject to the will of particular princes, and even provincial synods and national convocations are viewed jealously by the civil power. What can follow but that the Church loses the features by which its identity is recognized in different lands? It adapts more and more to local habits, until it is prouder of what separates it from other communities than of what it shares with them.”

My answer begins from what I have maintained against the Quaker: certain permanent ordinances express the character and universality of the Church. She therefore does not depend for her unity on the faith and feeling of her members. She bears a constant witness against the lack of faith or feeling in any or all of them. If this is true, and God means these ordinances to denote the universal, spiritual society, we should expect him to show the difference between them and whatever is only accidentally attached to them. Some accidents are unavoidable. Baptism and the Eucharist must be administered in some mode, there must be rules about the jurisdiction of Bishops, and if there are forms of prayer there must be set forms. Other accidents are not necessary but have become sacred, in parts of the Church or in all of it, for particular reasons. The general legislation of the early Church certainly tended to join a particular way of treating the ordinances to the ordinance itself. I do not say the early Church wanted this. I believe the proceedings and decrees of councils show a cautious and often subtle wisdom in separating what was merely needed institutional arrangement from what belonged to the thing itself. A higher wisdom was also at work, keeping different parts even of an empire like Rome from holding to one ritual as faithfully as they held to the Creed and the ordinances. Still, the effect of ecumenical government, somewhat for those who lived then and much more for us who only read their history, is to blur this distinction. It keeps us from seeing the Divine sign in its own simplicity, apart even from the most desirable and indispensable arrangements about it.

When the Bishop of Rome tried to perpetuate this ecumenical legislation in his own person, the danger became far greater. The signs of the Church were fearfully confused with the ecclesiastical appointments devised to make them effective, and nothing could so obscure the Divine origin and constitution of the Church. Her eagerness to claim power to legislate in emergencies hid the fact that she rested on any principles, or that God had legislated for her. Yet she could not in practice keep up a strictly universal legislation, because local habits, maxims and precedents interfered, which made the confusion of authorities and obligations worse. Then came the Reformation. It asserted God’s law as above the Church’s law, and when its maxims were perverted it suggested that men’s own wills or notions were above both. Meanwhile the Church within each nation had begun in practice to claim power to decree rites and ceremonies. Properly understood and used, that claim was the strongest witness against equating ordinances with ceremonies. It honored the ordinances as gifts to the universal Church. It treated the ceremonies as needful provisions for the spiritual body to manage. That body is bound to teach men by outward evidence that they belong to one family, and must not let its own theories of what suits this purpose mix with the great principles of God’s government. Various causes spoiled this witness. When a national ecclesiastical body was checked by individuals unwilling to submit to the rules every society needs, rules that are especially sacred in a society meant to be the pattern of human fellowship, it pressed its pretensions harder and identified what it decreed with the very being of the Church. This was especially likely in nations with a strict civil organisation, always exercising their powers of legislation. The ecclesiastical body would catch the habit of thinking it existed merely to legislate. It would be prouder of its rules for its own government than of all its influence on the heart of society. If such a mind became common, I can imagine no greater mercy than the civil power stepping in to stop clerical convocations or discourage provincial synods. Without such violence I cannot see how a national Church could have learned what its own powers were. I think it would have been crushed under its own decrees and, above all, lost sight of the only grounds of unity it can have with other nations. On the other hand, this discipline has shown, I think, what the substance and essence of a national Church are. Its substance is given by the ordinances it holds in common with Christians elsewhere. Its essence is the powers it shares with the other parts of the body, which are to be used first for the good of its own country. When this lesson is well learned, I have no doubt that each national Church will recover its synods and convocations, because it will then know how to use them. It will not use them from a lust for legislation, or in hope of reaching its chief aims by decrees. It will use them to satisfy scruples. It will use them to lead men away from restless study of externals, by not forcing them to arrange and debate these for themselves. It will use them to fix the ceremonies that best express the Church’s great ideas for a people of a particular climate, character and constitution. It will use them to establish and direct discipline and education more effectively. And it will use them to promote fellowship with national Churches willing to own themselves parts of one Catholic body.

Whether national Churches, once they understand their position, may again send representatives to a general council, and whether the princes may find this helpful instead of harmful to their aims, I will not inquire. I see no reason why we might not reach such a result if we follow God’s method. But I see the strongest reason why, if we violate that method and seek an independent existence, no councils, compacts or projects of union can be anything but evil in themselves and full of mischief.

Should we then say that Church unity belonged to the first six centuries, and that since national distinctions began there have been no traces of it? We have been able to see, I think, that the separation of the Greek and Latin Churches, so often lamented and a cause of shame and humiliation to both, nevertheless led to very blessed results. It freed the Church from a cruel and mischievous tyranny and let it develop its powers under freer and happier conditions. Likewise, if we consider the subject calmly and solemnly, with repentance for our sins and thankfulness for our mercies, I believe we shall see that without the sixteenth-century Reformation, European society must have sunk into the state of an infidel world. It would have been nominally ruled by the intriguing head of a little Italian principality, and really divided into warring states, each pursuing the most selfish ends and using religion only as a means to them. Since then it has been clear to thinking people that two principles struggle for supremacy in Christendom. One is embodied in Protestantism. It resists any claim of the spiritual power to rule beyond the nation, and always tends to set spiritual authority aside altogether. The other is embodied in Romanism. It resists the attempts of particular states to cut their subjects off from the rest of Christendom, keeps striving to uphold the Church as a separate power, and sets at naught the existence of each nation. These principles have fought for centuries. Suppose it is God’s purpose in our age to reconcile them, and to cast out of each the element contrary to his will, which men’s perversity brought in. Shall we then whine about not being born when the Church was making its first struggling efforts to assert its unity? Shall we not rejoice and give thanks that we are born in these latter days, when all things hasten to their consummation, and when the unity of the Church shall be shown to be the ground on which all unity in nations and in the heart of man rests?
