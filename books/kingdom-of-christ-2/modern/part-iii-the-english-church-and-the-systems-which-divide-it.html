Part III. The English Church and the Systems Which Divide it

Read through its Liturgy and Articles, the English Church holds Catholic, Calvinist, Lutheran, and Quaker truths together without their rival systems, its bond with the nation forged by history rather than contract.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Chapter I. How Far This Subject is Connected with Those Previously Discussed

Do these principles apply to our situation as Englishmen? If they do not, some flaw in them must have gone unnoticed. If they do, then how to apply them is the most important question we face.

I think the young English student of the Church is likely to be perplexed by questions of this kind. “Is our National Church really, as I am often told, the best in the world? If it is not, why may I not go and look for a better? It is easy to talk of accepting the state Providence has given us, but surely there are circumstances in which a Christian must count acceptance a sin. How do I know mine are not such circumstances?”

If the principles that have proved themselves in other cases fit this one, the reader will see at once that these questions are wrongly framed. We have maintained that there is a spiritual and universal society in the world, and also that there are national societies. According to the scheme of Providence the two cannot be separated, and when they are joined, the character intended for each nation is gradually developed in it through the spiritual body. Can we then be called on to prove any of these: (1) that the Universal Society has in England some constitution which it lacks elsewhere and which makes it better here; (2) that the principles uniting the Universal Society with the National Society among us differ from those that unite them elsewhere, and that we are better for the difference; or (3) that what is peculiarly our national character ought to be the character of every other nation? No one who truly loves his Church or his country will make such claims for it. He will ask instead whether the Church recognizes the constitution that belongs to all mankind; whether that constitution is so recognized here as to be compatible with the distinct national constitution; what character is intended for Englishmen; and how that character may be perfected or corrupted. Put in this form, the inquiry demands no acceptance that is inconsistent with being a militant Christian. Have we lost the Universal Constitution, or any element of it? We must work by every means to recover it. Have we lost our distinct national position? We must seek it again. Are we living inconsistently with either? We must find where the evil is and begin at once the work of personal reformation. The subject I now propose to enter will divide in this way. Since we take for granted the earlier steps of our discussion, I hope it will not occupy us long.

Section I.—Do the Signs of a Universal and Spiritual Constitution exist in England?

There is no difficulty in answering this directly. If the signs are Baptism, the Creeds, Forms of Worship, the Eucharist, the Ministerial Orders and the Scriptures, no one will deny that for twelve hundred years a society has existed in England made up of these elements. Through every change in the country’s outward circumstances, its national policy and its religious opinions, a body has lived in this land that has acknowledged not one or two but all of these signs, and has acknowledged them as the conditions of its own existence.

But is this the only point to consider? Ought we not to ask whether the body that acknowledged these signs has always given them the same meaning, or what meaning it gave them at any particular time? And may there not be two bodies in the country at once, differing from each other and yet both acknowledging all these signs? How then are we to decide which of them represents the Universal Society?

I. On the first question, my answer is this. If I am asked to take a vote of the members of the English Church, now or at any time since it was founded, to learn what the majority think about these signs, I decline. It would be impossible, but even if it were possible, I would be violating every principle of this book. I have said that members of a Church continually lose sight of the grounds of their society, and that permanent institutions are given to witness against our tendency to degenerate and to let us see the Divine purpose more clearly in each age. For the same reason I protest against trying to find the principles of the English Church by weighing the opinions of its most eminent writers, since I have argued that permanent creeds and institutions protect us from the private judgments and prejudices of such writers. But if one of these signs is so far distinctively English that it can express the mind of the English Church, I am willing to test the matter by that. A Liturgy is such a sign. I have shown how truly it is a sign of a universal society, yet each nation has always had its own Liturgies, so the appeal to it is fair. But how should the appeal be made? I could read my own opinions into the Liturgy as easily as into any other book. So the fairer way to learn its meaning is to take it from others, especially from those who have attacked it. Let us try that.

1. Again and again English Dissenters have complained that our formularies assert so plainly, solemnly and habitually that a baptized man must regard himself as regenerate, a child of God, an heir of the New Covenant. They say: “It is idle to pretend that dropping a few words from your baptismal form would remove this plague-spot. If that could be done, think what a monstrous delusion you have been spreading in such solemn moments for generations. To thrust the words out privately in this nineteenth century! They ought to be cast out amid groans and confessions of sin, for having mocked God and ruined souls. And even then the spirit of them runs through all your other services. You tell the children you catechise that they are members of Christ and children of God. Every confession and prayer you ask of adults old in sin rests on the same principle: you invite them to confess and pray as children of God, with the Spirit still among them.”

Everyone knows these charges are constantly made. Are they not true? Has any defender of the Liturgy who shared the Dissenters’ theology been able to refute them? And is it not painful that we should use equivocations and double meanings while addressing the most awful prayers to Him who is Truth? So I affirm, on the authority of those most opposed to me and not from my own conclusions, that Baptismal Regeneration is the idea of our Liturgy.

But is this tied to the idea of an opus operatum? I think that has already been answered. The Dissenter sees, as everyone who thinks sees, that the whole Liturgy is built on the principle that those who use it have not lost their baptismal privileges. The sin they confess is not having owned God as Father, not having remembered His covenant, and so not having walked in His ways. They ask to be restored to a position their lives need not have contradicted, though they have. In a word, the sacrament is not believed to have given men a temporary blessing but to have admitted them to a permanent state, which is always theirs, which they are always bound to claim, and by which they will be judged.

I know there are defenders who can clear us of this charge by ingenious special pleading. They say, “All this language presumes discipline. We have lost our discipline, but we need not therefore lose our prayers.” Not lose our prayers? We had better lose anything than go on mocking God. If the want of discipline makes the prayers false, and hardly half a dozen persons in any congregation would dare say they have kept their baptismal purity, and those few are the very ones who cannot join in these prayers or in any prayer but the Pharisee’s, how can we practise such profanity because we hope some day to have a discipline that will cut off most of those who now call themselves Churchmen? And does our Liturgy give the slightest sign that restoring discipline would make these prayers truer than they are now? Why, in the Commination Service, does she not announce the doctrine of the opus operatum? Why does she not say, “You were made members of Christ once, but the privilege is gone, the blessing exhausted; you have resisted the Spirit and He no longer strives with you; recover the gift if you can by penitence and prayer”? Why does that service, like all the rest, call men to repent as children, though rebellious ones, on the ground that God wills that they turn from their wickedness and live?

2. So much for Baptism. The Liturgy’s view of the Creeds is clear from how it introduces them. They are parts of worship: acts of allegiance, declarations by the whole congregation of the Name into which each person was baptized, preparations for prayer, steps to communion. The notion of them as mere collections of dogmas is never hinted at and is refuted by the whole order of the services.

3. On the Eucharist too it is safer to cite opponents. We have been told again and again that a Real Presence is plainly implied in our Communion Service, that the words must at least give that impression to an ordinary person, that no one holding the simple Zuinglian doctrine could have written them, and that anyone who thinks that doctrine true cannot use them without pain and contradiction. Yet, though there are constant warnings about the spirit in which to receive the sacrament, about the faith and repentance needed, and about the danger of careless and unworthy treatment of such mysteries, do not the earnest exhortations to partake show that it is regarded as a common blessing, to which all have a claim, and from which it is a perilous responsibility to exclude anyone whose open sins have not excluded him? The English Dissenter sees the ordinance as the right of a few who can give an account of their feelings, experiences and change of mind, and he keeps denouncing our service for departing from the rules he follows. On one side he finds the Eucharist spoken of in language so awful that it must, he thinks, come from times of pure superstition. On the other he finds the strangest notion of it as a bond of fellowship for the whole universe. He says: “From your phrases one would think you saw this sacrament as the very opening of the kingdom of heaven, yet you treat it as the right preparation for the most vulgar, earthly employments. Sometimes you seem to think men can eat the flesh and drink the blood of the Son of Man in these elements, yet you admit to it people who would struggle to explain the most ordinary propositions of Christianity. By restricting its administration to a particular class, you seem to treat it as a Jewish sacrifice or something more wonderful still. And yet you speak of Christ’s sacrifice once made on the cross as full, sufficient and satisfactory.”

Meanwhile nobody, friend or foe, claims that any passage in this service favours the idea that Christ’s presence depends on a change in the elements. Whoever holds that idea at once becomes dissatisfied with our eucharistic service and calls it cold, heartless, dead, and so on. Likewise whoever holds the Eucharist to be a sacrifice in any sense that makes Christ’s sacrifice on the cross less complete and finished for all mankind than the strongest Lutheran or Calvinist supposes denounces our Liturgy for departing from what the Mass embodies elsewhere in the Latin Church. So by everyone’s confession our Liturgy treats the feast as the highest Christian privilege and the most complete reality. It does so not because the feast changes our Christian position, and not because it brings before us One who is habitually absent, but because it lets us enter the fullness of our Church life, that truly human and divine fellowship which Christ, by His incarnation, death and ascension, has claimed for all whom He is not ashamed to call His brethren.

4. This being so, the communion service interprets the rest of our worship. Throughout, it is the worship of a body, a family. It is open, and has been exposed, to every objection that defenders of extempore prayer raise against any form that belongs to mankind in general and not to our own nation, family and circumstances, except as we connect these with the knowledge of God and His purposes for our race. It is equally open to the objections of those who think worship cold and dead if it leads from the visible to the invisible, and if it claims the privilege of approaching, through the one Mediator, the throne of the Absolute and Infinite. It proscribes nothing, and does not say how much or how little of the sensible may help us reach what is beyond our senses. It plainly recognizes human agency and help as the appointed, ordinary channel through which the blessings of Him who was made flesh descend on His Church, and through which the Church’s prayers and praises rise to Him as one united sacrifice. But it does affirm that all sensible helps and all human agency lose their meaning and become positively evil when they are made ends in themselves, or when they weaken the belief that the whole Church is admitted into the holiest place.

5. This subject cannot be separated from ministerial orders as our ordination and consecration services present them. Part of the complaint against these has been considered already: only those ordained presbyters may administer the Eucharist or pronounce absolution. The Dissenters argue: “You may say that πρεσβύτερος (elder) and ἱερεύς (priest) are different words, and that you give the first, not the second, to your second rank of ministers. But does not refusing these offices to the lower order clearly recognize the principle, even if you do not give it a name? If your Church felt as we do about the sin of giving these names to men, would she have come so close in her acts to such an assumption? Would she habitually act on a maxim that at least gives the impression she thinks it no assumption at all?” I leave to others the answering of these arguments; to me they seem irresistible. Nor can I clear our services of the charge of formally connecting the gift of spiritual powers with ordination, and of urging her ministers to believe that the Holy Spirit is committed to them for the ministry. I do not complain of anyone who serves as a minister in our Church and yet believes he has no such power. I would no more exclude him than I would depose a magistrate who did not grasp the full powers the laws give him. He may be using what he does believe is his far more honestly than I use what I believe is mine, and he is more honest than if he merely acknowledged the words without giving them a meaning. But the words are there, and he cannot complain if I take them in their plain sense. However little I enter into their force, and however little my conduct matches them, there are very few words I have heard that I could less bear to part with, or that I more feel I must learn to understand by acting on the conviction that they are true.

When Dissenters allege that, despite contrary tendencies in some of our divines, ancient and modern, our Liturgy recognizes the Episcopate as the root of all the other orders and as containing them all, I can no more quarrel with that than with the earlier points. They seem to me to have made their case, and I find that the answers made to them amount to nothing more than awkward, if ingenious, evasions.

But where in these services is the doctrine of the vicarial powers of ministers? Where is there one hint that the Presbyter absolves or administers the Eucharist, or that the Bishop exercises his functions or ordains others, as the minister and delegate of one who is absent from his Church? Those who hold this opinion at once exclaim that our services give a cold and unsatisfactory recognition of the mighty authority of the Priest and Bishop of the New Testament. They feel it necessary that he should be clothed with other attributes, in another mystery, than any the English Church recognizes in him. The very language that offends the Dissenter as asserting a perpetually derived and renewed power, in terms too high and profane, is the language that contradicts this notion of an inherent power.

6. Lastly, the Scriptures. Here the Liturgy’s intention is remarkably clear. Scripture is adopted into our worship: the service explains the lessons and the lessons explain the service. The Bible is read partly as a continuous history, the history of God’s revelation and of the Church’s growth and expansion, and partly in connexion with communion, the New Testament epistles expounding the law of the Spirit of life and the Gospels showing the image after which the Spirit would form us. This is exactly the relation between the Scriptures and the Church that I tried to set out in the earlier part of this book. The Protestant Dissenter says we set aside the Bible, though in any one month one of our Churches reads more of it than he reads in two years in his meetings, and our reading is continuous while his is casual and arbitrary. The Romanist says we set aside the Church’s authority in interpreting Scripture, though we make it a formal and habitual part of the services, in which the mind of the Church is expressed.

II. 1. I think, then, that I have fairly answered the question about the meaning the English Church gives to the signs it shares with other Churches. The other question was how to decide between two bodies in this country, both possessing these signs, as to which may fairly call itself Catholic. If what I have said is true, this is settled too. A body that owns its connexion with the Church of all earlier ages by the bond of sacraments, creeds, worship and ministerial succession has the primâ facie marks of Catholicity. If another body standing apart from it claims to be Catholic on the same grounds, it must show its reasons for its own claim and for rejecting the first body’s. Those reasons must be the ones we have already considered. The Romish body in this country denies we are Catholic because we do not acknowledge the opus operatum in Baptism, the new creeds of Popes, Transubstantiation in the Eucharist, an intermediate agency between Christ and His members on earth, the vicarial authority of ministers, a Universal mortal Bishop, or the Church’s right to hide the Scriptures from the laity. In a word, we do not acknowledge the system which, before we reached English ground at all, appeared to us anti-Catholic. In every land the Church exists either professing this system or protesting against it. Its existence is shown not by the profession or the protest but by the Signs to which they refer. If the Romish body says that it stands in certain notions about sacraments and orders, and not in the sacraments and orders themselves, that is a practical renunciation of its claim to be a Church. We say that we protest against these notions because they are incompatible with the acknowledgment of Christ’s Spiritual and Universal kingdom.

2. But we have seen that the confessions of the different Protestant bodies, contrary to their compilers’ intentions, have greatly interfered with the simple recognition of the facts in the creed, and that the Romish confessions sanctioned by the Council of Trent and Pope Pius IV. interfere with it still more. So we must show whether anything in the Church of England corresponds to these, any addition made on its own authority to the formularies admitted by the whole Church. Until we are satisfied on this, I think we cannot rightly understand our position toward the other parts of the Church.

In the sixteenth century we, like the Protestants and Romanists on the Continent, drew up a set of dogmatic articles, which have ever since been the test of orthodoxy for those who take orders in our Church and for students in at least one of our universities. Since different systems were then in vogue, and the purpose of the various religious bodies in making confessions was to identify themselves with one of them (the Genevan body with the Calvinistic system, the Romanist bodies with the Tridentine), we must ask how far our articles identify us with any of them. One remark made about them matters here: they carefully avoid intruding on the ground of the old creeds. They do not take the living forms of the creeds; they are a set of distinct dogmatic propositions; they would be ridiculous in worship; they are not meant for most of the laity; they belong only to the student. But these observations would be worth little if the articles taught the teacher a theological system alien to the spirit of the creeds, since he would pass that system on to his hearers.

How does the case stand? We have seen that the main characteristic of the Calvinistic system is that it makes the fall of man the central point of its divinity, and treats the incarnation and all the facts that reveal the Son of God as merely growing out of the fall and made necessary by it. (If a reader asks what a Supralapsarian is, he is not someone who thinks the union of mankind with its Creator came before the fall in God’s idea. He is someone who thinks the redemption of certain individuals out of mankind is the highest end of all God’s purposes, for the sake of which the fall itself was permitted and ordained.) The spirited confession drawn up by the Scotch preachers for the Kirk in the sixteenth century is built on this principle. Its first Article is on the Trinity, its second on the Fall, and then comes the explanation of the Church or Kirk as grounded on the predestination of certain individuals in the fallen race to eternal life. No finer model of a purely Calvinistical confession exists, nor one that better shows the direct opposition between the Genevan system and the old Catholic Creeds. We have seen that the Lutheran had a very different conception: he wanted to make Christ’s incarnation the centre of all his thoughts and to use the Apostles’ Creed as his symbol, as the Augsburg confession shows. But in his eagerness to assert conscious Justification as the one great principle of divinity, he was driven back onto the Calvinist’s ground. He had to start from the evil root to explain the process of restoration. So, as I remarked before, systematic Protestantism became identical with Calvinism, until the Arminian form developed, which is little more than a contradiction of Calvinism, a denial that the will of God is the originating cause of all good in man.

Now look at our Thirty-nine Articles. The first is on the Trinity and the second on the Incarnation, and the first eight all concern truths directly connected with the being of God, His manifestations of Himself, the Scriptures as expounding them, and the Creeds as illustrating the Scriptures. When this Catholic foundation is laid, the ninth Article turns to the fall of man, and then to the questions of free-will, justification and election that occupied men’s minds in the sixteenth century. On these points the language of the Articles seems to me as definite as it could be. The Calvinistic and Lutheran principles are plainly asserted, with no hint or prophecy of Arminianism. The Romish system, wherever it opposes the Reformers’ affirmations about God’s will and man’s faith, is repudiated; that is, Romanism is rejected in Articles nine to nineteen, as Calvinism, or pure Protestantism, had been repudiated in Articles one to eight. The principles of the Reformation are asserted in the one division not as necessary qualifications but as indispensable conditions of the great Catholic truths asserted in the other. And so, whatever the cause, the Articles have been thorns in the side of those wanting an English theological system fashioned from Romanist or Calvinist materials. They have encouraged people of all sects and schools to hope that their principles are contained in them in some sense, or can be drawn from them, or at least are not positively denied. Yet no sect or school, speaking its own language and being honest, would fail to confess that there are passages it would gladly be rid of, that adding or dropping a “not” would often suit it, and that it would like, if not to remodel them, at least to add its own commentary on every occasion.

I conclude this head by remarking that if my observations on the true meaning of Quakerism, Calvinism, Lutheranism and Unitarianism are accurate, the ideas and principles of each of these bodies are expressed in the forms of our English Church; only the systems they have grafted on those ideas, which have separated them from each other, are rejected. The idea of man as constituted in the divine Word, of a Kingdom based on that constitution, of a Spirit working to bring him into conformity with it, and of a perpetual struggle with an evil and sensual nature: this is the idea of Quakerism, and it is the idea of our Liturgy in every form and service. The idea of a divine Will going before all acts of the human will, the primary source of all that is in eternity and all that becomes in time, to which everything is meant to be subject, which alone can subdue what has rebelled, and to which every creature must attribute every motion to good within him, the first direction of his thoughts, and the power to persevere: this is the idea of Calvinism, implied in all the prayers of our Litany and formally set forth in our Articles. The idea of man struggling with his own evil nature, finding in it only a bottomless pit of evil, grasping at a deliverer, and finding that his life is only in union with Him, that he is strong only in His strength and righteous only in His righteousness: this is the idea of Lutheranism, involved in all our prayers and Creeds and restated in logical terms in our Articles. The idea of a unity beneath all other unity, of a love that is the ground of all other love, of Humanity as connected with that love, regarded by it and comprehended in it: this is the idea that has hovered about the mind of the Unitarian, and that he has vainly tried to comprehend in his system of contradictions and denials. This idea is the basis of our Liturgy, our Articles, our Church.

Section II.—Does the Universal Society in England exist apart from its Civil Institutions, or in union with them?

To this question the answer is unanimous.

To this question the answer is unanimous. The English dissenter says the Church is embodied in the State; it is an Act of Parliament Church. The modern civil ruler says the Church hampers the State in everything it does: the Archbishop crowns the Sovereign, the Bishops sit as a body in Parliament, and above all most of the nation’s education is in church hands. The Romanist says the Church has no claim to be called Catholic; it is a national body. So there is no doubt that the ecclesiastical and civil institutions are united, by bonds that only violence could break. But when did the union happen? How was it brought about? Who were the parties to it? History is silent. It records no meeting of Sovereigns and Bishops to settle terms, and no date when the Church began to acknowledge the State or the State the Church. As soon as we find the Church in the land, it is doing homage to whatever civil powers ruled there. As soon as it begins to exercise its own influence, the civil power feels that influence and is shaped by it. After that we do find records of dealings between the two, each with its own representative and its own object, though no formal line defines either. But these dealings do not set up a covenant, by which the State will protect the Church or the Church will serve the State. They are attempts by each side to win back some part of its province which it thinks the other has invaded, or to seize some part which the other has held peacefully until now. They presuppose a real, though imperfectly understood, relation; no compact could have produced them, and they did nothing to create one. The Church claims the right to assign the powers and jurisdiction of its own Bishops; the State holds that Bishops, like all its subjects, must acknowledge its paramount authority. The Church claims a spiritual government wholly distinct from the civil; the State says the Church’s ministers must submit to its laws and courts like other men. Every impartial and thoughtful reader of our history feels that there is right and wrong in each claim, and that Becket and Henry must each have been contending for a principle. Our annalists insist on choosing favourites and nicknaming opponents; young readers are eager for a firm verdict on every dispute; and the so-called fair critics try both parties by the standards of their own time, condemn both, and exalt only themselves. All this may keep us from saying outright that we owe deep debts to both champions, and that a higher power was working out its ends through both. Yet we all feel it inwardly, and in one phrase or another we all express it. We also feel that the history of these struggles is, if not the history of England, its heart and centre, and that it sheds more light on the conflicts of Kings and Barons, Normans and Saxons, old orders and new, than they shed on it. Those who have learnt that politics is not contained in the theory of representation—that to understand what representation means we must first know what there is to represent—have seen that these civil and ecclesiastical disputes hold the inward secret. We must investigate it before we can trace its working on the surface and in the outward machinery of Society.

So it was before the Reformation. And what was the Reformation itself? Its opponents of both kinds say it was merely a national movement: “Henry, not Cranmer, was at the root of it; there was more politics in it than religion.” I would not use that language, since I do not understand their distinction between politics and religion. But in their meaning I believe they are right. The most obvious peculiarity of the English Reformation is that it began with the Sovereign and not with theologians. So it was not a new movement but one of a series. The Constitutions of Clarendon, made under a rebellious Sovereign, and the statutes of præmunire, passed in the time of some of the most orthodox Kings and greatest persecutors of Lollardism, had both tried to cut off correspondence with the Church of Rome. The difference under Henry VIII was simply that a large body of Bishops and clergy had been led by their religious feelings to want that correspondence ended. They felt that the English Church could not hold its own position unless it became strictly national, and gave up its subjection to a foreign bishop, which the State had always wanted it to give up.

And what has been the state of things since the Reformation? A number of bodies or sects have gradually grown up in the country, saying that the principles of Protestantism were not asserted boldly enough at our Reformation. We stopped short, they say, and kept much of the papal system that other Protestant nations threw off. The Romanists, for their part, felt that the English Reformation was more fatal to their governing maxim than the Reformation anywhere else. They had hoped men might drop a new system of opinions and take up an old one. But a Church that had affirmed the principle of nationality, and come to terms with the Sovereign of its own land, seemed utterly incorrigible. The most earnest and intelligent Jesuits who came over saw that this state of things must be changed not merely by preaching but by plotting; many of them believed the best hope for restoring papal power lay in the triumph of the sects that professed a more vehement Protestantism. Another curious point: the Puritan body, as I have said before, was essentially Calvinistic; Calvinism was the principle of its life, and the earliest Puritan movements arose from the feeling that the English Church was not founded on the Calvinistic idea. Yet we imported the anti-Calvinistic doctrine, which the Puritans later identified with popery, from one of the purely Protestant countries of the Continent. Further, we had no connexion with this Arminian system at all until a Scottish King, bred under Presbyterian preachers, sat on the throne. To him we owe a very marked change in our position. Elizabeth had troubled herself as little as possible about systems of opinion; she merely tried to assert her place as a national Sovereign. James could only look at every question as a schoolman and a pedant. He had one living practical feeling: he was disgusted with the Presbyterian preachers, whose power in practice interfered with his. But he felt no sympathy or connexion with our Church; he only wanted the Episcopalian system to prevail over the Presbyterian. That system, with all that belonged to it, was to be established in Scotland and maintained here by the efforts of the State. So in both countries the feeling was called forth that there is a spiritual power distinct from, and higher than, the mere State power. In Scotland this spiritual feeling joined with national feeling, and the people revolted against a prelacy imposed on them by England. In England the mixing of spiritual with national feelings in the Puritan produced strange anomalies. The group in the Commons most sympathetic to Puritanism was busy defending the old forms of the national constitution against the royal prerogative. The Puritan clergy were raising their voices against old national and ecclesiastical forms and defending the rights of the spiritual man. Mixed with these claims, one can see from the first a desire for a more formal and systematic divinity than England had ever had. At length they triumph, and have to realise as best they can three aims: the liberty of the subject, asserted by the Long Parliament; the superiority of Christians to outward forms, maintained by their preachers against Laud and the Bishops; and the all-importance of one particular theological system. The first attempt ended in a military despotism. The second led to the rise and independence of the sects that revolted from stern Presbyterian government and sought freedom of conscience. The last was embodied in the deliberations, decrees, catechisms and committees of triers of the Westminster Assembly. It is a grievous thing that English Churchmen, from prejudice and partiality, refuse to study this period simply and fairly, from all sides, trying to do justice to the feelings of every party. For when it is so studied, and not twisted into an apology for some ecclesiastical hero or a sentence against his opponents, it shows the essential relation between spiritual and civil life. It shows too that no effort of ours, however unfriendly or uncomfortable we make that relation, can destroy it.

The Westminster Assembly did its best to establish uniformity of opinion, and left its ministers free only in modes of worship. The opposite principle had been the one recognised in England until then: the bond of national fellowship was supposed to be worship, and men with books and leisure might occupy themselves with the study of opinions. I do not know how far the Episcopal clergy at the Restoration saw that this was the question between them and their opponents; perhaps they thought it only a question whether the nation should adopt a more or less comprehensive system. If so, their old habits were stronger than their theories. The State felt it could not trouble itself about shades of opinion, whereas old forms of worship were practical and general, and One was over us who saw further than statesmen or churchmen. At all events, this was the result: the Act of Uniformity in Worship took the place of the efforts at dogmatic uniformity that belonged to the genius of Presbyterianism. Its immediate effect was to separate the Puritan clergy from what was now again recognised as the national Church. Then came various stupid efforts by the State to silence them or force them into union, mixed with royal experiments at a general liberty of conscience that would include Romanist as well as Protestant Dissenters. Resistance to these shows the strong sense of the people and Parliament that Romanism was something anti-national. That feeling was strong in the seven Bishops who refused to read James’s declaration. They believed the King’s act was suicidal, as it proved. Several of them, however, could not follow the principle to what seems to me its legitimate consequence: that when the King committed his act of legal suicide by deserting the country, he lay under a sentence of deposition from God Himself, for violating the covenant by which he held his power. The Convention Parliament took that pious view, and asked not whom they might choose by their own power or the people’s will, but who seemed designated to the office by the providence of God. (He refers us here to the celebrated passage in Burke’s Reflections, replying to Dr. Price’s sermon.) At no time, I think, was the religious character of the English State more clearly asserted than then, and at no time was it more important to assert it. For a change was beginning in the habits and feelings of men, here as elsewhere, which I mentioned in Part I: the change from seeing government as grounded in deep, mysterious principles to seeing it as the result of mere commercial arrangements, some imaginary artificial compact. I shall remark shortly how mischievous this change has been to the Church and nation of England. But the notion that it has led to any legislative Acts that formally or virtually violate the union of the ecclesiastical and civil bodies could only have grown up through this very habit of mind. We have supposed the Church and State to be tied together by some material outward terms of agreement. We do not know what they are; perhaps that the State shall recognise no subjects who are not Churchmen, which is to say that it should ignore facts. If so, they might be violated by Acts of toleration, repeals of test laws, and emancipation of Romanists. I give no opinion on the policy of any of these measures. Some may have been inexpedient, and all were defended as well as attacked with many feeble and imperfect arguments. But I do think it takes something far deeper and subtler than such measures to destroy a union which no human contrivance cemented, and which exists in the very nature of things. By carelessness, ignorance, faithlessness and immorality we may undermine our national life, and it is always exposed to these perils. But when the Acts of our legislators are evil, they are generally only reflexes of something evil in the national mind, which legislators cannot correct. And generally they are better than could be expected from the tempers of those who passed them, or of us who criticise them. Often even their errors and mischievous consequences may teach us, and may profit us far more than success in opposing them would have done. And so I am led from this subject to the one I proposed to consider next.

Section III.—What is the Form of Character which belongs especially to Englishmen? To what kind of Depravation is it liable?

From what I have said already, the reader will see that I think men’s first thoughts on this subject are well founded. Foreigners complain that we English are mere politicians, and we groan that we are devoted exclusively to politics. The proofs are many. The most striking come from men who are determined to escape the national disease and become artists, philosophers, mystics, anything but politicians. Watch them, and you will see they cannot realise the dream. Some speculation about the organisation of society, or some practical effort to remodel it, keeps mixing with their serene contemplations. They grow fierce and restless in the very effort to avoid the fever they suppose inherent in the English character, and which they know is in themselves. Other cases confirm the point still more. I have known people with no practical talent at all, whose attempts at action were ludicrously and painfully abortive. If they tried to realise some fine conception, they either made it contemptible by failing or fell back into the old ruts they were struggling to leave. Yet these Englishmen, whose gifts, if they have any, are the most un-English, feel as compelled as their countrymen to be political and practical. They cannot think unless they can somehow connect their thoughts with action. Despairing of acting themselves, they try to point out a method of action to others, aspiring only to the fame of the whetstone, “which can make iron sharp, though itself unable to cut.”

These arguments are decisive, but I think there are more cheering ones. Why do we turn to the literature of Queen Elizabeth’s reign as the one that best represents our national genius and shows what we are capable of? Because in every department it was more historical and more political than it has been since. Our drama draws its highest inspiration from the old records of our national life. Even the allegorical poet, the singer of “Fairy Land,” had to weave a history of Prince Arthur into his twelve moral virtues and the battles of the inner man, and to blur the image of Gloriana with that of Queen Elizabeth. For all their dreams of poetry, scholarship and philosophy, the deepest and most earnest thoughts of Sidney and Raleigh were about policy and politics. And what nation can show profounder works of exegetical or dogmatic divinity than ours? Who can keep pace with our countrymen when they turn their minds to the laws by which God has formed heavenly, human and natural creatures?

Hooker’s work is the highest specimen of a class. Considering it, and the life and character of the man who wrote it, I think we must feel that the habit of mind God has given us offers very little excuse for any lack of meekness and gentleness, of superiority to the low notions and canons of this world, or of converse with the hierarchies of heaven. I do not want to exalt this form of character above every other. I cannot tell whether it is better or worse than the French or German. I know only that it is ours, and that it can expand into what is most noble or sink into what is most base.

We ought to look at it in both conditions, so that we do not separate hope from humiliation, so that we know both our responsibilities and our temptations, and so that we can honour the good when it is mixed with the evil nearest to it. We need that charity in every part of our history. One cannot help seeing in the English Reformers of the sixteenth century a tendency to a kind of diplomacy one does not like to see in holy men, and it is easy to represent it as pervading their whole character and explaining their acts. Soon afterwards you find them suffering with a constancy that their detractors call worthy of heroes, but that we have been used to call worthy of martyrs. (What separates a hero from a martyr? I would say the hero feels he acts by some power of his own, while the martyr feels he depends on the strength that is perfected in weakness. We may safely appeal to the Reformers’ discourses and letters in prison to decide which feeling is more characteristic of them.) Their political bias did not, I think, lower the tone of their minds, tie them more helplessly to outward things, or weaken their faith in the invisible. Its main effect was to make them think of Christ’s Church as a Kingdom rather than a system. In the dust and bustle of affairs, their conviction that this kingdom was a reality and not a metaphor may have led them to forget that it is the type of all kingdoms and is not moulded on the maxims of any, even those that confess it and do it homage. But in silence and suffering this thought gave their faith a fixedness and substance that even the most devout schoolmen seldom reach. They knew they believed in a Person. In the hour of trial and death they looked directly to Him, not to any dogma or system of dogmas, for strength and comfort.

That this way of thinking about the Church is eminently English became evident in the civil wars. It characterised every class of thinkers. It was at first less marked among the Episcopalians, where one would most expect it, because the systematic tendency had become prevalent through the influence of James I., and Laud especially seems to have caught it. His faults were far more those of a schoolmaster or a collegian (Mr. Carlyle makes this remark in his Lectures on Hero Worship) than of an arrogant, usurping politician. They made him unable to deal with the energetic national impulses of the time. But the sense of the Church as a kingdom returned to the Episcopalians in their humiliation. It prevailed above every other view in Jeremy Taylor. Despite his learning and his fondness for casuistry, he could not bear to think of Christianity as a system. He saw it as a life, but a life joined to an order and lived in the dependence a subject owes his sovereign rather than a pupil owes his master. So one can trace a curious sympathy between him and the most extreme mystics and spiritualists of that age. All spoke of a divine kingdom, and none could be content with language that did not convey it or acts that did not try to realise it. Even Milton, who was like a star and dwelt apart, was dreaming of a polity in his last age as in his first. In his commonwealth all men might be kings and priests, but they were to be kings and priests, not professors and doctors.

But we must look at the other side of the picture. When the feeling of spiritual life and government decayed, as it did in the later seventeenth and early eighteenth century, imagine the effect on a people whose political feelings were their deepest. Our literature could not separate itself from our social life. It was mere mockery when it tried to retreat into some Arcadian world. It had always been real and homely, and had to remain so. But if all realities had become conventions, and what was homely had become base, we can understand the limits of our best literature and the fearful debasement of our worst between the English Revolution and the French. The degradation of our professed statesmen, the loss of all high ends in their policy, and the maxims and practices that made Sir Robert Walpole’s name and administration immortal are equally explicable. Still there were signs of English strength and of the direction it naturally takes. In the physical world men busied themselves either with the mechanism of actual things or with God’s laws and order. Either study suited the truest and noblest part of our character, and here there were true and worthy results.

But the commercial activity and scientific discoveries of this age were gradually gathering an immense population into our cities. Who cared for these people? The Church had some prelates of high and even comprehensive views, many humble and sincere country pastors, and many men able to think vigorously about the moral constitution of individuals and society. In general, however, its habit of mind was well expressed in Warburton’s theory of the alliance of Church and State, and in the practice of sending bishops to Ireland to support the English interest. It seemed to have the means of influencing the aristocracy, but the aristocracy was commonly infidel. Through its lesser members it should have reached the trading classes, but they were mostly under the influence of dissent. It seemed unaware that a new class of poor men was coming into existence. Members of the National Church were undoubtedly the first to go out and evangelise the mining and manufacturing districts, but the Church’s rulers regarded them with anything but sympathy and took no pains to give them a right direction. Quiet country clergy disliked them because they opposed the order and regularity those clergy had always associated with religion. Others hated them because they appealed to feelings that were dormant in themselves but found alive in their flocks. Then came the French Revolution, with its terrors and warnings. The clergy began to feel less like parts of an obsolete machine existing for some unintelligible purpose, and more necessary to the commonwealth. The aristocracy began to acknowledge them in that character. Its scepticism vanished, and it spoke respectfully of religion and its teachers as exerting the influences of fear and hope that alone could make property and government secure. This was the new tone made popular by the character and patronage of George III. and the dread of French disorganisation. It was not a very elevated tone. While the war lasted it was mixed with much that was generous and patriotic among upper-class laymen, and the clergy who shared it became active magistrates, careful of their domestic and relative duties, and zealous in defending what seemed to them old and English. Joined to these useful dispositions came the following:

  • a tendency to keep up customs simply because they existed and could claim some moderate prescription;
  • acquiescence in the maxims of society even when they seemed to conflict with higher morality;
  • great impatience of enthusiasm and mysticism, and of all that cannot at once be brought under existing convention or obvious expediency;
  • suspicion of any great effort of active virtue and self-sacrifice;
  • a feeling that the Church must sympathize with the aristocracy and overlook its sins to preserve good order among the people;
  • a strong sense of what subjects owe their rulers, with no matching sense of what rulers owe their subjects;
  • an inclination to assert clergymen’s privileges chiefly by treating it as rude for anyone to voice infidel notions in their presence;
  • great anxiety for State encouragement of religion, on the ground that otherwise it would not thrive or win fashion and the world’s opinion;
  • a vehement dislike of dissenters, as disturbing the quiet and regularity of society and bringing vulgarity into religion;
  • anger and restlessness at any new doubt about the English Church or Christianity that could not be removed at once by the arguments for Establishments in Paley’s Moral Philosophy and for the Gospel in his Evidences.

The spirit of this State Churchmanship was plainly the spirit of an age of our national Church, not of the Church itself. The Church continued to express itself in the Liturgy, and, when it needed dogmatic language, in the Articles. The younger and more active members soon became aware of the contradiction. They began to look for some System that would be a refuge from the dreariness of political Anglicanism. What they have found is our next inquiry.
