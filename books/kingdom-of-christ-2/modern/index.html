The Kingdom of Christ

Volume II

The Kingdom of Christ, Volume 2 (Fourth Edition), by Frederick Denison Maurice, restated paragraph by paragraph in modern English. This paraphrase follows the order and content of the original without adding interpretation. Drafted with claude-sonnet-5-5; reviewed by a human editor.

Contents

The one-line summaries are guides drafted with the help of AI, not Maurice’s words.

  1. Part II.— (Continued.) of the Catholic Church and the Romish System

    Baptism’s promise of a universal, spiritual family is confirmed by the Apostles’ and Nicene Creeds, recited unchanged by peasant and philosopher for sixteen centuries, against Quaker, Protestant, Rationalist, and Romanist accounts of doctrine and tradition.

  2. Section III.—Forms of Worship

    Prayed unchanged across nations and centuries, the Church’s ancient liturgies prove that worship is the common speech of one family, not the private impulse Quakers require or the extempore prayer Protestants prefer.

  3. Section IV.—The Eucharist

    The Eucharist, kept for eighteen centuries by every rank and nation, is a sacrificial feast where Christ’s finished sacrifice is truly present, not a bare memorial or a change in the elements.

  4. Section V.—The Ministry

    An unbroken succession of bishops, preserved despite Christendom’s divisions, embodies Christ’s headship and absolving power, a claim the Quaker reduces to inward impulse, the Presbyterian denies, and the Romanist turns into a vicar’s authority.

  5. Section VI.—The Scriptures

    Scripture records a real, developing kingdom, so its canon, its inspired but undictated language, and its miracles all confirm one order, against rationalist dissection and the Romanist habit of hiding the Bible from the laity.

  6. Chapter V. On the Relations of the Church with National Bodies

    Oaths, marriage, property, and just war rest on a national order visible already in the Ten Commandments and in pagan Greece and Rome, carried forward into the nations of Christian Europe.

  7. Section II.—The Quaker

    The Sermon on the Mount fulfils rather than abolishes judicial oaths and just retribution, answering the Quaker’s case against war, oaths, and a paid clergy.

  8. Section II.—The Pure Theocratist — Section III.—The Separatist

    The Covenanter and Nonjuror would have the nation punish heresy by the sword as ancient Israel did, while the Separatist would keep Church and State wholly apart, but each misreads their true relation.

  9. Section IV.—The Patrician

    Those who idealize the early Church’s first centuries as a golden age of discipline and unity forget its persecutions, heresies, and corruption, so national churches mark not decline but further providence.

  10. Section V.—The Modern Statesman — Section VI.—The Modern Interpreters of Prophecy

    A statesman who claims sole authority over education, fearing the Church more than divided sects, cannot properly nurture family life, science, art, or morality, as a national Church can; and restoring the Jews would testify to the sanctity of nations without displacing the universal Church, as Christ’s return will unveil rather than replace the kingdom already here.

  11. The Romish System

    Romanism, the enemy of nations as it was the corrupter of creeds and sacraments, calls for reform, not abandonment: whoever stays in the Church of his fathers and holds it to be a Church will be delivered from popery.

  12. Part III. The English Church and the Systems Which Divide it

    Read through its Liturgy and Articles, the English Church holds Catholic, Calvinist, Lutheran, and Quaker truths together without their rival systems, its bond with the nation forged by history rather than contract.

  13. Chapter II. Modern English Systems

    Liberal, Evangelical, and Catholic parties each hold a truth that collapses into its opposite once made a system, so a national Church free of party spirit can better serve dissenters, the poor, and the colonies.

  14. Note on the Athanasian Creed

    The Creed’s language of equality and subordination completes rather than contradicts Nicene doctrine, and its damnatory clauses warn against a sin anyone can commit, not license to judge others.
