Note on the Athanasian Creed

The Creed’s language of equality and subordination completes rather than contradicts Nicene doctrine, and its damnatory clauses warn against a sin anyone can commit, not license to judge others.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

In my article on Creeds I spoke of only two, and a reader may suppose I have some objection to the third. If I had objections, I would have stated them openly rather than leave them to be inferred. Admitting them would take little courage. Fewer people reject this Creed than in the last century, but those who do are still many, and they are respected for their learning, piety, and influence. Its strongest defenders will find so much to fault in my opinions on other subjects that I would hardly win them over by agreeing with them on this one.

I left the Athanasian Creed out only because it had no bearing on my subject. Its opening formula is not “I believe” but “Whoever wishes.” It has never been connected with Baptism. No Church has used it except as an occasional service. Its antiquity, though venerable, is certainly less than that of the others. So its merits and defects rest on different ground from theirs. I will now explain why the usual objections seem to me to carry little weight, and where I think its value lies.

There are chiefly two complaints. 1. It is said not to agree with the Nicene Creed, which so clearly asserts the Son’s subordination to the Father. 2. It is said to contradict the command “Judge not, that ye be not judged.” If I thought either charge true, no Church’s authority could make me use this formulary. But the more I have considered them, the less I have found in either.

1. The passage said to conflict with the Nicene doctrine is the one declaring that Christ is equal to the Father in His Godhead and inferior to the Father in His Manhood. The objection runs that this ties the Son’s subordination to His human nature. According to the Nicene Creed, as Bishop Bull has finely developed it, the subjection shown in our Lord’s acts on earth belonged to the very idea of His Being. The flesh He took could not change the law of His existence; it was the medium through which that law was shown. I do not doubt this. “Not I, but the Father” seems to me the key to the mystery of our Lord’s self-sacrifice, that is, of His innermost life. How can I take it as anything but the expression of His own Personality? It follows that the fulness of the Godhead was in the man Christ, that He was perfect God. Indeed the objection itself assumes that His acts as a man would not fully exhibit Himself if they belonged only to an assumed nature. But suppose we need to express two truths: His perfect and complete Godhead, and His subordination as Son to the Father, each necessary to the other and meaningless in practice without it. Why may we not look to the union of humanity with Divinity as what supplies the language for both? The idea of subordination, apart from any inequality, lies in the very nature of the Godhead. It is brought out and expressed through the inferiority of Manhood to Godhead. The Sonship of Christ is the type and ground of the relation of the human to the divine. What could be more complete and beautiful than the Old Church’s language on this?

Do we not feel that if we had only the Nicene Creed, and no new heresy had called for another exposition, we would be in danger of losing a truth because we had only one imperfect form of language to unfold it? The Apostles’ Creed without the Nicene would lead us to think only of the relation of the Divine Being to us. In the same way, the Nicene Creed without the Athanasian would lead us to think merely of divine relations, forgetting that an absolute ground shows itself in them and through them.

2. The charge of uncharitableness is far more popular and easier to grasp, so I may meet it in a popular way. We are commonly asked: “You may explain away these clauses with ingenious sophistries in your study, but when you read the Creed to the people in church, do you not feel you are not speaking the words, or acting in the spirit, you would wish when you read Matthew chapter seven or First Corinthians chapter thirteen?” I can answer only for myself, though I doubt not hundreds could say the same with a clear conscience. To the best of my knowledge, reading this Creed has never tempted me, however I may have been tempted at other times, to one hard thought about any man, living or dead. I do not think the Creed calls on me to think such thoughts. Its awful language forbids it. I dare not ask who has committed the fearful sin it denounces, of “confounding the Persons and dividing the Substance.” It may not be the man who has used the most confused and heretical expressions, nor the man the Church thinks most self-willed and refractory. It may be the man resting most contentedly in his orthodoxy. It may be myself. Have I not a witness within that every wrong act or thought of mine, so far as it dulled my sense of the difference between truth and falsehood, right and wrong, was of the nature of “Confounding the Persons,” and brought me in danger of it? And that every self-willed, unkind, or schismatical act or thought was of the nature of “Dividing the Substance,” and tended to bring me into it? For this Creed takes me into another region from words, names, and forms of the intellect. It uses them to correct the abuses they have produced, and as signs to show me the way to deeper truths and principles. It is my own fault if I stay in the outer region and do not let the Church guide me into the inner circle. It is my own fault if I do not warn others and myself of the connexion between eternal truths and principles and that doing good or doing evil, to which, as the Creed’s last articles declare, eternal life or punishment are attached.

But why keep this Creed, when some may misuse it to condemn their neighbours instead of to their own and others’ good? Because if I parted with it, I think I would not help charity and would greatly injure truth. The Old Church’s language may sound stronger and fiercer than ours, but it rests on the words “This is life eternal, that they may know Thee the only true God.” The bottomless pit the fathers really dreaded was Atheism, the state of the human spirit left without God. I believe the more we return to this idea, the more inward charity we shall have, the better we shall understand our glory and our perils, the more we shall have common hopes and objects, and the freer we shall be from vulgar selfish desires and superstitious fears. I could not give up this Creed without saying that its meaning and principle belonged less to our time than to former times. I believe they belong more to ours than to any. For this is the question in debate now. Are we to behold the unity, grounded most deeply in that name of God which this Creed speaks of, informing all society and all nature? Or are we to see everything broken, divided, and unharmonised, with a dark form of self-love, embodied in some visible tyranny, above us, and a gulf of utter nothingness beneath?
