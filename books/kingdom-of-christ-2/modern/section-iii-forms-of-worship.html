Section III.—Forms of Worship

Prayed unchanged across nations and centuries, the Church’s ancient liturgies prove that worship is the common speech of one family, not the private impulse Quakers require or the extempore prayer Protestants prefer.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Every traveller will tell you how much modes of worship differ from country to country; they change, he says, with every degree of latitude. Within one district he sees certain practices persist, along with respect for the traditions that produced them. Still, he notes that time leaves its mark almost as plainly as place does. If an invasion or some other lucky accident brings a more cultivated people to bear on a lesser one, the old customs become more reasonable. As that cultivation spreads, unless one class manages to confine it within certain limits, doubt about old traditions becomes general. If a people is left to itself, on the other hand, its mind grows coarser every day, and the old superstitions lose every trace of whatever meaning and worth they once had.

These remarks, familiar to every modern reader, come from true observation, and they are not limited to Pagan or Mahometan countries. I believe that where Christianity is found, the influence of place and period is far more noticeable than anywhere else. The nationalities of modern Europe are more sharply marked than those of the rest of the world at any time, and it is almost foolish to say that one century differs more from another in Europe than in the East. Equally certain, as liberal writers keep telling us, is that these national differences and social changes affect religious opinion very strikingly, with nothing like it in China or Hindostan.

If so, is it not remarkable that certain forms of worship, actual worship, have lasted through all the revolutions of Christendom? They have defied national customs and languages, and have held their ground against every variety of opinion on human and divine subjects.

Is it not strange, for example, that we in England in the nineteenth century use forms of prayer written by Greeks in the third and fourth centuries? Indeed the whole plan of our liturgy, from beginning to end, was taught us by nations from whom we are divided in taste, feeling, political institutions, stage of civilisation and religious antipathies. This covers the assigning of particular services to particular seasons, the use of Psalms, the ascriptions, and the acts of confession, thanksgiving and adoration. Think of our northern character, our cloudy skies, our Teutonic independence, our vehement nationality. Then remember that every week of the year, and perhaps every day, at the times we hold most solemn, we use words we owe to Hebrews, Greeks and Latins, and that in these words the simple folk of England, for all their narrow notions and local customs, find comfort and delight.

Now suppose Baptism means that we are brought into God’s family, and so can glorify his name with one mind and one mouth. Suppose the creed teaches us, as children of that family, to acknowledge that name together and singly, and to know how it relates to us. Then acts of worship ought to be, above all acts, those that most belong to our position and in which our fellowship is most fully realised. The more we read the Bible and enter into its sense, the more this feeling must grow in us. The Bible teaches two texts: all division comes through idolatry, and all union through adoration of the one living and true God. It illustrates and presses them from the record of the dispersion at Babel, where men built a tower with its top in heaven for the worship of natural things, down to Pentecost, when the little band of Apostles in the temple were heard by the multitudes, each in his own tongue, magnifying God. If anything is to break down the barriers of space and time, it must be the worship of him who is, and was, and is to come, whom the heaven of heavens cannot contain and whose dwelling is with the humble and contrite heart. If anything is to reconcile those whom these accidents of mortality divide, this must be the means. I have confessed that men have turned worship to the opposite use, making it the slave of their circumstances, the badge of their divisions, the instrument of their hatred. The question is whether anything in the world witnesses against this tendency, and whether God has given us any sign that these separations come from our choice, not his will. I say that these forms of worship, preserved through so many generations and fitted to every locality, are such a sign. Using them, I have a right to believe that the blessings of Pentecost were given once and never withdrawn, and that in the deepest and most practical sense there is a community which differences of tongue and the succession of ages cannot break.

Objections.— The Quaker.

The Quaker protests vehemently against this conclusion. Forms of worship, he says, are not only no sign of a spiritual commonwealth; they are incompatible with it. The Spirit blows where it wishes. Prayer is given by the Spirit, and by prepared forms we make it the utterance of man’s will and reason.

That last assertion is perfectly true. We do make prayer the utterance of man’s will and reason, and we consider it their highest and most perfect utterance, the one in which alone they fully realise themselves. The logic of terms and definitions cannot tell us what the human Will is. It can express only half its meaning, because it describes the will by its intrinsic properties, whereas the will’s essential character is that it is always going out of itself. Logic describes it at rest, but it only is while it acts. In prayer, however, we know truly what the will is. Prayer shows its inmost nature and proves that the greatest apparent contradiction in language is the greatest reality: the will gives itself up in order to be itself, and dies in order to live. By acknowledging another will as the only will, it gains its own freedom, just as by trying to have a being of its own it becomes a slave. “Father, not my will, but thine.” Where do we see the human will in such perfection, with such distinctness of life and power, as in those awful words? The same holds for the faculty that sees as for the one that determines, for the seat of wisdom as for the source of action. Reason knows itself only when it forgets itself, and sees only when the sense of sight is lost in its object. So Reason too finds its deepest meaning and expression in worship.

But do we therefore deny that the Spirit of God is the author of prayer, or, in Barclay’s words, deny that all prayer is spurious which does not proceed from Him? No; in affirming the one we affirm the other. We believe the Spirit of God is the only awakener of the spirit in man, and that will and reason not called forth by Him remain helpless victims of nature and sense. Unless He directs these powers, they only serve what they are meant to rule and tighten the bondage they are meant to break. We believe that anyone in past ages, heathen or Jewish, who used them rightly was taught to do so, and to the degree that he did so, confessed the inspiration. But we can exercise them as heathens and even Jews could not, because we know that a living Person actuates and governs them, and we know what kind of Person He is: whose Spirit He is, from whom He proceeds, with whom He dwells. This knowledge is far deeper and more awful than that of someone who merely felt himself the subject of an inspiration. Being so deep, it is incompatible with excitement, with distortions of manner or voice, and with the notion that we are unconscious animal utterers of imparted sounds, rather than living, conscious, voluntary, rational agents of One who, promising the Spirit, said: “Henceforth I call you not servants, but I have called you friends, for the servant knoweth not what his Lord doeth; but whatsoever I have heard and learned of the Father, I have made known unto you.” We believe we must attribute every act of our minds, affections and will to this Spirit. If the purpose we direct them to is wrong, the gift and power are still His and only the purpose ours; if it is right, we shall own that He is the author of that too. We also believe that every operation in nature, the growth of every tree and the budding of every flower, is to be referred to Him who first moved upon the face of the waters. But we do not call this a spiritual influence, because, though wrought by a spiritual Being, it is wrought upon unspiritual subjects, on things and not persons.

These are some inferences that follow directly from Baptism as a new birth and the Creed as the proper act of the new-born creature. What is born of the Spirit is spirit; it seeks its spiritual home and Father, refers its movements to His inspiration, and so attains its own distinctness and freedom. What is born of the flesh is flesh; it seeks the earth it came from and acknowledges no other attraction. The contradiction of humanity is when the human spirit glorifies itself and, as the necessary punishment of that sin, abdicates its own throne and sinks into the slave of the flesh, infecting it with its own sin. The glory of humanity is when the human spirit renounces itself and, as its reward, gains knowledge of Him from whom it came, victory over the flesh, and power to communicate its own life to it.

The Quaker’s objection, that forms of prayer proceed from man’s reason and will and not from the Divine Spirit, therefore denies the very nature and possibility of prayer. And the denial has been of a very practical kind. He admits that prayer is necessary, both a sign of moral health and a means of producing it, yet he dares not pray unless he has a sensible impulse urging him. I will not dwell on the Quaker use of “sensible,” though it seems to me significant: those who most abhor appeals to the senses in worship, and think the sights and sounds God has filled the universe with cannot be redeemed to His service, still grossly confuse impressions on the spirit with impressions on the senses. The important point is that the idea of life as a conflict, which one would think always present to Fox’s mind, is thereby set at naught. If the Quakers understood that the true will and real self is always at war with the merely sensible impulse, they would surely have believed that the natural man’s reluctance toward an act we know to be good and feel to be necessary is one of the best proofs that the Divine Spirit prompts it. But the truth is that the idea of a constant, living, personal presence has practically deserted those who once seemed to make it the whole of their religion. What they have kept is almost only the notion of an influence, an inspiration, visiting certain persons at certain times, which Christianity shares with Paganism. Is it wonderful, then, that they cannot understand how the Spirit should have taught men in distant generations to express their deepest wants in the same words, or how through these words they should have secret and awful communion with each other and with the Most High? If so, what better proof do I need that these forms are one of the clear and indispensable signs of a spiritual and universal fellowship?

The Pure Protestant.

The pure Protestants who have rejected Liturgies have little sympathy with the Quaker’s objections to them. They do not deny that prayer, like any religious act, is voluntary. Because it is voluntary, they say, forms are an intolerable bondage. “Each man should be able to express his own wants in his own way. In his chamber each man does, or should, lay his feelings and wishes bare before God. That is the rule and standard of prayer in our Lord’s words, ‘When thou prayest, enter into thy closet.’ But the minister is, or should be, chosen by the congregation. He knows the circumstances of its members, and they look up to him as fit to preach the Gospel to them. So it is right that he should pray for himself and them as the feeling of the moment suggests. This is probably a preparation for his sermon, and so full of earnestness and unction. Forms of prayer are unsuited to both purposes. They cannot be adapted to changing circumstances or connected with the feelings of pastor or people. They are impositions from another age, affronting to the understanding and painful to the conscience of those who use them.”

This statement assumes that prayer is, in its first notion, individual. A particular man wants certain blessings, so he asks them of Him who he believes can give them. Many people find this self-evident, and think whoever doubts it an enemy of common sense. But I believe it contradicts the experience of every religious man. He soon learns that passionate eagerness to get some good thing for himself, whether fine weather for his crops or the salvation of his soul, is no help to prayer but the greatest hindrance to it. Explain it as you will, it is a fact. People of different sentiments confess it in different language, and it keeps presenting itself to those who visit dying beds. The selfish object floats before our minds, plainly if it is an earthly one, in hazy and rather terrifying images if it is invisible and unknown. But the object, He to whom we pray, is far off, and we hardly discern Him at all. We think of Him as a Being who can inflict evil and may choose to bless. Or if childhood teaching has given us better knowledge, self-seeking perverts it, and we rise feeling that our sacrifice is not accepted: “we are very wroth, and our countenance falls.” How, then, is this natural kind of prayer ever changed? The pure Protestant appeals to our Lord’s words, “When thou enterest into thy closet.” Then say, “Our Father which art in heaven, hallowed be thy name.” What wonderful teaching! It does not show how the selfishness of the closet may be carried into the temple. It shows how the breadth and universality of the temple may be reached in the closet.

When you are most alone, if you would pray, you must still be in the midst of a family. You must call on a Father, and must say not my but our. Do you want to be very holy? That must not be your petition; you must say, “Hallowed be thy name.” Do you want an assurance of heaven for yourself? You must say, “Thy kingdom come.” Do you want some favourite project accomplished? You must seek it as “Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven.” Do you need bodily or spiritual supplies? You must desire the same for all your brethren: “Give us this day our daily bread.” Do you want forgiveness for your own sins? The prayer is still “Forgive us our trespasses,” and the gift is received only as it is passed on, “as we forgive them that trespass against us.” Do you feel your fellow-creatures are your tempters? You must still acknowledge their temptations and yours to be the same. You must ask that they not be led into the very temptations they cause, or you will be their tempter as well as your own. All this is because the evil we pray to be delivered from is a common evil, the same in root and principle though it takes countless forms. It is the evil of selfishness and individuality, which we are inclined to make our very prayers a means of serving. It will encompass and possess us unless we learn to join in the ascription: “Thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory.”

I do not mean that many objectors to forms have not kept these truths and entered into them heart and soul. But I maintain that just so far as they have, the reason for their complaint ceases. If individual prayer is not the highest and most essential prayer, but rather no prayer at all, then the prayer of the congregation is not a sum of individual prayers. It is the prayer of a body, each member of which professes to have renounced his selfish position in order to come, as one of a family, to seek the Father of it.

In what sense, then, can extempore utterances be said to declare our wants best? Which wants? Do the congregation feel they have sinned and wish to confess? Is that a local feeling belonging to one set of circumstances or one period? Or is it a human feeling, belonging to men as men? “But each man has his own particular sin, his own burden, of which he himself is conscious.” Undoubtedly. But is not his sin and burden just this, that he has chosen a scheme of his own, followed his own tastes and inclinations, and so forgotten his Father in heaven and his brethren on earth? Does not every particular sin spring from this root? And does not this explain the individual character of sin and the personal responsibility for it, on which so much stress is, rightly, laid? The load lies on each man’s separate conscience. It is the nature and law of conscience to single out each man, sever him from his fellow, and make him feel that the whole universe’s share in his guilt does not lessen it for him. But conscience reproves us for this very thing, for choosing to be divided when we were meant to be one. It has reproved men for this since Adam’s fall. Ever since Christ died that we might all be one, as He is with the Father, it has taught every Christian that this is emphatically and most awfully his sin. So there seems no reason why one generation’s language in confessing this sin should not be the language of all. There is no reason why it should not be. It would be the greatest blessing if it could be, because the sense of sonship and brotherhood would be realised and revived in the very act of acknowledging disobedience and selfishness.

Or does some member of the congregation want to give thanks for a blessing given to him in particular? Must his feeling be local and temporary because the occasion is? If it is, does it not cease to be a true feeling? If the particular blessing does not enlarge his heart and draw him to contemplate other blessings, above all the common and permanent ones, which are the greatest though perhaps noticed only when they are taken away or some startling novelty draws attention to them, then God’s purpose in giving it is not accomplished. The man has not really profited by it. If he has, his feelings become human feelings. They do not need a specific, self-chosen expression. He can find them in the Psalms of David, and utter them in the language of Christian men of other climes and periods. He can give thanks for creation, preservation and redemption, and for gifts that let him enjoy this life and another, given to his race as well as to himself. He can ask that they, as well as he, may have above all other good things a thankful spirit. His own special mercies will then be understood and appreciated.

Or does a member of the congregation long for some means, not of declaring his own sins or thankfulness, but of praising the name of God and glorifying Him for His great glory? Is that a specific, local, temporary, individual emotion? Can it have a specific, local, temporary, individual expression? Is it too humiliating, too limiting for the largeness of a modern intellect to use the words of other days and say, “We praise Thee, O God; we acknowledge Thee to be the Lord!” or “Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Ghost: As it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end”?

Or, lastly, does the same member feel his need of spiritual and temporal mercies and want to ask for them? The Lord’s prayer showed how he should ask, even alone in his chamber. He must not look for them as meant for him otherwise than as a child of a Father, one of many brethren. If they are temporal, food and clothing, does he dare to seek them in order to keep them to himself? Then his prayer becomes a sin. If they are spiritual, the blessing itself is closer communion with his Father and wider communion with the family. Must he limit these by the notions and phrases of his own time? Is it a great hardship and bondage to be obliged to use a more general language, which one would think a more becoming one?

Someone may say: “Every prayer was composed in some age. Why suppose that prayers handed down from another time have the qualities you attribute to prayer more than those composed in our own?” I answer that I do not say they must be better, nor why. I have only been arguing against those who say that because they come from another time they cannot suit us. I do believe, however, that the prayers written in the first ages of Christianity are in general freer, more reverent and more universal than those poured out since. I do not think this a singular opinion, and I would rather have it tested by the feelings and sympathies of religious men in different periods, especially in times of great suffering, than by my own theories. Still, I find it no harder to explain than why Homer has a fresher, truer feeling for nature and men’s outward doings than the poetry of the seventeenth or eighteenth centuries. If the age of the Fathers had the feeling toward God as the source and end of all things that I attributed to it in the last section, it is not strange that their devotions were simpler, purer and more human than those of men who were occupied, and by Providence’s order, as I think, meant to be occupied, with subtle questions about their own minds or the law and course of nature. Whether the succession and order of devotional acts may have much to do with man’s history and circumstances as well as God’s nature and plans, and whether later ages may therefore have thrown light on this which the first did not have, I will not say. To those who deny all order in devotion, and think it almost a sin to lay down offices of confession before thanksgiving, thanksgiving before prayer, and prayer before the higher communion, it can be no great derogation from the primitive times to admit that they grasped this spiritual sequence only in part. And if such people still want a further reason why particular acts of praise and prayer suited older times better than modern ones, the considerations we have been following contain the answer. For several centuries there has been a constant tendency toward greater individuality of thought and feeling. It has a true ground, though it may have led to very false results. But when unsupported by another tendency, it is in itself unfavourable to the worship of God and to fellowship among men. A vehement reaction against it has begun in all parts of Europe. One fruit of it will certainly be an aversion from all those utterances which modern Protestants have dignified with the name of devotion. If another consequence is not a return to the old forms and a delight in them, we must expect a reign of atheism.

The Philosopher.

When the modern philosopher objects to forms of worship, it is mainly because they support and perpetuate two harmful superstitions. The first is that a Being who is by His idea and law unchangeable and perfect can be swayed by our petitions into better purposes than His own. The second is that He can be pleased to receive praise from His creatures. The Utilitarian school states these complaints most broadly and precisely, but a large section of Rationalists adopt them in practice, with different wording and a different theory of man.

Both parties, by every word and act, philosophers or ordinary men, admit that evil exists and ought to be, and can be, reduced. The Utilitarian traces it to bad systems of government; the Rationalist traces it to man’s ignorance of himself and his powers. Each looks for his own Avatar and a millennium to follow. So each acknowledges an Ideal that men should agree with, do not agree with, and may be brought to agree with. Where, then, do we differ? Not in admitting the actual contradiction; we simply own what we cannot deny. We differ in this: our Ideal is a living Being. We believe He gave all things their right type and order, and that they are still very good in relation to Him. We believe their disorder comes from the voluntary renunciation of allegiance by the only creature able to make it, that all such disorder is against His will, and that restoration begins in the submission of those who caused the confusion. That submission is the confession that His will is the good will. One of its chief acts is to enter into His will and ask that it be exerted to remove the curses the evil will has caused. This is the rationale, in cold and poor words, of the ancient litanies that still express the longings of the most earnest people everywhere. They do not assume that anything in God changes. They are cries for the vindication and preservation of His unchanging order, and confessions that every act of His providence since the world began has aimed to make that order manifest and bring the universe into line with it. Nor do they rest on the false fancy that the world is right, that people fill their proper relations, or that things are not disordered by the sin of those meant to direct them. They assume that man is God’s minister, able to carry out His intentions toward His involuntary creatures, and able, because he has a will, to defy them. His proper condition, wherever he is, is dependence, doing another’s will. His proper way of fulfilling it here is to cry out for the setting right of whatever is independent, has lost its centre, and struggles to stand alone and so cannot stand, and thereby of everything else that has decayed through it.

It may be objected: “This supposes a restoration has already taken place. These prayers are meaningless unless those who offer them think themselves, on some ground, better off than those they pray for.” I answer: certainly. This is the very point I have pressed, that prayer does suppose a restoration, that the ideas of prayer and of a Church cannot be separated, and that a Church not built on the confession of a restored humanity is a contradiction in terms. But note: a restored humanity. Those who pray therefore claim no superiority to their race, not even over its worst member. The essence of their prayer is a cry that the sins they feel in themselves, and have committed, may no longer be their masters and the masters of the universe. They feel this more than other men, not less. They do not push evils off onto their neighbours; they identify their neighbours’ sins with their own and take responsibility for them. Only as members of a redeemed race and family can they claim the privilege asserted for them: to be new creatures, to shed their selfish natures, to disown the misery their rebellion made, and to enter the blessedness their Master won by obedience for all who will share in Him. So the Philosopher is right that this superstition of prayer, if it is one, has been kept alive by forms and would likely die out without them. I do not mean that the sense and need of prayer could die out in man. I mean that its one condition as a true and reasonable service is that men present it as members of a body or family continuing the same from generation to generation, which turns the notion of a human race from a dream into a reality. These forms embody that condition, and where they are neglected it is nearly lost.

I still have to speak of praise, which the Philosopher thinks unworthy of men to give or God to receive. His ground is that praise and flattery are the same thing. Flattery offends an imperfect being so far as his feelings are right and is tolerable only so far as he is weak and vain, so to a perfect Being, if He took notice of such folly, it must be altogether odious. I take issue on every point. Praise given to a man who does not deserve it is abominable because it is false. Praise he deserves but which is offered to bribe him for future favours is offensive because it is mean. Praise he deserves and which has no unworthy motive may still be wrong because it is imprudent, since men in their imperfection are made vain even by deserved commendation. But with these circumstances absent, I confidently affirm that no occupation is so elevating and delightful to a man as praising and thanking his brethren. Generous men in all ages have felt this. When self-interest was farthest from them, not even respect for the person admired, or fear of harming him, has stopped them from expressing gratitude for his favours or delight in the beauty of his character. With no ignoble aim these outpourings have often gone to kings and great men, because the thankful, humble heart felt their acts as condescension and saw a special fitness in their virtues. They have gone also to suffering friends, poor scholars, persecuted saints, and especially the dead, from whom nothing could be expected and to whom praise could do no harm. The true analogy from human experience, then, is this. If God lacks the imperfection that would make Him liable to the mischief of praise, if all the benefits His creatures have received come from Him, and if all the goodness and truth of which man’s is the reflection are in Him, then no act is so suitable and joyful to man as thanking and blessing Him. If anyone finds in this act a low, cringing wish to win some good from the One magnified, let him know that whoever begins in that spirit will soon be struck by its ridiculousness and incongruity, or wearied by the heartless, hypocritical effort. He will be unable to persevere, or at least to persuade himself he is doing more than repeating incoherent, meaningless sounds. The freedom and enjoyment of this act consist in the loss of self and escape from self. The worshipper has found the object toward which his eyes and those of all creatures were meant to look. Beholding it, they reach the perfection of their being and lose the selfish possessiveness that is incompatible with perfection. They gaze on Him who is all-embracing Love, in whom no selfishness can dwell, and all-clear, discerning Truth, from which darkness and falsehood flee. They are changed into the same image, and their praises are only responses to the joy with which He looks on His redeemed creation and declares it very good.

Let this service seem foolish to whoever will. We know it must be acceptable to God, because He is a spirit and truth and seeks those who will worship Him in spirit and truth. We also know it meets all the deepest wants that men, in the student’s garret, the palace and the hut, have each tried in different ways to express.

Take the man of earnest meditation. He hates the world’s turmoil and is angry at its meanness, yet through many thoughts of pride and discontent he keeps a desire for its good. He learns that seeking truth is the proper end of his life and finding it his only happiness. He strives and toils and suffers, and if the vision of some principle of living power dawns on him, he shouts “I have found it!” through the universe. His joy is real. Yet when he thinks of the thousands of men like himself to whom his discovery will do no good, who must still groan and die, his labour seems vanity and his truth a dream, and he curses himself for living so far from human sympathies. Take the gentle, generous man, raised among kindly family influences, whose imagination was trained early on lovely things and whose heart and conscience are unseared. He sees a beauty living and moving through all things and pursues it with insatiable passion. He cannot doubt his faith is real, though men call it a delusion; what has so possessed and exalted his being cannot be a lie. But what do pain, confusion and death mean? Are they merely shadows to make the light shine brighter? No! They master the light and obscure it. He grows sad; the glory has left the earth, and he cannot see how it could return. So in solitary hours, according to their tendencies and education, men have been haunted by the vision of a truth worth dying for and a loveliness that must be life’s only charm. One has seemed to live only in cold words and propositions, the other to keep changing shape and finally vanish.

Meanwhile the business of the world has gone on. Kings have reigned and dynasties changed, and men have felt that unless there were some awful Law which those kings acknowledged, and which outlasts every change of dynasty, society would be a dream and an impossibility. Philosophers have felt that such a Law must exist; politicians, that they must create the impression of it. But what is this Law? There are times when the question cannot be put aside, when it is asked and must be answered. For men rise up and say it is a cobweb of the imagination, which swords cut through in former days and which they with their brushes can now sweep away. Where is its birthplace and home, the warrant of its authority, the guardian of its permanence?

Suppose even that question answered. There is still a universe of distinct living beings groaning for daily bread. What shall we say of them? “Make them philosophers,” says one; “teach them to understand the truth of things and see their fair proportions.” That is plausible; let us begin. “No,” says another, who seems wise, “that will never work. What will your people care about sun and stars? They are men; they feel other men have ruled them, and not for their benefit; they want to rule themselves. Give them a share in the State, and then it will be time to make them natural philosophers.” We are convinced. We see that men cannot be satisfied with contemplating things at a distance; they must feel they belong to a system and that it does not move without them. We do not like giving up the hope that our brethren may know more of the world’s wonders, but we admit that the world they actually live and move in must be one of human feelings and hopes. Yet when we ask where and how you are carrying out the experiment of raising the poor man to a sense of his position, giving him citizenship and political power, we must confess we see only a scheme to rob him of what he already has, to take from him all sense of dignity, freedom and equality, and reduce him to hopeless slavery.

Now suppose it were possible that truth and goodness are not abstractions or formulas but realities. As traces of them have been seen in the acts of persons, suppose they dwell absolutely in a Person. Suppose this Being is King of kings and Lord of lords, from whom all law derives its life and power. Suppose He has set up a witness for Himself in the heart of the poorest man, and decreed that there should be desires in that heart which nothing short of His infinite perfection can satisfy. Suppose He has called this poor man to be a citizen of His kingdom, a member incorporate in it, and has said that he, as much as the richest man, is concerned in its order and organisation and may urge on the wheels in which the spirit of the living creature moves. Would it not then be true that the cravings of the philosopher, the needs of the statesman and the hopes of the wayfarer all find their highest interpretation in this worship, said to be the idlest of ceremonies? Are not the recorded deeds and desires of the world unintelligible without it? If this ceremony were abolished, and the idea of a perfect Being united to man, inspiring his prayer and hearing it, were lost from the universe, would not the philosopher’s imperfect hope die too? Would not belief in Law become impossible? Would not each man sink further into solitude and brutality, finding no one able to raise him and no one who was not helping to deepen his degradation?

The Romish System.

But these old Liturgies are still, in a sense, Popish. Any modern nations that have adopted their prayers received them through Popish hands, and so they carry a Popish stamp of approval. Some parts may even have been written by Bishops of Rome or by people who acknowledge their supremacy. How can this charge be answered? Can anyone claim there is an exact date at which what we call Catholicism ends and what we call Popery begins? Would we reject a prayer of Bernard’s as past the limit? If not, may there not be a prayer by À Kempis, or even by Pascal, that we would not entirely disown?

I answer as I did in the earlier case: I want no chronological lines. I am ready to use a prayer of À Kempis or Pascal, or of many a person less commonly tolerated among us. I have already explained why I think the older prayers are generally better than those composed in Europe over the last several centuries, but that explanation does not bear directly on this question. If the only distinction between the forms of the Catholic Church and those born of the Romish system is one reached by special pleading about birth dates or the soundness of particular men, I would rather leave the question unresolved.

But suppose the main and characteristic glory of the Church is this: that it is brought into the Holiest of the Holies, not into a figure of the true, but into the presence of God Himself. Suppose this is the great point separating the older forms from the cold efforts of modern devotion, that they claim this privilege with holy fear and confidence. Suppose ascriptions to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost are always on the lips and in the hearts of those who wrote them, giving their prayers both essence and body. Suppose each member is assumed in these forms to join the whole Church in every confession, petition, and thanksgiving, and that this union of each with all is involved in the prayers being offered for the merits and mediation of the one Lord of the whole body. On that ground the poorest member may join saints and angels about the throne, because the life and virtue of both are in Him. To these same causes the older prayers owe their freedom from the fetters of time and place that mankind, in its King, has shaken off. If their Catholicity consists in these qualities, then we have another measure, more righteous and more safe, for judging the system that takes the Catholic name. For this system, even in its mildest form, embodies the doctrine that members of Christ’s body cannot enter the Holiest of the Holies, cannot present themselves before God, cannot ascend where Christ has gone before them, unless they approach through intervening mediators. This notion is constantly embodied in the forms everyone recognizes as truly Romish. The mediators are not merely ideals of human excellence and beauty but helpers and heroes of particular towns, professions, and individuals. Even those most eager to deny the charge of positive idolatry will not deny this much, and I want no more. I do not care to dwell on the practical results that seem to me to follow inevitably from these premises, by a necessity stronger than the merely logical. Nor do I care to establish what seems to me written in sunbeams on the history of Europe: that once the first fatal step is taken, doubting or denying that communion between God and His creatures is really established in the incarnate Son, and that men’s union with their Lord is completed and cemented in Him, the result must be a steady downward course from divine worship to hero-worship, and from hero-worship to nature-worship. I simply take my stand here. I say to the Romanists: “By these acts you have set aside, as far as in you lies, the very meaning and end of the Church’s existence. You have destroyed its principle of union and fellowship, and reduced it to incoherent fragments held together by no divine law, and so needing some wretched human law to give it consistency.” I repeat, as far as in you lay, for you have not finished the work. A mightier power has been crossing your schemes and preparing their eventual confusion. Within you, not outside you, there has been a seed of life that these seeds of corruption and death sought to merge with because they could not destroy it. These old, holy, reverent forms have mocked your inventions as no vulgar Protestant scoffer could, by witnessing that the blessings those inventions offered were not too great for men to dream of, but too poor and pitiful for them not to trample underfoot once they know what curse they have been delivered from and to what height they have been raised.

These forms bear witness to holy men whom we are to remember, and whose special graces we may sympathize with, just because we are united, like them, to Him of whose fulness all have received, grace for grace. Let such figures be multiplied if you will; let each age add its share to the goodly company; let every blessing Christ has given through them to His flock, or to any part of it (for blessings to a part are blessings to the whole), be thankfully commemorated. The forms protest nothing against such remembering; they teach how to do it rightly. But the moment any of these holy men is regarded so that his departure from this world is no longer a sign to the poorest man left in it of his own fellowship with an unseen Lord, but a restraint on his spirit, a fleshly obstacle to communion, an earthly dream clouding the vision of a heavenly reality, the principle of these forms is attacked. Any new language introduced to sanction such an inversion or denial of the communion of saints stands in plain contradiction to the living words in which they embody it.

These forms invite us on certain days to remember our Lord’s acts: His condescension, humiliation, and triumph. They teach that if we forget the days, we risk forgetting what they speak of, and so sinking back into the dark, idolatrous, divided state from which Christ’s work has brought us. There is no return to Jewish outlook and hope; deny the fulfilment and we lose the expectation too, keeping only a confused dream of a possible blessing. But suppose men, through some degrading sensualizing of this testimony, come to fancy that the Church is not really redeemed, justified, and glorified in Christ, and that these unobtained blessings are to be bought by keeping days or by observances that preserve their fellowship with the Church. Then the forms commemorating these days, as the great signs and trophies of Christ’s finished work, refute that shameful delusion in advance far better than any words devised after it has spread. These forms also authorise days and seasons in which members of Christ’s body may enter into His humiliation and chasten themselves with His stripes, in order to keep down the evil inclinations that separate them from their brethren, to sympathize with human sorrow, and to realise the blessings given to the whole Church. But if a selfish, lying spirit should proclaim that by these fasts and penances for subduing the flesh a blessing is to be obtained which is given without money and price, that the individual who performs them is raised to a higher individual condition and may claim something for himself which an ordinary Churchman cannot, then these forms of humiliation pour such contempt on that godless and uncatholic pride as no one who thinks all restraint of self-indulgence vain and childish has ever managed to express.

I might go through many other cases, but these will do as hints. They prove, I think, that these ancient forms of worship hide something of the power I attributed to Baptism and the Creed: a power before which all human systems, and so the Romish, the most complete of them all, must at last shrink and quail.
