Chapter IV.— (Continued.) the Signs of a Universal and Spiritual Society

Baptism’s promise of a universal, spiritual family is confirmed by the Apostles’ and Nicene Creeds, recited unchanged by peasant and philosopher for sixteen centuries, against Quaker, Protestant, Rationalist, and Romanist accounts of doctrine and tradition.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Section II.—The Creeds.

In the last section I defended my view of Baptism against the Quaker, Protestant, Philosophical, and Romanist theories current about it. On my view, Baptism is the sign of admission into a spiritual and universal kingdom, grounded on our Lord’s incarnation and resting finally on the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. I have failed in my purpose if the reader has not also seen that Baptism, understood this way, justifies many of the Quaker, Protestant, and Philosophical principles considered in the first part. It is also one step toward satisfying the idea of a Church, one, indivisible, and imperishable, to which the Romanist clings with such honourable tenacity.

Quakerism teaches that man is a creature prone to sense who rises above it through union with an invisible Teacher. Baptism embodies that doctrine and turns it, as Fox wished, from a mere doctrine into a living fact. The Calvinist says the only foundation for faithful action and sound hope is the belief that we are God’s elect children. Baptism offers that foundation. It tells men they are chosen of God and precious, and it makes this rest, as Calvin and every earnest Calvinist felt it should, on the will and word of God rather than on our feelings and discoveries. Its distinction between the new man in Christ, who alone can be raised and glorified, and the old man, who is to be utterly abolished, is finer, clearer, and more practical than anything the exclusive Calvinist has reached. It condemns the unclean living into which a believer in his own separate election so easily falls, as wholly incompatible with the knowledge of God that is eternal life. Yet it treats no living man as outside God’s covenant. Philosophers say that man can only be or do what accords with his constitution, and that if some miraculous process could make him something else, that power would be harmful. Baptism declares that man’s true constitution is union with God, and that separation from Him violates the only order by which, as reason and experience show, he can live. Men do live anomalously, and by their own account they do so by following their natures. Baptism declares that those who will are taken out of this inconsistent condition and into a reasonable one, in which they may live as long as they remember God’s covenant. Finally, Romanism demands a direct, visible, permanent token that all can acknowledge, showing that God has established the true, Divine, Catholic body on earth. That body must be the same from age to age. Its members must be under spiritual discipline, invested with mighty privileges, laid under mighty responsibilities, and trained for a glorious condition. Baptism accomplishes this demand in a larger sense than the Romanist will admit. By this sign we claim him, and the hundreds of thousands in East and West whom he has anathematized, as members of the body of Christ. By this sign we also protest against him and them whenever any act or theory degrades the spirituality or narrows the universality of that fellowship, and so, as far as in them lies, voids God’s covenant and purpose.

I now want to ask whether other notorious facts can be explained only on the same principle as the existence of Baptism. These would be facts that seem on their face to show a spiritual and universal constitution of society for mankind, and that warring parties deny have this meaning. They would establish their claim by helping us justify and realise the leading principles of each of those parties and reconcile them with each other. The first that presents itself is this.

In every Christian country today there is a document called a Creed. We need not inquire closely when it was formed. Grant that its origin is obscure and that we cannot say who gave it its present shape; here it is, and it has outlasted many storms. The Roman empire has passed away and modern Europe has risen from its ruins. Political and religious systems have been set up and overthrown. The physical world has undergone mighty alterations, and our conception of its laws is altogether changed. The languages spoken when the Gospel was first preached have given way to others. Yet this “I believe” remains, substantially what it was at least sixteen hundred years ago. In that time it has not lain hidden in an antiquarian’s closet. Peasants and children in every land have repeated it, as a record of facts that concerned them as much as any noble. In most of Europe it has been said publicly every day of the year. Though thus hawked about and, as men would say, vulgarized, the most earnest and thoughtful men of different countries, periods, and stages of civilisation have felt that it touched the most permanent part of their being, concerned each of them personally, and was the symbol of the humanity they shared with their brethren. Reformers in conflict with all the prevailing systems of their age have gone back to it and said they lived to reassert its truths. Men on sick beds and martyrs at the stake have said that holding it fast let them look death in the face. And, to descend lower yet say what may seem more wonderful, many today have asked the philosophers of their own and earlier times how to understand the world, fight its evils, and love their fellow-men. They declare that in this child’s creed they have found the secret the philosophers could not give them, and which, by God’s grace, no one shall take from them.

A man who has noticed these facts, and has settled that they must mean something, would want to inquire into the nature of a document so widely spread, so long-lasting, and so valued by such different people. I think he will find that it differs from every digest of doctrines, religious or philosophical, that he has seen. A man is speaking in it, and its form is I believe. What is believed in is not a scheme of divinity but a name. There is a Father who made heaven and earth. There is His Son, our Lord, who was conceived, born, died, buried, and went down into hell, who ascended, sits at God’s right hand, and will come to judge the world. There is a Holy Spirit, who has established a holy universal Church and makes men a communion of saints. He is the witness and power by which they receive forgiveness of sins, and he shall quicken their mortal bodies and enable them to receive everlasting life. The Creed is plainly an act of allegiance or trust. Since it has always been joined with Baptism, we must suppose it takes its interpretation from Baptism. If by Baptism we are acknowledged as spiritual creatures united to a spiritual Being, by the Creed we claim that position and assert our union with Him. The name into which we are adopted there is the name we confess here. The acts done for all mankind, which warranted our particular admission into the covenant, are the acts we proclaim here as the warrant of our faith and fellowship. So far the form fits its apparent object. But does it also fit the idea of Christ’s kingdom that the Bible develops? There we found the first postulate of such a kingdom to be God’s condescension to man, the Creator taking notice of the creature. The second was man’s apprehension of God, the creature’s recognition of the Creator. By grace are ye saved; by faith are ye saved. The position, one of union and fellowship with another and of self-renunciation, is freely given. The power to claim it is given. Then comes the claim itself. This seems to be Scripture’s testimony, and the relation of the Creed to Baptism, and their common relation to that name and kingdom which Scripture reveals, remarkably expounds it.

But there is another creed with apparently equal authority, perhaps in use earlier in the East and recognized by the West ever since the age of Constantine. If the two clashed, or were built on different principles, or did not both connect with the idea I have described, the evidence from the preservation of either would be weakened. Or, if no such differences appeared and one could conveniently replace the other, with nothing distinct in each, it would be puzzling to account for the existence of both as universal symbols. To test these objections I urge the reader to compare the two documents thoughtfully. First, in reading the Apostles’ Creed as a declaration of the name into which he is baptized, does he not feel that it proclaims the distinct personality of Father, Son, and Spirit, as shown by certain relations in which they have been manifested to men? Then does not another question arise in his mind, as history shows it has in others: is there not a more mysterious and awful relation implied and prefigured in these? Does not the name express such a relation? Is not knowledge of it, as the ground of those relations, part of the revelation given to us? It is one of the deep things that cannot be understood (who understands the mystery of his own human relations?). Yet it lies so immediately beneath the facts that most concern us, is so needed to interpret and reconcile them, and has been so eagerly felt after in all ages, that if it is not disclosed to man’s heart and reason, they will be tormented by such dreams and imaginations about it as must make the acknowledgment of the Divine Unity impossible.

The Nicene Creed agrees with the Apostles’ altogether in form and principle. It is still I believe, still belief in a name and not in notions. It differs in uniting with a declaration of the Divine relations to men a declaration of the relations within the Godhead.

It speaks of this marvellous subject to every peasant and child. That is a strange fact, doubly strange when one remembers that teachers and priests in all ages have tended to think only a few initiated persons fit to know anything about the name and nature of God. That tendency mingled with the awe and reverence of the very ages that handed these creeds down to us. It is strange that the doctors of the Church allowed the Apostles’ Creed to be heard in every cottage. It is scarcely credible that they did not say the deeper creed, though it embodies the principles and data of the other, was only for theologians. Yet so it was. If it was God’s purpose that His name be revealed to men, and if His name, which seems to most of us tied to the highest and most esoteric abstractions, is really the only ground of a universal society, then we can interpret these facts. I now want to consider what other explanations have been offered for them.

Objections.— The Quaker.

To the Quaker it seems obvious that the invention of creeds is one clear sign of that mystery of iniquity, which has always arisen to counterfeit and destroy the kingdom of Christ. The faith a Christian exercises in the Divine Invisible Teacher is wholly inward and spiritual. A creed throws it outward, turns it into propositions, makes it the language of a whole congregation, and so reduces it to nothing.

I considered one side of this objection when I spoke of the differences between the Quaker and the Lutheran. It is exactly the objection to acknowledging the manifested Word, and it comes from a desire, more or less conscious, to separate the idea of a spiritual object from that of an actual person.

Mixed with this root dislike, however, is a feeling of a very different kind: that mere conceptions, opinions and notions are quite inadequate to the needs of a spiritual being, being only poor pictures of what is real. I agree with this most heartily; no doctrine have I been so anxious to maintain throughout this book. How we may be delivered from opinions and notions and rise out of them into another region is the very problem I am investigating. The history of Quakerism has helped me in this inquiry, at least negatively. It shows, I think, that there is no surer and more direct road into mere notionality than rejecting all common and united forms of utterance. A man treats the apprehensions and conceits of his own mind as alone sacred, and they become his tyrants. As far as he can express them, they become tyrants over the minds of others. In no society are there so many traditional phrases that once had a meaning and have lost it, or are rapidly losing it, as among the Quakers; in none is there greater bondage to such phrases, or greater dread of exchanging them for equivalents. So, without pressing again the point that all universality is lost this way, and that a body professing to be for all mankind became within a few years the narrowest and most peculiar of sects, I maintain that the experiment of doing without a Confession as a means of promoting spirituality has been made, and has failed utterly. Once more I claim our strongest opponents as witnesses in our favour. By the character of their arguments and the results of their practice, they have made it more probable that, if there is to be a kingdom of Christ on earth, a creed would be one of the divinely appointed means of preserving it — a creed that presents a living object, revealed in living acts, to the faith of all men.

Modern Protestant Objections.

The moment we say divinely-appointed means, the modern Protestant, or Evangelical, steps in and demands how we dare claim such dignity for a mere human composition, a mere ecclesiastical tradition. The Bible, he says, is the Divine document, and to claim that character for anything else is a gross intrusion on the Bible’s rights.

I would beg the person who raises this objection to ask himself whether he seriously believes the Bible is the only document, the only thing, that Divine care and providence has preserved for men. If he says boldly, “I do think this,” the debate is over. We are then reasoning with someone separated from absolute Atheism by the narrowest plank, so fragile that it will soon break. He cannot long believe this and still acknowledge a book marked above all by its insistence that whatever men possess they must attribute to God’s care and providence. But if he indignantly disclaims any such wicked idea, as he surely will, I would ask him next to consider calmly what I have said that offends him. Have I said the creed is a substitute for the Bible? That it is necessary because it supplies information the Bible lacks? That it corrects or qualifies anything the Bible asserts? I have maintained none of these. I have said: “I find a document that has lasted sixteen centuries or more. It explains to me the meaning and purpose of the Bible and shows me that the Bible has done what it set out to do. As a declaration of the name of God, it proclaims that what the Bible undertakes to reveal has been revealed. As an act of faith on the part of men, it proclaims that the faith by which the Bible says we are saved can be exercised.”

Is the doubt, then, why the creed should be necessary to those who already have the Bible, if it only affirms the Bible’s principles and facts? The history of Protestantism answers. In the hands of its orthodox teachers, the Bible was reduced to dry propositions, whose limits they were forever fighting over. In the hands of its Unitarian and Rationalistic teachers, it was reduced to dreary truisms not worth fighting about. You talk of the Bible and the Bible only, but when it comes to proof you give us in its place dry husks of logic or pompous inanities dignified with the name of simple truths. We want the Bible as it is, in its life and reality, and experience shows we shall not have it without some witness to the principles it embodies.

Again, Protestants affirm that the doctrine that faith justifies is the article by which the church stands or falls. So said Luther, and he looked to the creed as the great witness to it: the “confession of the mouth unto salvation,” in which “the heart’s belief unto righteousness” is expressed and fulfilled. The modern Protestant finds such language dry, cold, and carnal. What is the warm, juicy, spiritual language he has put in its place? History replies: endless controversies about the nature, mode, effect, signs, attributes, and qualifications of a living or dead faith. In them everything is remembered except the object of the faith and the person who exercises it. They bewilder humble Christians and drive them to despair. A practised logician finds great acuteness and ingenuity on both sides, but regrets that on both the favourite argument is begging the question. Am I then wrong in claiming the pure Protestant as a witness that this Catholic creed is an essential sign of the kingdom of Christ?

Rationalistic Objections.

The rationalist denies that the creed can be a permanent symbol of human fellowship, because it rests on the acknowledgment of certain events. “These events,” he says, “could not have won such belief unless they pointed to great principles that characterize us as members of a race. They point clearly to the sense in all men of something Divine; to the possibility that it might overcome evil, sorrow, and death; and to the feeling that it must submit to sorrow and death as the way to that victory. This is the essence of the creed, and it is universal. It can be traced in heathen and Jewish records, and it has outlived all the fables surrounding it in both. So it will probably outlive the so-called facts of Christianity as well. That is the more likely because the documents recording those facts are put daily to closer analysis, and the evidence for them seems daily less decisive.”

In the earlier part of this book I considered the general meaning and effect of this argument. I tried to show how true its basis is: belief in a divine humanity has existed in every age and taken countless forms. But I maintained that all these forms presumed some more perfect form. None reached its aim, and none revealed The Man, the head of the race. Yet each testified, more or less clearly according to the clearness of its light, that such a one must exist. When a great man claimed to be this, he became a tyrant and oppressor, in our Lord’s words a thief and a robber, a denier of humanity rather than its champion. So you do not weaken the creed’s authority at all by producing instances of this worship from ancient or modern history, or by dwelling on the tendency they show. The more of them the better, and the more closely they are examined the better. They prove that humanity has the idea you speak of. They prove just as strongly that humanity can never be satisfied with the idea alone. They declare it to be the idea of an actual, living, perfect Being, who would prove his perfection by entering fully into the lowest condition man has known and rising into the highest man has dreamed of. Unless the lowest humiliation and the greatest exaltation are combined in acts, the idea is not fulfilled and still waits. That is, we wait for a person who will do exactly the acts the creed describes. No others will serve, because no others will be universal enough to show that the one who does them is the Man. We are asked, then, for the evidences of the creed. Our answer is this: you have shown why it was believed, and what need in the deepest heart of mankind required it. It was believed not on the evidence of documents but on the plain proclamation of men who had the whole universe against them. They told men: Christ must be; you have been asking for Christ in every land and age; Jesus the crucified is the Christ. Three answers were given. The first was, “There are a thousand Christs; every kingdom and district has its own.” That would have satisfied, had men not also heard the other proclamation: “You are members of one body, and so you need one Head.” They felt this to be true, and the thousand could not prevail against the one. It is no wonder this answer is revived now, when the sense of being one body has so far left us that the principle that goes with it is readily abandoned. But I hope I have shown that there was never so strong a cry for universal, united fellowship as in this day of division, a cry from many opposite corners of the earth and many kinds of men. So this reply, having failed once, will not prevail now. The second answer was, “Beneath these different Christs there is an ideal Christ, and it is he, not they, whom you are to worship.” The people accepted the philosopher’s doctrine but said, “This is the ideal Christ, and here he is shown to us.” It is not surprising that this argument is repeated in a day when abstract notions have so replaced living truths. Yet I think we have seen that there was never more impatience with abstractions, or a more vehement demand for embodied realities, than now. If men have an idea of a universal Prince in their minds, they will either go on believing it realized in Jesus of Nazareth or look for its realization in someone else. This brings us to the third answer, made to the creed’s proclamation in the first ages and often since: “This crucified man is not the perfect Being we look for. We want a warrior, a philosopher, a poet, with qualities altogether different from those the Gospel narrative brings out, though we grant those too have a value of their own.” For twelve centuries a large part of the once-Christian world has believed this. Another part has declared that in the Cross it sees love triumphing through suffering, and in the Crescent only power claiming dominion over weakness; the first a bond of fellowship among the members of a suffering race, the other a pledge of universal slavery. Most of us are ready to confess with shame that the spirit of the Cross prevails very little in the nations that still profess to honour it, and that self-sacrifice is very generally and systematically denied to be the law of our being. So it is quite reasonable to expect that the experiment which succeeded in the nations of the East will be made, under other conditions, in the West. We have had many preparatory Antichrists, sovereigns who reigned by strength of mind and will and scorned every other right. Why should we doubt that this image will be still more fully manifested?

May God preserve those who live in the day when it is manifested and the world goes wondering after it! In that day, when intellect and will are crushed under the car of the idol they have set up, and the poor man cries and there is no helper, may God teach his saints to proclaim these words to the sons of men: He was born of the Virgin; He suffered under Pontius Pilate; He was crucified, dead, and buried, and went down into hell; He rose again on the third day; He ascended on high; He sitteth on the right hand of God; He shall come to judge the quick and the dead. May they be enabled to say, This is our God; we have waited for Him.

The Romish System.

Anyone who holds that the creed is an heirloom of the Church, kept for men by God’s providence, and that each generation of her members must guard it as an essential sign and necessary safeguard of her existence, acknowledges the authority and value of a Tradition. Only a feeble and cowardly thinker would shrink from the name after accepting the thing. The creed has been handed down, and what is handed down is a tradition. But the Romanist is the great defender of tradition. How, in principle, can someone who values the creed in this way differ from him?

I need not ask how far any given Romanist would approve language like this: Scripture is not of itself sufficient to make known the whole system the Church requires. The notions, opinions, and explanations of the Church’s doctors are authoritative and necessary, partly as explaining Scripture, partly as supplying what it lacks and was meant to lack. Together with Scripture they make up the ecclesiastical doctrine. I will not ask whether some particular Romanist writer objected to this. It will be granted, I think, that so far as he did, he was rejecting not an excess or exaggeration of the Romanist theory but a characteristic and integral part of it.

Compare this statement with my view of the creed, and the two are not exactly the same. There are differences, and I do not choose to use the phrases Romanists use. But is the difference one of terms only, or is it vital, pointing to an entirely different idea of what this document and the other documents of antiquity are for? I answer by translating my words, “The creed is the sign of a spiritual and universal society,” into what I believe are equivalent terms: The creed has protected the meaning of the Scriptures against the tendency of Church doctors in various ages to disturb and mangle it. It has protected the humbler members of the Church against the doctors’ inclination to rob them of their inheritance and keep it for themselves.

I have already illustrated these propositions with the doctors of the Reformed bodies. I have maintained that the Bible, left to their mercies, would have lost all its significance, and that we would have been fed with stones instead of bread. I speak only of an inclination, at times a very dominant one, in these teachers. I do not say there were no counteracting influences in their own minds and in those of their hearers. I have said again and again that there were and are, and that the more we consider the meaning and aim of the Reformation, the more of them we shall find. But I do say that this inclination needed a strong and Divine power to resist it, and that this power, which delights to work through humble instruments, has to a great extent worked through this child’s creed. I will now try to show in what sense and within what limits similar remarks apply to the early teachers whom the Romanist and we both profess to honour, and to the papal writers whom he reverences and whom, I trust, we do not despise.

It was the great glory of the greatest philosopher of antiquity to say that man needs to know what is, and cannot be content with opinions and notions about what may be. His being will not rest on these, nor will society. The ground of both must be a reality, an invisible spiritual reality, not a scheme or theory about this matter or that. The first Fathers of the Church sympathized deeply with this philosopher for that very reason. They felt he was asking for exactly what a revelation, if it were one, ought to give. They felt that we have been given not notions and dogmas about certain things but a revelation of God himself. By saying they felt this, I mean it was the deepest and strongest conviction of their minds, the one their admirers have always acknowledged as the great charm of their writings. To know God is eternal life. The Church is the society that rests on the Name and Unity of God and makes them known to man. I ask any lover of the Fathers whether he will not name these as the two great principles their words and lives illustrate.

If this is so, the Fathers’ theology must be most precious. Through endless confusions they worked their way into the clear heaven of these truths: God is, he is one, and his unity is not a dead material notion but a unity of life and love, the foundation of all unity among men. If we have no sympathy with those who first saw this light and rejoiced in it, and above all if we mock them, we must expect it to grow less clear every hour, to us and to our children. Suppose the Fathers, with the idea of God always before them, tended to merge the ideas of man and nature in it, instead of seeing that each must be distinct in order to keep its proper relation to the other. May not even this fault help us, if we use it humbly and faithfully? Their works are given to students, expressly and exclusively. They are committed to men with a particular vocation and responsibility, who most need to be taught how prone we all are to worship idols of the cave and idols of the forum. We set up notions shaped by our own temperaments, or popular in our age, in place of the great principles of which they are false likenesses. Students need this lesson so that they may see the glory of what is free and universal, and be freed from the preference our devil-infected nature has for what is esoteric and self-exalting. If the lesson is learned at all, it must be learned from the examples of good men, not bad, from those whose light makes the darkness visible, not from those in whom all is dark. Why then should we value the Fathers less because they can teach it?

Alas, students did not use the Fathers this way. They used them in a way they could not have used them had they heartily admired what was most precious in them, or had they not lost that admiration through the vanity of possessing something others did not share. They drew notions, opinions, and theories from the writings of saints who had declared that men thirst not for theories, notions, or opinions but for the living God, and must have that thirst satisfied or perish. So the Bible became to the student of the Fathers, as to the Protestant, a mere heap of notions. He too proclaimed that finding out these notions was the great problem of human life and the necessary step to everlasting salvation. But men generally could not take this step, since they could not find the true notions. The Fathers must help them, interpreting the Bible and supplying its deficiencies. Still we are at fault, for the Fathers are as unattainable as the Bible. What each of them affirmed, and what they agreed in affirming, could be ascertained no better than what each biblical writer affirmed, or what they agreed in affirming. So there must be a living authority able to pronounce on the point. Where was it? Was it the whole Church of any given age, or some particular member of it? The first was plausible but impracticable, so the last was adopted. Where the need was clear, the commission was not hard to find. A man was enthroned as the dogmatist of Christendom, appointed to say, and able to say, what men ought to think. Thus another stone, perhaps not the keystone, was added to the Romish system. But the system was not all that existed in the ages that gave it birth and brought it to maturity. Another element was at work. Men still repeated their Paternosters and Credos, and eminent men felt: Here lies the deepest wisdom; no decrees and dogmas can reach the sense of the Scriptures, the sense of the Fathers, like this infantine lore. So through the very heart of school divinity ran a stream of simple faith, a silent acknowledgment that the truth had been revealed, and that the endless complications of our minds, and the various forms in which we can see it, need not stop us from knowing and loving it. By degrees this faith was more and more obscured. Opinion became all in all, and corruption and infidelity flourished alongside growing superstition and slavery. Still there were holy and brave men, even in the later schools, who sought a truth beyond opinions. The mystical writers spoke of beholding God and dwelling in God. Ficinus and the Platonists, at the revival of letters, declared there was a way of seeking the substantial and the real. But the “I believe” turned the glorious hope of the one and the philosophical idea of the other into a fact for men. Then Pope Pius IV had to do openly what had long been done covertly: defy antiquity and invent a creed of his own. Thanks be to God, he could not do this effectively! In the nations that acknowledge his infallibility, it is still not his creed but the Apostles’ that mothers and nurses repeat to their infants, that children lisp in their own language, and that priests teach them. Surely the words are not always dead sounds, and some day they may start into life. Protestants may find in them the very heart of that Reformation doctrine which the systems of Protestantism have set at naught. The Churches that seek a centre of unity by crouching to Rome may find in them both the bond of their fellowship and the charter of their liberation. The Greeks may wake to the conviction that centuries of estrangement have not deprived them and the West of these common symbols, and that it cannot be God’s will for them to be divided. What a day that will be for the Catholic Church! What a day for the Romish system!
