Section II.—The Pure Theocratist

The Covenanter and Nonjuror would have the nation punish heresy by the sword as ancient Israel did, while the Separatist would keep Church and State wholly apart, but each misreads their true relation.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Under this name I include various groups that differ from one another in many ways but share a certain Jewish spirit, the spirit most directly opposed to that of the Quakers. The Scotch Covenanter is perhaps the most remarkable example. The Fifth-Monarchy Man shows a very different form of it. Some of its general features can be traced even in the Nonjurors of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, who looked on the Puritan factions with contempt and horror. It has come back in at least two forms in our own day. On the whole it is rather characteristic of Great Britain, so I shall be brief about it here.

The Theocratist believes, as I do, that the Old Testament is the great key to the meaning of national society. He believes, as I do, that the Lord is King of every nation as He was of the Jewish nation. He thinks, as I do, that a nation is to make wars, administer oaths, and inflict punishments in the name and under the authority of its unseen Ruler. From these premises he draws inferences. They vary with his other feelings, tempers and beliefs, but all seem to rest on the same scriptural authority.

The Covenanter says that the Jew existed to witness against idolatry and was to draw his sword to put it down. The modern nation exists for the same purpose and must use the same means. By its obligation and oath to God it is bound to root out every form of faith that is idolatrous or tends to idolatry.

The Fifth-Monarchy Man does not see the pattern of a theocracy in any existing nation. But one is about to be established, which will fulfil the prophecies about the kingdom cut out of the mountain without hands. He is not clear whether this society is to be universal or national, a Catholic Church or an Ancient Israel. He finds features of both in the Bible and tries to unite them.

The Nonjuror recognises the Church and the nation as distinct. But the nation exists in the person of its king, whose purpose is to support and spread the Church and put down heresy. That was the function of the old Jewish king, and it should be the king’s function now. For this he is anointed with oil and his person is sacred; he may not be deposed or set aside for any offence.

From what I said about the Sermon on the Mount, the reader may already understand my main point of difference with all these classes, but it is worth bringing out in relation to each. The Covenanter has the strongest warrant for saying that the Jewish Commonwealth was in all its parts a protest against idolatry, and that the moment it forgot that protest it virtually ceased to exist. I think he is also right that every nation exists on condition of bearing this protest. Sensual worship destroys it. Acknowledging an absolute unseen Being, and rigidly excluding whatever would confuse Him with His handiwork, is implied in the very nature of law and is the only footing on which law can be enforced.

A nation, then, must strive against idolatry. But how? Under a national, legal dispensation, where law comes first and spiritual principles are seen as supports of law, the nation must use against idolatry the same means it uses against other offences. Idolatry and all false worship must be crimes punished by the State. This has obvious inconveniences, but it is unavoidable, since the nation’s existence depends on it. Under a spiritual dispensation we escape this necessity. Idolatry moves from the circle of outward crimes to the circle of inward sins. The Lawgiver’s sword is felt to be inadequate to reach it, and the sword of the Spirit is known to be more effectual. The Spirit claims idolatry as part of its province and leaves to the legislature only outward crimes, which idolatry, if not rooted out of the heart, must cause. So the Covenanting doctrine, that because the Lord is our national King we must treat offences against Him as offences against the law, applies only to a State existing without a Church. Neither God’s honour nor the nation’s safety binds us to any particular method of avenging the one or preserving the other. If we believe, as believers in a Church must, that spiritual methods are the most Divine, and, as readers of history must, that they are the most effectual, we have no excuse for following Jewish precedent at the cost of sacrificing principles that the Old Testament implies and the New expounds.

It is evident from these considerations, I think, that the Scotch Covenanter was trying to establish a Jewish and not a Christian nation. That is, he wanted a nation professing Religion, not a nation that recognizes a Church as the ground and vital principle of its own existence.

Of the Millennarians I will say only that the records of their thoughts are well worth studying. Their confusions between what is distinct and what is universal, and between what is spiritual and what is legal, are the same confusions we find scattered through all ecclesiastical history, only concentrated in a small compass and stripped of restraining and correcting influences. And in the writings of Sir Harry Vane, if he is to be counted one of them, we may find very deep principles and remarkable distinctions. They need only the acknowledgment that the Catholic Church had embodied them ages before, to be as practically important as they are profound.

It will seem very strange that I should charge the Nonjuror, usually spoken of in England as the highest of High Churchmen, with the same legal, anti-ecclesiastical spirit we saw in the Covenanter. But the fact seems to be this: he thought of the Church as little more than the Canon Law embodied in a set of persons and institutions. He set this law far above the national law and resisted as Erastian any attempt to bring one under the yoke of the other. But of the Church as a spiritual, sacramental body, constituted not in laws but in a Person, showing the principles and essence of laws and so not to be confined by their formalities, he seems to have had little idea. So he gave little thought to how the Nation could serve the Church except by spreading its opinions and putting down its enemies. Nor did he consider how both might serve the purposes of Him who is absolute Love and absolute Righteousness, toward a spiritual creature living here under visible and earthly conditions. The same habit of mind that must have so obscured the New Testament for him seems not to have helped him much with the Old. Otherwise he must surely have seen that, however unlawful it is for subjects to depose kings, Scripture often says that the King of kings uses His own prerogative in deposing them. And if we live under His government as the Jews did (which is the Nonjurors’ own doctrine), we must have a right to ask whether He has ever shown displeasure at a monarch who broke a national covenant by setting him aside and appointing another. For it may be that in refusing allegiance to that other ruler, or denying that he is rightful as well as actual king, we are making light not of the privileges of subjects but of the authority of God.

Section III.—The Separatist.

By this name I mean those who say that Nation and Church ought to be separate bodies, though unlike the Quaker they do not regard national life as an evil thing. Their reasons are these: the State is secular; the Church, if it is a true Church, is anti-secular; to unite a secular body with an anti-secular one is monstrous. The results, they say, are an invasion of the rights of conscience, constant disputes between the two societies, and an impossibility of reformation. These are the doctrines commonly held by the successors of those English Puritans who were themselves such vehement asserters of the religious character of the State.

I need not reply at length, for I have granted their premises as fully as they could wish. A secular and an anti-secular society can live together only as deadly enemies. If the State is secular, the Church must desire its extinction, since she lives to destroy what is secular. What puzzles me is how Christian men can speak so tolerantly of something they label with so purely evil a word. If they do not use secular in an evil sense, where is the point of their contrast? How is the Church anti-secular, except by being opposed to something wrong and ungodly? If anti-secular means only spiritual as opposed to legal, the phrase is a cheat. We deny any contradiction between the legal and the spiritual, and so will they: they say constantly that Law and Gospel are not contrary, though the Gospel can do what the Law, weak through the flesh, cannot. They resist, as we do, the idea that what is evangelical is anti-legal, while asserting, as we do, that it is entirely different from the legal because its power is inward and not merely literal. What I have been maintaining is that a nation, just so far as it is a nation, is anti-secular in one way, just as the Church is in another. Both are God’s appointed instruments against the evil, rebellious, disorderly principles that make up the Scriptural idea of “this world.” Both are open to invasion by the principle they are meant to resist, and both have been infected by it. The Church became secular when she tried to realise herself as a separate body; the Nation became secular when it tried the same. Each did so by violating the law of its existence and refusing to be what Scripture affirms and history proves it was meant to be.

After what I said under the last head, little needs saying about the charge that this union is unfavourable to the rights of conscience; I have already explained how the notion arose. A nation, if it is to survive, must put down whatever undermines it. It finds that principles lead to acts, and that notions about the Supreme Being lead to evil conduct toward men; so it tries to extirpate those principles. Nations in union with the Church have used the sword for this, and Churchmen may have encouraged them. But the nation did not need to do it, and the Churchman should have known he had better powers for repressing such evil. He should have warned the statesman off his province and told him how impotent his arms are against spiritual enemies. Whether he did or not, the joining of his spiritual power with the national power is the great, the only witness to this truth. Separate it from the State, and the ruler, however liberal his notions, will use his power to keep down opinion. Preaching to him about the sin and folly of it will be in vain. He cannot ignore that opinions divide his subjects, make them rebellious, and lead to open mischief. He does not care about them in themselves, but if he is an honest man he cannot refrain from dealing with them by the means he has when the interests entrusted to him are at stake. You say he should leave them to spiritual influences. What influences? You denied that the spiritual had anything to do with the nation, and said the statesman must treat it as entirely unconnected with him.

Here, as everywhere, I have spoken to what I believe is the real feeling of those who raise the objection. They feel there is a Conscience in man, to be reverenced as God’s witness in his creature. No truth do I more want to assert. Without conscience, without an inner ear to receive the outer voice, I do not know what the Law would address by its terrors, or the Gospel by its promises of life, or what a Nation or a Church could mean. But is it not curious that those in our day and country who talk most of the rights of conscience in their speeches are least willing, in their more learned discourses, to admit the fact of a conscience, and even denounce the idea as inconsistent with human depravity? To this all must come who do not believe that man has a constitution, and that his depravity is shown in refusing to abide in it and choosing to be a separate, self-willed creature. We say his Conscience continually protests against his self-will; that the Nation’s office is to restrain that self-will by stern and righteous punishment when it breaks out into acts; and that the Church’s office is to bring out, by gracious and loving methods, the true free-will of which self-will is the base counterfeit. Likewise the nation’s office is to punish the overt acts of folly springing from private judgment that disturb the commonwealth, and the Church’s is to teach men to rise above private judgment to the clear, manly judgment that makes a good citizen. Sad when men are taught to indulge self-will and selfish judgments, and these evil qualities are clothed with the awful name of conscience. Sad for national order and for spiritual life.

I have considered the complaint that the relations of Church and Nation have always produced conflicts earlier in this chapter. The fact is undoubted: the Nation has tried to usurp the Church’s prerogatives, and the Church the Nation’s. All history is full of it; it is equally full of disputes between parents and children, brothers and sisters, kings and subjects. If we could get rid of relationships, we would not read of their being violated. But can you get rid of them without unmaking God’s world and turning it into a wilderness? Can what speaks to man’s inner life stand in no connexion with what speaks to him as an inhabitant of this earth? Has he not both spirit and body? Does their constant variance prove there is no law of fellowship between them?

But, it is said, union with the Nation hinders the Church’s reformation when she becomes corrupt. The historical evidence for this is particularly weak. Some great reformations were conducted against the civil power, such as Hildebrand’s in the eleventh century and many attempted by the religious orders. Others were carried on with civil rulers: the Lutheran in Saxony, the Zuinglian and Calvinistic in Switzerland, and those in Sweden, Holland and England, where the rulers were patrons and promoters. I see evils in all of them, both those done against the civil power and those done by it. But is the English dissenter prepared to say the first kind were much better in conduct and results than the last? If not, he must abandon his principle that the Church can be purified only by standing wholly aloof from the State. If so, he must defend himself as he can from the charge of Popery. To me it is clear, from experience as well as reason, that the State is an excellent admonisher of the Church about her inward corruptions, because it meets the outward evils that are their fruits; and the Church is an excellent admonisher of the State about its sins, because the spiritual man most clearly sees their effect in destroying the nation’s heart. But each does mischief if it tries, by its own maxims, to set the other right.
