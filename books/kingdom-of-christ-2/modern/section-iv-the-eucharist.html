Section IV.—The Eucharist

The Eucharist, kept for eighteen centuries by every rank and nation, is a sacrificial feast where Christ’s finished sacrifice is truly present, not a bare memorial or a change in the elements.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

In all the old forms of worship we have been discussing, one service is thought higher than the rest, giving them their worth and meaning. It belongs to a feast, called sometimes the Lord’s Supper, sometimes the Eucharist, sometimes the Communion.

This feast exists today in every part of Europe and in districts of Asia, America and Africa. It has lasted 1800 years. It has therefore outlasted all the changes we noted when considering Baptism and the Creed, and it has been the most holy symbol to nations divided by race, political institutions and habits that seemed to make impassable barriers. Within each of these nations there have been endless conflicts between rich and poor, nobles and plebeians. Yet even when those conflicts were fiercest, the feast was acknowledged as the highest gift to the great and still as something the lowest were meant to share. The line between the untaught and the scholar was drawn even more sharply than the line made by wealth or honours. The baron might need the serf, but the student seemed to live in a region entirely his own. Yet he acknowledged that in this feast he found the deepest subject for his thoughts, and that the most unlearned could possess its blessings as fully as he. When the Reformation came, one of these facts may be supposed to have ended: the feast was no longer the natural centre of religious and philosophical meditation. There was certainly a change here. It was the effort of the Reformation to detach itself from this centre, and to some extent each reformed body found a separate centre of its own. But it is equally true that, despite this effort, the Reformers had to make their views of this feast the distinguishing feature of their systems. Because they could not agree about its character and validity, neither the dangers from a common enemy nor their sympathies for one another could hold them together.

The strife went on through the seventeenth century, as new religious and philosophical systems were completed or established. Protestant countries no less than Romish ones still had in the Eucharist a strange remnant of the past. It could not be ignored and was very hard to fit into any system, yet it had to be fitted in, because it kept asserting its power in defiance of them. In the eighteenth century the same methods used to shut out the invisible elsewhere were applied here too. Even so, tens of thousands of men and women across Europe would then have given up their lives, or anything dearer, sooner than give up this feast. Now, in the nineteenth century, not a few people who have reflected on these experiments have reached a deep, inward conviction. They believe that whether Christianity remains a practical principle and truth in men’s hearts, or is exchanged for a set of intellectual notions and generalisations, depends mainly on whether the Eucharist is acknowledged and received as the bond of a universal life and the means by which men share in it.

Suppose this notion is utterly extravagant and false. It must still be interesting to know what this institution is, which has won so many willing and reluctant testimonies to its importance. Describing its nature may be hard without entering the points on which these parties disagree, but its origin is not disputed. Protestants, Romanists and Greeks, all who receive it, trace it to the same time and practise it under the same authority. All would say: “The night before the crucifixion of Jesus Christ, while keeping the passover with His disciples, He took bread and wine, blessed them, and said, ‘This is my body, this is my blood; do this in remembrance of me.’ This is what our custom means, and we keep it in obedience to this command.”

These words were spoken to a little band of disciples, and only to them. No multitude was present, as at many of our Lord’s discourses, and no distant bystanders to whom He might have said, “What I say unto you, I say unto all.” Nor does any express language say that the command given to these poor fishermen that night was meant to reach other ages. The words might only mean that a deeply loved person, about to be parted from his friends, was leaving them a token and memorial of their time together. “This is my body, this is my blood” might sound strange and exaggerated. Yet at what seemed a final separation, such a friend’s words would be especially simple and awful, as we know His other utterances were. They might mean only: this will remind you of my person, and this of the blood about to be so unrighteously shed. That explanation, however embarrassing, would be the easiest, indeed the only possible one, unless something about the whole character of the speaker, His other acts and purposes, or the time when He spoke, fixed the words to a different sense.

Suppose, then, that the speaker was the Son of man and the Son of God. Suppose that at that very time He had been declaring Himself the way by which men must come to the unseen Father, the truth, the life, and to stand to His disciples as the vine to its branches. Suppose He was about to give them a Spirit who would guide them into knowledge of the Father and the Son, had told them they were His appointed messengers of these truths, and had prayed that not only they but all who believed through their word might be one in Him as He and the Father are one. Suppose He connected all these mysterious words with giving Himself up to death. Suppose death has been felt in every age and country as the great barrier between the visible and invisible worlds. Suppose that in every country that reached any national fellowship, sacrifice, the giving of animals to death and offering them to some unseen Ruler, was felt to be the means of approaching Him, winning His favour and removing His wrath. Suppose sacrifices were the most essential part of the Jewish institutions and worship, the only way a Jew could approach the God of his nation as a member of it. Suppose the Jews were taught, by the law that appointed the sacrifices and by the prophets who expounded it, that these had no value in themselves. They were accepted when performed by God’s appointment through His priests, as the offerer’s confession that he had broken his relation to the head and members of the commonwealth, as a submission of the will, as a prayer to be restored to the place lost through self-will, or as an expression of the total self-surrender implied in belonging to the Divine society. Suppose the feast the disciples were keeping with their Master was the most purely national and sacrificial of all, the one celebrating the nation’s first deliverance and founding, recalling that it rested on sacrifices, in which every Jew enjoyed his covenant, rejoiced that God was his King, and knew he was an Israelite. Suppose all this, and then consider whether the interpretation that seemed the only possible one, though a difficult and perplexing one, does not become irrational and monstrous.

Consider whether anyone who believed what we know the Apostles believed about their Master, His Person and His kingdom, could attach anything but the very highest significance to words about His body and blood. Consider whether people who believed what they did about their own office and work could suppose that significance limited and temporary. Consider whether people who, as we know, connected the kingdom they served with the earlier dispensations could believe anything but this: that by the same simple, wonderful method used in all countries, appointed in their own by God’s authority, which had let the Jews enter their covenant and whose neglect had again and again cheated them of it, He meant to give them everything He came to bestow on mankind. Could they doubt that in eating this bread and drinking this wine they fully shared in the sacrifice with which God had declared Himself well pleased? That they really entered the Presence which the Forerunner had entered for them? That they received in this communion all the spiritual blessings which the union of Godhead with human flesh opens to that flesh’s heirs? Could they doubt that the individual death they claimed in Baptism was here actually reached through fellowship with Christ’s death, and the new life they claimed as members of His body was here reached through the communication of His life? Could they doubt that if their spirits were to be raised to see the infinite and absolute glory, here they were admitted to that blessedness; that if their hearts desired a manifested, embodied king, here they were united to Him? Or that if spirit, soul and body were to be governed by God’s Spirit, each delivered from its own corruption, quickened, and given living power, here each received the strength and renewal to do its work, overcome its temptations and be fitted for its perfection? Could they doubt that if they were baptized into the name of the Father, Son and Holy Ghost, and this deepest unity was the foundation of a union among men that no barrier of time, space or death could break, here they were actually received into communion with that awful name and with all the saints who live by beholding and delighting in it? Could they doubt that here the partial views, one-sided words and opposing thoughts of men find their meeting-point and reconciliation? Here is the clear, living expression of distinctions that in verbal theology become dry, hard, dogmatic oppositions. Here it is grasped how faith alone justifies and yet faith without works is dead; how we act, and yet not we but Christ in us; how he who is born of God cannot sin, and yet if we say we have no sin we deceive ourselves; how we may be persuaded that nothing shall separate us from the love of God in Christ, and yet may tremble lest we be castaways. Could they doubt that it was their office to present Christianity in its different aspects to the different needs of their own and later ages, and that it was this sacrament’s office to show it as a whole truth, at once transcendent and practical? It surpasses men’s thoughts and does not depend on their faith and opinions, yet it belongs essentially to man, as the governing law of his being and the driving power of his life. Could they doubt that they were laying the Church’s foundation on earth, and that this sacrament was to give it permanence, coherence and vitality through all generations? And if this was their faith, why, I ask, is it not ours? What has happened to rob this sacrament of its meaning, or to make that meaning less fitting or less necessary for us in the nineteenth century than for those in the first? The answers to these questions are various.

Objections.— The Quaker.

In this case, as with Baptism, the Quaker believes we have clung dangerously to Jewish precedent: we have kept signs that should have been abolished and followed shadows when we should have grasped the spiritual substance. Here, he thinks, the sin is worse. The essence of Christianity is the real sacrifice that we, following Christ’s example and by his power, can offer. In place of surrendering ourselves, the true self-annihilation, we have a ceremony dressed in great names and invented attributes, so that we can excuse ourselves from any practical sacrifice.

I am willing to be charged with repeating myself so as to notice again the first and more general of these complaints, because the special arguments attached to them throw new and valuable light on them. The difference between us and the Quakers in the earlier case was this. They suppose that the Christian Covenant, being spiritual, dispenses with the method that had divine sanction under the earlier dispensation. We believe that because it is more spiritual than the Jewish, it needs another application of the same method, so that the difference may be seen. Having the sign of the lower covenant to set beside the sign of the higher, I can understand how the one surpasses the other. The Quaker cannot make that comparison; he only talks of the distinction, and can neither grasp it in fact nor put it into words. He rates the old covenant far below its true value, yet in his thoughts keeps reducing the new to its level, and in practice makes the new covenant’s perfect spirituality consist merely in lacking a feature that the old one’s lesser spirituality required. That concerns the preliminary act or condition of the covenant. Applying the same rule to its results, privileges and enjoyments, the Quaker asserts that the Jew realised the blessings of his covenant in a sacrificial feast, and that since the blessings of ours are spiritual, such a method is impossible for us. We affirm that the privileges the Jews realised in their festivals were spiritual: looking up to an invisible Guide, King and Friend and rejoicing in him, and feeling themselves a nation. These are exactly the privileges the human spirit craves, God gave them to the Jews in a simple, reasonable way, and once we understand what was given, it is hard to imagine any other way of receiving it. We affirm too that our privileges are higher than the Jews’, but only as the perfection of what they had imperfectly. They are still fellowship with God and fellowship with our brethren — but fellowship with God as a Being who has entered into direct union with our race in the Person of his Son, and with a Race in its Head, not merely with a particular Nation. So we want to know what in the character of these blessings makes a united festival unsuitable for realising them. It was suitable, even necessary, for realising the others. Show us, by something more than repeating the words “carnal” and “spiritual,” how the change has come about.

We grant freely that the institution must change in nature as what it expresses changes. We grant that Christianity is nothing if it is not the actual, substantial realisation of a union that was before largely prophetic and ideal. We grant that a mere shadow, a pictorial feast, would be more inconsistent with the Gospel than even with the Law — though inconsistent with both, since in each case the feast ought to put those who receive it into real possession of what they were then able to possess. But granting all this, the questions remain: “Can there be no feast suited to the position of Christians, as the Passover was to that of the Jews? Have those who deny there is such a feast, calling it a mere ceremony and phantasm, shown that they keep the substance of Christianity?”

To examine this last point, let us ask why the Quaker protests against this institution. The Christian sacrifice, he says, ought to be real: a man giving himself up to death after the example of our Lord. Christ’s death was most real, carried into every act and word; how can we think we show that death in a service less actual, individual and continuous? From this it is clear that, for the Quakers, the end of Christianity is individual self-denial or self-sacrifice: Christ perfectly sacrificed himself, and by his power in us we may do the same. I believe I acknowledge as strongly as he does that Christianity involves this, and has no meaning if the principle of self-sacrifice is not at its root. But since we both agree that our Lord’s example is the one we are to follow, and that the type of sacrifice is in him, I must ask whether he treated sacrifice as the object of his life or only as its indispensable condition. His answer seems to me very plain. He declares that he came to glorify his Father’s Name and to do his Father’s Will, and that he came to die for the sheep. Because he glorified his Father’s Name and not his own, and because he would not be an individual man but identified himself with the lowest condition of those he was not ashamed to call his brethren, we see in him the perfect example of self-sacrifice. Forget this and the whole idea of his life is lost. Imagine him coming into the world not to manifest God but to display a specimen of glorious heroic self-sacrifice, not to die for men but to show how he could die — and the example perishes. We would have an object that no one used to contemplating his Lord with any love or devotion could bear to look at. Yet if we believe the end of our own lives is self-annihilation, we must either make this change in the image we profess to copy, or forget it altogether and fix our eyes only on ourselves.

That is not all, as the Quakers’ history has shown. When this doctrine of self-sacrifice and self-annihilation has not led them into conscious self-righteousness and self-glorification, it has produced a miserable confusion about their own lives and duties. If the Spirit of Christ is leading us to complete crucifixion, they have asked themselves, shall we not be resisting him if we keep alive any particular affection or faculty? Yet the same conscience that seemed to demand self-sacrifice also said, How dare you crush the powers, energies and affections God has given you, for which you must account to him? The difficulty is wholly practical and the contradiction agonizing. Its fruits have been as distressing to those who watched as to those who suffered. Some, thinking such feelings must come from a dark superstition, have fled to infidelity or indifference. Others, more earnest and sincere, seeing that the sacrifice of Christ had been lost sight of in these efforts at self-sacrifice, have violently denounced all such efforts as godless and vain, and, using sound language about the all-sufficiency of the one sacrifice, have made it a foundation for Antinomian doctrine and practice.

But suppose we kept steadily before us these thoughts. The hallowing of God’s name is the end for which our Lord lived and we are to live. To thank and praise him for what he is and has done, and so to enter into the perception of it, is the highest happiness we can reach. Our Lord, one with the Father, showed in every act of his life perfect sympathy with him, delight in him and submission to him, and the voluntary sacrifice of his body to death was the final and consummate act of these. Self-will and disobedience are obstacles to communion between men and their Creator, and equally to communion with each other; so the same act that removed the one obstacle removed the other. The cross of Christ is the centre of all fellowship; and while we seek fellowship there, affirming that we exist only as members of Christ’s body and draw our life from him, we may find strength habitually to deny ourselves as he did. We then have an idea of Christianity altogether different from the Quakers’, yet including all its practical truth — one that must have hovered, as we know it did, before the minds of the early Quakers, or they could not have conceived their own narrow and fragmentary notion. A person living in this light must see Christ’s sacrifice as distinct from all other sacrifices, because only through it are we brought into God’s presence or made one body. He cannot see it as separate from any other sacrifice, because all sacrifices draw their worth and meaning from it. He must regard self-sacrifice as a necessary element of Christian life. He cannot let it become self-conscious, and so self-contradictory, by treating it as a means of procuring a blessing, when it is the fruit and fruition of a blessing already procured. He must hold that every Christian is obliged to mortify his selfish nature in order to offer God an acceptable sacrifice. But he cannot confuse mortifying the evil nature with destroying or weakening any faculty God has given. Those faculties are impaired and ruined by the dominion of the evil nature and are strongest when it is most subdued. They must be kept strong because God requires them as a sacrifice, and the more they are sacrificed to him the stronger they become.

So we have seen another case in which the Quaker, refusing to keep what he calls a mere form, has utterly perverted or lost a principle. I do not charge it on him as a special sin that he has inverted the notion of sacrifice, put means in place of ends, and brought in self-righteousness under the name of self-forgetfulness. These tendencies belong to all ages; they are the tendencies of our individualizing natures, and in this the Quakers are no different from other men. The sin I do charge them with is this. Christ, out of love and mercy, gave mankind a simple and wonderful testimony against these narrow notions and dividing tendencies. He embodied the complete idea of his kingdom in a living feast, which we, seeing things partially, from different sides and through the prejudices and false colourings of particular times and places, keep reducing to some name, notion or formula of ours. He made this feast effectual for imparting a faith far above the level of ordinary theories and speculations, and gave it as a bond to all peoples, languages and generations. Yet they chose to fancy that his ordinance signified nothing, and that they had a much better storehouse for his truths in their own fine thoughts and spiritual apprehensions. For this sin I maintain they are suffering the punishment, in the almost entire loss of the Spirituality and Universality they hoped to attain by these means.

2. The Zuinglian, the Calvinist, the Lutheran.

1. Pure Protestants would generally agree in one objection to what I have said. They would say that calling the Eucharist a sacrificial feast is dangerous language, incompatible with a full recognition of Christ’s finished sacrifice on the cross. “If it be sacrificial it must be propitiatory; the words are convertible; then what becomes of the doctrine of the Atonement as the Reformers held it?”

From this shared negative point my opponents soon divide into classes. The first see the Eucharist as a mere memorial of a past transaction. When I treat it as a substantial feast, in some strange way identified with the spiritual things it speaks of, and as a channel through which actual blessings are received, they say I am using phrases that Scripture does not warrant and that are contrary to plain sense and experience.

The second class think differently. For them the true believer does realise an actual, mysterious blessing in the Sacrament. He does not merely recall a past good; he is conscious of a present one, and Christ is with him in the feast. My mistake, they say, is supposing the good exists in the Sacrament apart from the faith of the receiver, and that doctrine unsettles the very foundation of Protestant Christianity.

The third party disagree with this. They hold that the Sacrament has a reality it does not receive from the mind of the partaker, because Christ is actually consubstantiated with the elements. The error in my principle, they say, is that it supposes us brought into a holy and Divine Presence and yet offers no explanation of how so wonderful a thing happens.

Before I take up the objection on which Zuinglians, Lutherans, and Calvinists agree, let me recall what I said under the last head. I said that Quaker history had shown the great danger of supposing that our Lord’s sacrifice is merely a pattern of our sacrifices, or merely the power by which they are effected. It must have an entirely distinct character, or it is worthless as either an example or a power. I also said that this distinct character is exhibited in this Sacrament, and that the Quakers, by losing the Sacrament, have lost the sense of it. I do not think this supports the suspicion that I confuse the sacramental act, which is performed by men and so is by hypothesis one of our sacrifices, with the sacrifice of Christ, or that I suppose the need of the one proves the other incomplete. Every word I have used points the opposite way. Because the sacrifice once for all accomplished its object, bringing our race, constituted and redeemed in Christ, into acceptance and union with God, it was therefore fitting that there should be an act by which we are admitted into the blessings thus secured. Because those blessings were given not only to the generation of the Incarnation and death but to all generations, the act should be renewed through all generations. And because the blessings belong not to one moment but to every moment, the act should be continually renewed by us on our pilgrimage. So when we call our feast, like the Passover, sacrificial, we do not deny that it commemorates a blessing fully obtained; that would break the analogy in the moment of using it, for the Passover commemorated a complete deliverance and the founding of a nation upon it. But that deliverance was accomplished through a sacrificial act, and yet the act was perpetually renewed in the Passover, because the nation understood that it subsisted by sacrifice and, by renewing it, realised the permanent, living character of the good bestowed. So it is here. Christ’s sacrifice is the only thing with which God can be satisfied and in whose sight He can look on our race, so it is the only meeting-point of communion with Him. Once that communion is established, it is by presenting the finished sacrifice before God that we both testify to our position and realise its glory. Otherwise we have a name without a reality, and with the words “finished and complete” we rob ourselves of the very thing that makes them worth prizing.

Why Protestants have overlooked this will be clear from my earlier remarks. The worth of Protestantism lay in asserting the distinct position of each man as a person and not merely one of a mass. This truth had been working its way into clearness for many centuries, in a strange and painful process. Conscience is what tells each man he is a person: it makes him feel what he did in the past to be his own, and gives him an awful assurance of identity, responsibility, and permanence. Overburdened with the sense of evil, it looked for a remedy and was told to perform certain services in hope of finding one, and with each attempt the sense of evil grew. The Reformers found the whole scheme a delusion. The services presupposed the very freedom of conscience that men sought to gain by them; without it they were not true godly services. So the emancipation of conscience was what the Reformers sought as the step to all good, and they declared it could be had only by faith in Christ, grounded on acts of complete redemption done on their behalf.

I have argued again and again how true this language was, what a curse came on the Church from denying it, and how necessary it was to proclaim it then and always. But it is equally certain that, as the Quakers took self-sacrifice, so the Reformers took the emancipation of conscience to be not a necessary condition of our moral being but its end. Whatever contributed to that end was necessary; whatever did not was worthless. Belief in Christ’s sacrifice on the cross had given their consciences peace, and that it had any other purpose was more and more forgotten. So it became necessary to explain how it brought that peace, and there began all the theories of sacrifice, satisfaction, and imputation. I have called these fatal both to the principles of the Reformation and to the practical life of Christianity: they gave no comfort to the humble heart, led to disputes and separations, and prepared the way for the infidelity of the eighteenth century. These hungry notions of the understanding replaced the Reformers’ clear, simple belief that we are adopted into Christ by Baptism and are therefore children of God, free to draw near to Him in all duties and services, confessing the sins that have polluted and separated us. The result was confusion. Men knew they were not approaching God with pure consciences, and the Reformers had said that if they did not, the service was a mockery. So they searched here and there for some better kind of faith to relieve them, and, finding none, judged the whole Gospel a dream and a fable.

2. But beneath all these dark imaginations and sad results lay, I believe, the Reformers’ own imperfect grasp of the nature of the Communion. The Zuinglian says this feast is nothing but the memorial of a past transaction. That it is the memorial of a past transaction is assumed in every word I have said; otherwise it could not be called Eucharist or be analogous to the Passover. But the Passover did not refer only to the past. The Jew had been brought out of Pharaoh’s government into God’s, and in commemorating the nation’s emancipation he claimed for himself a privilege that belonged to it then. It would be insulting the Zuinglian, I think, to suppose he saw the Christian ordinance as different in this respect. He is practical and rational, and must know that no men ever celebrated heartily and affectionately an event they did not suppose to be, in some sense, the cause or beginning of an improved condition with which they were connected. So by “simple memorial” he cannot mean that the feast has no present, continuing interest. If he did, he would be supposing, against all his professions, that our Lord’s religion imposes a dry, dreary, meaningless ceremony as a test of obedience. But if he grants, as he will, that certain effects of our Lord’s death are ours, and that the feast recalls these effects and not just their cause, the question follows: what are these effects? The great one, in which every other is included, is that we are made capable of entering God’s presence, that a mercy-seat is revealed where man may meet his Maker. If so, this must be one of the effects the Eucharist brings to mind. I do not object to the word recollection; it fits a Living Actual Presence perfectly well. What I plead for is the duty of recollecting that presence in the Eucharist, because it is there.

But the Zuinglian will ask, Why there and not elsewhere? The question can mean two things. It may mean: why may we not feed on Christ’s sacrifice at all times, and so enter the presence of Him who perfectly delights in it? Or it may mean: God is omnipresent, so why are we not always in His presence? These are thoughts of very different kinds, coming from very different states of feeling. The first sets before us the highest standard of perfection a Christian can propose, and yet, if what I have said is true, a real and reasonable one. That the Church is brought into God’s presence is the first principle of the New Dispensation, and especially involved in this sacrament. If each of us ought to count himself a member of the Church, this privilege is ours not in proportion as we raise ourselves to individual excellence but as we give up such distinctions and yield to the Spirit who dwells in the whole body. My answer here is what I said of the Quaker doctrine. If we acknowledge that the light is somewhere concentrated, that it reveals itself in a way it has chosen, and that the revelation is for all and not only for us, and if we acknowledge this in practice, then we are at least on the road to the blessing we are rightly told to seek. Otherwise we shall imagine we produce the presence by our own acts of meditation or faith, and glorify ourselves for them, and for a reality we get a dream. Then we gladly turn to the other doctrine, which boasts that it asserts “the Omnipresence of the Deity.”

So I believe it has gone with the Zuinglians. An early disciple of the school, with an almost superstitious veneration for the Bible, would have rejected this phrase as contrary to its letter and spirit. He would have asked how it fits with Genesis, which speaks of God meeting Adam in the garden, coming down to see the tower men had built, and appearing to Abraham at his tent door. A Zuinglian of the next century would perhaps have learned to call these passages “figures” and “eastern allegories.” Still he would have reasoned that honest men use figures to convey some truth, and that if he took “ubiquity” and “omnipresence” in their ordinary sense he must suppose the Word of God less honest than the word of men, since these stories would then contradict a truth rather than imply one. But a Zuinglian of the third century has mastered all these difficulties. He disposes of the scriptural expressions by calling them “anthropomorphic,” signs of a low state of civilisation. Or, less honestly, he passes over them altogether, assuming that “Omnipresence of the Deity” must be good and true whatever else, in the early thoughts of men or in the revelations they led toward, turns out to be false.

Let us consider the philosophy of this phrase. It was adopted to convey that the limits of space do not apply to a Divine and absolute Being. But does it convey that to anyone who reflects on his own thoughts? Is “everywhere” less a word of space than “somewhere”? Did the ancients imprison the Divine Essence in forms less when they made it dwell in every tree and flower than when they pictured it as Jupiter seated on the Thessalian mount? No. The more they gave their Jupiter personal qualities, believing him capable of love and hate and a father’s feelings, the less they conceived him under the limits of space and time, compared with when they translated his name as “the air” and thought of him as a subtle fluid spread through the universe. In the first case they were dreaming of a Spirit men could converse with: a Spirit mixed of good and ill, their own image, but still to be grasped by what is spiritual in man. In the second their thoughts were wholly physical, no less so for being rarefied and subtilized; or if there was anything else in them, it came from the older faith.

This agrees with the passage of our Lord’s teaching so often quoted to prove a different doctrine. He told the woman of Samaria that a time was coming when men would worship the Father neither on Mount Gerizim nor at Jerusalem. He does not give as the reason “God is everywhere.” He rises to a higher level: “God is a Spirit, and they that worship Him must worship Him in spirit and in truth.” And with this he joins what modern thinkers would take for its direct contradiction: “We know what we worship, for salvation is of the Jews.” Such language would be wholly inconsistent with the omnipresence doctrine, but not at all with the doctrine “God is a Spirit.” Every step of Jewish revelation and history had presumed that truth and prepared for its full manifestation, bringing out more fully the idea of God as the Holy One, the Moral Being, the object of trust, awe, and reverence. And nothing expressed this more than the arrangements that seemed to localize the Divine Presence. Because He was the Holy One, He must not be worshipped in all the forms of nature; He must be seen as distinct and personal, approached in the temple through the priest with the sacrifice. By these means, now thought so sensual, men were taught that they were not to apprehend God with their senses, but that what in them desires truth and holiness must seek Him, and that He was by a wonderful method drawing it to Himself. So although simple people who had sought God without the law might be far better prepared to welcome Him who brought their sins to mind and told them all they ever did than the proud idolater of the law could be, yet those who had profited by the law were better prepared than any others to see God’s glory in the man Christ Jesus. These were the Israelites indeed, without guile, who had served God day and night in the temple and waited for the consolation of Israel. They felt no contradiction in the perfectly Holy One inhabiting a body of human clay; it was a low, carnal notion of the Godhead, one that really identified Him with physical things and so subjected Him in practice to the laws of space, that made it seem a contradiction.

I maintain, then, that the highest, clearest, most spiritual, most universal idea of God a creature can reach is not the one he gets from a dream about omnipresence. It is the one he enters when he contemplates the fulness of truth, holiness, and love: the absolute and perfect Being pleased to identify Himself with a human soul and body, to suffer with them, to raise them out of death and to glory. Here we do not try to merge complete spirituality and distinct locality, each demanded by man’s reason and each necessary to the other, into a wretched abstraction called ubiquity, a notion empty of substance that only puffs up the mind with the vague sense of holding a great idea it really needs but has missed altogether. I would hesitate to speak so of a phrase so common among religious people, and which may carry sacred associations for them, if I did not see that it has perplexed little children, made moral and Christian education almost impossible, brought infinite vagueness and weakness into our preaching, and prepared men’s minds for settled, hopeless pantheism. That it has also lowered and confused our feelings about the Eucharistic feast is implied in all its other effects. But here it has met an enemy able to cope with it. The impression that this sacrament is a reality, in spite of all attempts to prove it a fiction, has kept alive the belief that God’s presence is a truth and not a dream, and that we may enter it in a better and truer way than by fancying ourselves in it when we are only indulging pleasant sensations and high conceits.

Note: Mr. Coleridge has said all this in these words: “All this comes from the young men of this day having been educated to understand the Divine Omnipresence in any sense rather than the alone safe and legitimate one, the presence of all things to God.” (Aids to Reflection, p. 398; see the whole passage, pp. 388–401.)

But if I insist that we can hold communion with a spiritual Being only with the spirit, how do I differ from the Calvinists, who admit a Presence in the sacrament to those who believe? I do not think I differ from them except where they differ from themselves. I no more suppose our spirits can perceive a spiritual object without faith than that blind eyes can see a natural one. Faith is the exercise in which the spirit lives, as sight is the exercise in which the eye lives. What more does the Calvinist require? He requires that we suppose no object is present unless something perceives it; and having fallen into that contradiction, he goes on to suppose that faith is not a receptive but a creative power, making the thing it believes. We have seen how strong the tendency to this belief has been among all Protestants, but also that the Calvinist’s creed had characteristics that should have especially saved him from it. His principle is to refer everything to the will of God and suppose that nothing originates with the creature. How then did he fall into a hypothesis so foreign to his deepest convictions? He was driven into it by his habit of resolving belief in the Divine Will into the doctrine of individual Election. He cannot suppose God has any higher end in His manifestations than redeeming and sanctifying particular men. So the idea of the God-Manhood, of God manifesting Himself in His Son, shrinks into a mere expedient for showing mercy to the favoured members of the race. By necessary consequence, the belief that God has devised a means by which men, as members of a body, may apprehend the Head of that body is lost in this strange attempt to conceive a presence that is not a presence till we make it so. Still, it is a curious fact that the form and principle of Calvin’s doctrine, as distinct from his system, was mainly upheld by his faith in this sacrament; and that when his followers drew nearer and nearer to the Zuinglian doctrine on this subject, as they inevitably did, the system became more prominent and exclusive.

It is a great transition from either of these views to the Lutheran, which so unequivocally asserts the actual presence of Christ as the ground of faith and not grounded upon it. It might seem that I must now change my approach: having asserted this presence against those who deny it, I must, if I find any difference with these Protestants, warn against carrying a true principle too far. But I shall make no such change, and I see no such danger. I complain of the Lutheran, as of the Zuinglian and the Calvinist, for seeking the deliverance of the individual conscience as an ultimate end, and so failing to acknowledge the completeness of the blessing Christ has given His Church. Whatever logical perplexity the Lutheran has fallen into, whatever violence he has done the understanding by his theory, whatever confusion he has introduced between the sensible and spiritual worlds, comes, I think, from not taking the language of our Lord and His Apostles in a sufficiently plain and literal sense. Our Lord says: “This is my body.” St. Paul addresses the Ephesian converts as sitting in the heavenly places with Christ, and tells the Philippians their bodies shall be made like Christ’s glorious body. Surely this is Christianity: the Gospel of the deliverance of spirit, soul, and body from every fetter that keeps each from its proper function and from its right relation to the others. And this emancipation is connected with, and follows from, our union as members of one body with Christ, the crucified, risen, glorified Lord of our race. Now if these are the privileges of Christian men, and if this sacrament asserts and realises them, what a low notion it is that we are invited to hold communion not with Christ as He is, not with His body exalted at God’s right hand, but with a body consubstantiated in the elements.

Think of the freedom and the fellowship of hope, not only compatible with humiliation and fear but inseparable from them, that come from intercourse with the Prince and Forerunner. He has actually broken through the barriers of space and time; His body was subjected to the events and sufferings of mortality; and He is now glorified with the glory He had with the Father before the worlds were, and will be manifested in the sight of quick and dead. Set these thoughts beside the words “This is my body” and the command to show forth His death till He come, and then reflect, if you can, on the logical dogma of Consubstantiation, the notion that all these blessings somehow dwell in the bread and wine. What we need is that bread and wine become a perfectly transparent medium through which His glory is manifested, so that the spirit of the worshippers beholds nothing but Him into whose presence they are brought. For this the elements need a solemn consecration by the priest, through whom Christ distributes them to His flock. It is not that they may be clothed with new attributes or acquire some essential, miraculous virtue, but that they may be diverted from ordinary uses and become purely sacramental. No doubt the world is full of sacraments: morning and evening, the kind looks and parting words of friends, the laugh of childhood, daily bread, sickness and death all have a holy sacramental meaning and should be seen as such. But they have another meaning that keeps this out of sight. If we want them translated to us, we need some pure, untroubled element with no significance except as the organ through which God’s voice speaks to man and man may answer: “Thy servant heareth.” We believe this bread and wine are such when redeemed to His service. Let us not deprive them of their ethereal whiteness and clearness by the colours of our fancy or the clouds of our intellect.

Rationalistic Objections.

The philosophical objections to the Eucharist in our day usually run something like this. “The Christian mysteries are plainly a continuation of the mysteries that were so important in pagan worship, adapted to new circumstances. It would be wrong to say that either has no meaning or is merely priestcraft’s invention. The Samothracian worship certainly expressed, to its participants and to the Greeks generally, something deep and awful that lay beyond the senses and ordinary experience. The priests made use of the feelings about the invisible that are so strangely woven into our nature, sometimes for good and sometimes for evil. While their own faith lasted, they helped keep alive what was good in their countrymen. The harm came when they went on practising the rites long after any value could be attached to them. That produced insincerity in the priests, and growing superstition and debasement in those who looked up to them. Then the old religion passed away, and after a period of mere scepticism some new one, suited to the world’s stage of progress, had to be introduced, and it too must have its mysteries. They were bound to go through the same process: first honestly received as symbols of what men’s hearts and consciences dreamed of, then sinking into impostures. To speak of them now as if they had any reality or significance is deceitful. In intelligent people it is consciously so. In others it is the mad fanaticism that tries to galvanize what has long been dead.”

Few will deny that this statement is plausible and contains some undoubted truths. The danger is twofold. Some, shocked at the conclusion, will not dare to ask what truths are hidden in it. They will go away uncomfortably aware of being only half honest in a service they feel they cannot give up. Others, taking no more trouble to test assertions that come with such an air of wisdom, will adopt them as the explanation of difficulties that seem half historical and half personal. They hope that if the difficulties can be cleared out of history, they will stop perplexing us in our own lives. So let me concede at once: 1st, that there is a point of connexion between the Christian mysteries and those of the old world. 2ndly, that the old priests did partly keep alive in the worshippers a sense of what is true and unchangeable, and partly sanctify and perpetuate the passing notions and limited knowledge of their own or an earlier stage of civilisation. 3rdly, that they did, as reported, gradually become deceivers of others and of themselves. 4thly, that neither of these evil tendencies was confined to the heathen world; both have appeared just as strongly in Christian Europe, and have attached themselves especially to the sacrament we are discussing. 5thly, that none of us is free from either tendency now, and we are as liable to them as our forefathers in any age. 6thly, that the temptation to those galvanic experiments on obsolete customs has been strong at all times and may be particularly strong now. I make all these concessions without hesitation, and will now examine them in detail.

1st, no one pretends that the resemblance between the old mysteries and the Christian ones lies in similar rites or practices. According to these philosophers it lies in the deep acknowledgment, in every age, of something the senses cannot grapple with, which is most awful and necessary for men. Both parties admit that this feeling belongs to the most permanent part of our being, that it cannot satisfy itself, and that the deepest principle of every faith and every society must be mysterious. That much is involved in saying that the mysteries of Eleusis have something corresponding to them in the Christian Church, and as far as I can see, no more than that.

2ndly, the ancient priest and the ancient worshipper were confused in this way. He believed in an awful Being above man, whom the senses cannot know, and he believed in an outward universe that speaks to the senses. Were that Being and this world distinct or the same? Did he stand apart in his own awfulness, or was he to be seen in outward forms, or recognized in the hidden powers and life of the universe? Was he nearer to man, as the heroic ages believed, or to the world in which man lives? Nothing could solve these puzzles, and the confused, indefinite character of the mysteries expressed them well. Hence the mixture, so often mentioned, of a permanent faith with passing notions. The physical world was unexplored. Observations, which naturally varied from region to region, were the groundwork of all study of it; they were generalised into theories, and the theories became part of the priests’ theology. Its nature was therefore fixed by locality and changeable as experience grew. But because it was grafted onto what could not change, it was treated as equally sacred, and the two flourished and suffered together. Hence—

3rdly, we can explain the insincerity that marked the later priests as against the earlier. The moral side of the worship, the reverence for what the inner man desires, and the affections and sympathies that can be given only to what is personal, were strongest in the infancy of nations. Beneath these feelings lay a sense of the absolute and unapproachable, though it was not yet brought into distinct consciousness. The physical notions attached to these acknowledgments of moral relations were simple, drawn directly from simple observation. In time the moral feelings grew weaker and baser, while the observed facts and the inferences from them multiplied. The remnants of ancient faith, and still more of ancient fear, became inextricably mixed with these. Human desires and sympathies attached themselves to visible things and embodied themselves in them. These became the real objects of worship, in spite of a constant struggling conviction that they were not meant to be, and that the inherited ceremonies did not properly fit them. Meanwhile the philosopher widened his outward observation. He felt an impenetrable depth and an indefinable extent in the world around him, and he detected inconsistencies in his countrymen’s anthropomorphic notions. Having crushed his own human longings and lost his sympathy with individual men, he began to speak of the world as the one great mystery. What he said in abstract language was really the habit of thought of the whole age. The priest secretly confesses it as his own too. But from fear, or affection and reverence, or an honest conviction that he has something the philosopher lacks, or mere ignorance, or all of these together, or lower motives still, he clings to the old forms and language. He clings the harder because he doubts whether one part of them is any less insecure than another, and so dreads that losing any part will mean losing the whole. The people meanwhile feel wants that the philosopher ignores, and they know he despises them. They have no guides left to anything higher and nobler than their own conceits, and these conceits must be gratified at all costs. The priest tries to gratify them, and sinks lower himself while dragging them down.

4thly, any student of modern history can see the parallel between heathendom and Christendom in both these cases. In the first ages, the deep thoughts about God’s being, unity, and relations to men that I spoke of in a former section mingled with speculations about the outward world and its creatures, and sometimes almost lost themselves in them. These speculations came from no revealed authority and rested on no careful study or experiment or satisfactory data; they were mostly the product of mythological or philosophical tradition. But men whose souls were wholly taken up with divine contemplation could not separate them from what is divine. They felt that God must be the author of the outward world, that it must be made for his glory, and that it holds marvellous types of the spiritual. They also believed that the visible and invisible had been brought into close and inseparable union by the incarnation of the Son of God, and that every part of their own lives and of creation was to be shaped by the New Life that had been manifested. They could not see how to hold these principles and yet not give a certain sanctity to their own conceptions of the universe. Then the world of the Fathers collapsed, and new forms of society grew up in Western Europe. By God’s merciful providence, much of the treasure of past times was hidden from the Latin and Teutonic nations, so that nothing would hinder them from following their own appointed course of discipline. That discipline led them into investigations the Fathers had only partly entered, or had kept entirely subordinate to the higher theology: investigations into the nature of man and the laws and conditions under which he exists and acts. The schoolmen pursued these with earnest and holy feelings, and, I think, with really beneficial results within their own sphere. Yet they led to greater confusion in both theology and physics, because both were viewed through the forms and colours of the human intellect. The invisible relations that heart and reason acknowledge, and the visible things the eye perceives, were alike subjected to our conceptions and theories and treated as inseparable from them. In this sacrament the results were so striking that they are a clue to those in every other direction. In the Fathers’ age there was often a blending of physical and spiritual language. It generally came not from doubting the distinct reality of the unseen, but from a desire to clothe the outward universe with some of its glory. In the middle age, all such expressions had to be stiffened into a theory. Logic had to fuse theological idea and physical notion inseparably, and claim dominion over both. It did not matter how much the understanding recoiled at its own invention. The dogma was established, the sacrament meant transubstantiation, and anyone who held the institution sacred had to accept the opinion at all costs.

5thly, given this state of things, it is no wonder that for a time faith in the sacrament, as a witness of real communion between man and his Maker, could uphold the notion attached to it. Nor is it a wonder that later the extravagance of the notion helped destroy a faith already weakening from other causes; that the priests made desperate efforts to keep both alive together; that they used arguments which strengthened the evil part of their scheme and obscured its purer element; and that the result in their own minds and their flocks’ was ever-increasing insincerity. We must not suppose the Reformation stopped these effects, or that afterwards they were confined to those who stayed in communion with Rome. Every great shaking brings out what is true and sound in men’s hearts, and also makes the untruth of those who have willingly yielded to it more palpable and more dominant. The first effect, I have no doubt, appeared in the hearts of many Romanists. They were thrown back on their higher moral principles, which they thought the new doctrines attacked, and which could not be sacrificed to intellectual puzzles and contradictions. On the other hand, the decrees of the Council of Trent must have led those who accepted them to identify the formula with the principle more consciously and directly, and the same cause would stir the reformed bodies to invent some opposing formula. While the controversies these attempts awakened went on in religious circles, men in general became occupied with the new class of thoughts I mentioned in my first part. The method by which the outward world had been investigated was shown to be unworkable. Men were taught to study the world in itself without imputing their own conceptions to it. The new method was rewarded with remarkable discoveries, and gradually, as I observed, a pursuit that had produced and promised such great results took the place of every other. This alone was held to rest on sure data, and any other region could be examined only by those data. Naturally, all the physical mixtures that had intruded into theology were scornfully rejected; that was the first step. The next was disbelief in the very forms and conceptions of the mind that had so darkened the face of nature. Romanist theology was interwoven with both physics and logic, and Reformation theology, less with physics but even more with the forms of the understanding. So an almost unavoidable suspicion of both grew up. That suspicion might have been profitable, as in the end I trust it will prove, if it had not been accompanied by a complete disbelief in spiritual realities. Moral corruption was the primary cause of that disbelief, and the pride of physical speculation only the secondary. This incredulity found its way into the hearts of the priests of both communions. In some it produced a general latitudinarianism: a willingness to abandon all ancient forms and tolerate any language, because there seemed to be no truth any of them pointed to. In others it produced a stubborn clinging to everything that had been identified with theological principles, whether by old tradition or modern innovation. The root of these evils was the same in the third age as in the first: God and the world were confounded. What the spirit of man demands for its satisfaction, what humanity seeks as its object, was identified with the visible things the spirit is meant to rule, and in which humanity is meant to see the image of realities greater than itself. So the mysteries of Christianity were treated differently in each age. The first age tried to connect them with misunderstood earthly things, to glorify those things. The second connected them with the forms and conceptions of our own minds, through which physical things had been viewed. In the third age they were declared wholly meaningless by the wise and prudent, who had learnt the right method of studying nature and the impositions the mind practises on itself. They were held on to with a loving but trembling faith by the poor and childlike, who, amid all perplexities, will not give up what their hearts tell them they need.

6thly, I have now traced the causes and progress of the confusion common to the history of the pagan and Christian worlds. The question is who is likely to fall into it in our day, and how it can be avoided. I do not deny that we, the priests of the Christian covenant, are in danger of it. We hear denials on every side. We are told that things are obsolete which we feel were never so needed as now. We are told that whatever is objective is worthless, while history shows that men’s subjective notions and fancies have brought every kind of perplexity into the world. We see a constant inclination to reject what seems to us solid and precious, and we are warned that it will grow without limit. What is more natural than to throw ourselves back on the past and commit ourselves to what we take to be firm resistance to the spirit of the age? We would try not only to keep what we have but to recover what we have lost. We might count among our losses the early apprehensions of the physical world that belong to the infancy of society and the logical systems of its boyhood, and think the effort to regain them a proof of our reverence for God’s sacraments and an acknowledgment of their permanent, real character. Such feelings are most natural, and just so far as we yield to them we shall certainly try to galvanize the habits and notions of a bygone period.

But why should we not yield to them? I answer: because in doing so we show that we are not free from the spirit of the age but infected by it, and that we are not impressed with the permanence and reality of God’s sacraments but have given in to the prevailing scepticism about them. For what is the spirit of the age, as it appears in the philosophers whose objections we are considering? I have tried, in an earlier part of this work, to show that no one is so disposed as they are to confound God with the world, and to look at this visible universe, with its mysterious powers and properties, as the real Being, or at least its greatest manifestation. This pantheistic tendency is especially ours at this time. In all past times it has been the source of the confusion between the permanent and the transitory, the essential and the accidental, which we are rightly told to beware of. So Christian priests will not be the only galvanizers. For the most remarkable galvanic experiments on record we are indebted to the philosophers of the age after Christianity was proclaimed, to Plotinus, Porphyry, and Jamblichus. They tried to revive the old pagan forms, expressly to give a body to their philosophy, which they felt otherwise lacked one, and to resist the progress of the new kingdom. There are signs of the same inclination among us now, and we shall see more of them. Pantheism has never existed, and never will, in the naked, essential character it claims. It will beget idolatries, and since man’s imagination has nearly exhausted itself in producing them, they will be old, not new. How can they be withstood? I believe in no way so effectively as by putting forth this sacrament simply: not clothed in fantastic rites and emblems, but in its own dreadful grandeur. It is the bond of communion between heaven and earth. It witnesses that man is not a creature of this world but has his home and citizenship in another. It witnesses that his spirit is not the function or creature of his body, and so need not draw its enjoyments from what the eye sees and the ear hears; his body is the attendant and servant of his spirit, to be raised by it and to bring all visible things under it. And it witnesses that the Son of man is seated at the right hand of the Throne of God, and that those who believe in him and suffer with him are meant to live and reign with him there. The forms of nature and the forms of the understanding have tried to reduce this sacrament to their own level. It remains a mighty power in God’s hands, to raise man above those forms into communion with himself.

The Romish System.

After the remarks under the last head, it may hardly seem necessary to defend what I have said about this sacrament against the charge of Romanism. But I have maintained that the Eucharistic feast is sacrificial, that Christ is really present in it, and that the words of institution are to be taken literally. What I have said also implies that a certain order of persons first received the sacramental elements and that a certain order should administer them now. So, even at the risk of some repetition, I should show where I differ from the Romish theory on each of these points.

1. I need only ask the reader to compare what I said about the Catholic and Romish ideas of Baptism and of worship with what I have said in this section about sacrifice. He will see the principle governing all three. I complained that, in the Romish theory, the baptized man receives only a momentary gift and is not admitted into a permanent state, and that the worshipper is buying some future benefit by his devotion rather than claiming a blessing already bought for him. He must act in line with these maxims when he deals with the sacrifice that is the foundation of the Christian’s state and the completion of his worship. So he regards the Eucharistic sacrifice as the means of obtaining what Christ’s sacrifice has not fully procured, or what we have lost through our sins and negligence. This is the view commonly given by Romish writers, and it is the view the Reformers attacked. My doctrine is that this feast gets its sacrificial character from the fact that a complete sacrifice has been offered for man. That is more formally and practically opposed to Romanism than the doctrine now prevalent. There is no formal opposition between denying that a Eucharistic sacrifice exists at all and affirming that it carries out an incomplete sacrifice made by Christ. The two contradict each other but cannot be compared; each gains strength from the other’s denial, while their supporters can hardly say what each asserts or by what test they could be judged. Nor is there a practical opposition. Protestants keep losing sight of Christ’s finished sacrifice in their anxiety to stress human faith. Romanists strain by an effort of memory, aided by visible images, to bring back the very event of the crucifixion with all its circumstances. So the one side unconsciously confesses that there must be acts of ours in which the blessing is realised, and the other that all virtue lies in the one sacrifice and not in our repetition of it. I maintain that acknowledging the sacrament as the sacrificial feast of the new dispensation realises and harmonises these two truths. It satisfies the meaning the Romanist feels he cannot give up, and so lets him throw aside, as degrading, dangerous and antichristian, the doctrine that has been among the greatest barriers between him and his Protestant brethren.

2. The same habit of mind that produced this view of the Eucharistic sacrifice explains why the Church, after hard struggles, accepted transubstantiation. I have found the intellectual origin of this dogma in scholastic philosophy, but that philosophy could never have given it currency without a moral predisposition in men’s minds. The Middle Ages were marked, and made strong, by a cry for some signal proof of God’s condescension to our low estate, and a sense of weakness that only a mighty act of divine humiliation could meet. The belief that man’s spirit was to be raised out of its grave of sense and made capable of actual communion with the invisible and the absolute hovered about many minds and was conveyed in many emblems and enigmas. Some earnest, thoughtful men actually grasped it, but it never entered the practical life of the period. Many wished to show forth acts of bravery, condescension and sacrifice, and so glorify God, and a few aimed to inspire others with the same ambition. But everywhere one can trace the wish to see God’s likeness in visible things under earthly conditions, rather than the longing to see Him as He is. I need not ask whether good or evil prevailed in this temper. Most will admit it was very imperfect, and only resolutely one-sided inquirers deny that its imperfection exposed it to gross sensuality. In such a state of mind, the thought of communion with Christ where He is could not be as clearly present to the best men at their best moments as it may now be to indifferent men who barely realise their own vision. So a substitute for that faith was eagerly welcomed: a way of believing Christ present by a fresh act of descent into the circumstances of human nature, with the understanding’s objections swept aside. I think all church history shows what sensuality and death grew out of this notion and were fostered by it, what profaneness entered the speculations it prompted, and how it joined with every other shape of idolatry. But I do not believe the demonstration will be heeded. Facts will be perverted or explained away, and the natural results of a system condemned as if they had nothing to do with it, unless men are led to see that there is a spiritual truth this doctrine has been counterfeiting and hiding. To that truth it is far more directly opposed than it can ever be to the Protestant and infidel notions set up against it.

3. From these remarks, and from all I have said in this section, it is plain that I do not try to dispose of the papal notion of a real Presence merely by saying that what is spiritual is also most real. I regard that proposition as nearly the most important a theological student can remember, and as the one Romanism most habitually denies. But I have maintained that fully acknowledging Christ’s spiritual presence requires us to acknowledge that He is clothed with a body. If we lose that belief, we adopt a vague pantheistic notion of a presence hovering somewhere in the air, instead of a clear spiritual apprehension of a Person in whom all truth and love dwell. The spiritual organ therefore demands an actual body for its nourishment, and through it our own bodies are meant to be purified and glorified. This sacrament meets the needs both of the redeemed human spirit and of the body still waiting for redemption. But these admissions only sharpen my difference with the Romanist. A Christian who seeks a real, not imaginary, object, and wants his body as well as his spirit raised, must desire fellowship with Christ as He is: ascended at God’s right hand, in a body of glory and not of humiliation. On this ground he must reject every theory that imagines a descent into the elements. And he must feel that the intellectual contradiction such theories contain, and even boast of, is the counterpart of a spiritual contradiction still more gross and dangerous.

4. Before I conclude I must say a few words on how my views differ from the Romanists’ about those who administer this sacrament. The pure Protestant says that the Romanist, unhappily linking sacrifice with the Eucharist, must suppose that the Christian Church has priests as the Jewish one did, and that he himself, rejecting the first idea, rejects the second. But I have carefully linked sacrifice with the Eucharist. It follows that if I suppose it administered by human hands at all, I must suppose those hands in some sense sacerdotal. Indeed, if the Jewish sacrifice has passed into something higher, it seems almost necessarily to follow that the Jewish priesthood has too. And that is my belief. I think a Melchisedec priesthood has succeeded to an Aaronical one, as the power of an endless life has succeeded to the law of a carnal commandment. I think the man who presents the perfect sacrifice to God, and himself and his people as redeemed by it, has a higher function than the one who presented the daily offering or made the yearly atonement. I think the man permitted to feed the people with this bread and wine has a higher work than the one who came out of the holy place to bless the people in God’s name. And I complain of the Romanists for lowering this office, stripping it of its spiritual and Catholic character, and reducing it to the level of what existed before the incarnation, or below it. No honour given to the priest’s person can make up for the degradation of being treated as if he stood outside the veil, pleading for admission to God’s presence instead of claiming it for himself and his people. No emblems of his own mysterious glory and beauty can make up for losing the belief that he may behold his Lord’s glory with open face. Above all, the distinctions made between him and his flock show that he is not like his Lord, not one of many brethren, but has only the melancholy pleasure of imagining blessings reserved for him that others do not share. The grossest and most offensive of these, however it is defended, is letting him alone receive the sacramental wine. In this he is far below the Jew. The high-priest believed he was one of a kingdom of priests, and that he received his garments of beauty and his holy mitre as their representative. Answering Korah’s complaint, “Ye take too much upon you, seeing that all the congregation are holy, every one of them,” a Jew would have said: ‘We take on us what has been put on us because the congregation is holy, and it would not be holy if we were not consecrated to be witnesses and preservers of its holiness.’ A Jew could see that the oil on Aaron’s head ran down to the skirts of his garments. Surely it is not for Christians and Catholics to set up an office in the Church against the Church itself, and to set at naught the ascription they are appointed to offer in the name of the whole body: “Unto Him that loved us and washed us from our sins in His own blood, and hath made us kings and priests unto God and His Father; to Him be glory and dominion for ever and for ever.” But I am intruding on the subject of the next section.
