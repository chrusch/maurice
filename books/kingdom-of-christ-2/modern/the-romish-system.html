The Romish System

Romanism, the enemy of nations as it was the corrupter of creeds and sacraments, calls for reform, not abandonment: whoever stays in the Church of his fathers and holds it to be a Church will be delivered from popery.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

I will conclude this part of my work with a few remarks on the charge that I underrate the guilt and punishment of Romish apostasy. A reader who has followed my last chapter and this one will hardly suspect me of going easier on Romanism when it appears as the sworn enemy of nations and national Churches than when it appeared as the corrupter of creeds and sacraments. One evil seems to imply the other. The same assumption that made it an uncatholic principle made it an anti-national one: it perverted the idea of spiritual power, and so interfered with civil power. Yet from another side, what we commonly call the Church of Rome has given, and still gives, a striking witness that Christ’s Church is the kingdom, and not merely a collection of sects held together by particular dogmas. I have never concealed this, for no fact that concerns the history and government of the world ought to be concealed.

But what do we mean by the Church of Rome? I mean the diocese over which the Bishop of Rome presides, and I know no other. Others may have invented a different notion of it, but I do not adopt it; if I did, I should be adopting the Romish system. This Italian diocese may be in a very corrupt state, and I am afraid it is. Many who belong to it and acknowledge its Bishop’s jurisdiction would admit as much. They must therefore agree with us that it should be reformed. Perhaps they would also agree that the reformation will likely bring many punishments and judgments for its members’ sins. I have partly considered the nature and roots of those sins in this chapter. The nature of the judgments I am not competent to consider, nor do I wish to. They may be heavier or lighter than those that will fall on other parts of the Church; they may even leave Rome a prey to some infidel power. That question is in the best hands, and we must leave it there, as we may leave the fate of the Spanish, the Gallican, or any other Church.

But the end of these judgments, however tremendous, will, I believe, be the destruction of a false, apostate system. You say the system is part and parcel of the Church, so that if one perishes the other must. That is just what I know nothing about, and you know nothing about. But I do know that as long as a man is alive and struggling, I have no business to say that he and his disease are identical, or that curing the disease must kill the patient. Here I am at issue with our modern interpreters of prophecy. I do not differ from them by hating the Romish system less. It often seems to me that they do not hate it enough, that they do not see where its extreme evil lies, and that they tolerate some of its evil in themselves. I find this lack especially in what they say about the Latin Church. They do not see that popery is continually undermining the Church, and so they do not feel that the more you persuade men to be Churchmen, the more effectively you deliver them from popery. They cry to members of the Latin Churches, “Come ye out of Babylon, and be ye separate.” Taken as Scripture gives them, and as the whole context explains them, no words are more important. But how are they to be obeyed? The usual answer is, “By leaving the corrupt Church you belong to.” It seems to me that whoever does so is very likely to carry the Babylonian system with him and leave behind only the good elements that were mixed with it. But whoever stays in the Church of his fathers, insisting that it is a Church, and that those who have struggled to strip it of its distinct ecclesiastical character are his enemies and must be resisted, will, I think, arrive at deliverance from popery. He will not be delivered as the Protestant nations were in the sixteenth century. God has a different method for freeing His servants in each age, but I cannot see why this one should be less effective; I hope and trust it will be far more so. At the Reformation the serpent was scotched, not killed, and it could not be killed while anything good remained in it. Once the belief in Christ’s kingdom is separated from this system, once men believe they are not necessary to each other, the moral power of the papacy is gone. What does it matter then if all the physical power in the world is granted to it for a time, if kings send it presents, if every form of infidelity and false worship combines with it? The Church may then confidently take up the prophet’s words: “Associate yourselves, and ye shall be broken in pieces; gird yourselves, and ye shall be broken in pieces; take counsel together, and it shall come to naught, for God is with us.”

Footnote. I had developed at some length, for each of the European nations, the idea hinted at in this section. My aim was to show that everywhere on the Continent there are signs of a struggle that phrases like “democratical tendencies,” “dissatisfaction with old opinions,” and “commencement of a new era” explain very imperfectly. It may include all these, but anyone who overlooks the relation between Catholicism and Nationality will understand it very little, and will miss that the history of modern Europe has been one continual effort to establish or to break that relation. I had inquired further whether this question has lost its application to the United States of North America, or whether it will take precedence of every other there too. But I found I was being led almost unawares into dreams of the future, which may be meant for each of these nations, and such dreams seemed hardly fitting in a book that aims at discovering what is solid and certain. The lesson of the whole is in the sad, consoling dirge the ancient poets sang, now the chant of the whole Church militant: “Cry woe, woe, but let the good prevail.”
