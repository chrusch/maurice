What is Revelation?

Sermons on the Epiphany, with Letters to a Theological Student on Mr. Mansel's Bampton Lectures

First published
1859
Length
121,000 words, about 9 hours' reading

Against the growing skepticism of the nineteenth century, Maurice argues that revelation is not merely information about God, but God’s own self-disclosure in Jesus Christ. God does not simply tell us about Himself—He makes Himself known.

The first half of the book consists of seven sermons showing how Christ reveals the Father through His life, miracles, parables, and dealings with ordinary people. The second half is a series of letters responding to Henry Mansel’s Bampton Lectures on the limits of religious thought, defending the possibility of truly knowing God while insisting that He always remains infinitely greater than our understanding.

Contents

The one-line summaries are guides drafted with the help of AI, not Maurice’s words.

  1. Preface

    These sermons and letters answer Mr. Mansel’s Bampton Lectures, questioning what a merely ‘regulative’ Revelation leaves for a preacher to proclaim, and appeal to troubled young men rather than learned doctors.

  2. Contents
  3. Sermon I. The Magians

    The star that leads the Magi and Paul’s preaching to the Ephesians are one law in different forms: God unveils his Son to the inner spirit, satisfying every nation’s craving for his glory.

  4. Sermon II. Christ among the Doctors

    The boy Christ, hearing and questioning the doctors in the Temple, reaches beneath their commentaries to the plain sense of their own sacred words, and shows them a power mightier than the letter they served.

  5. Sermon III. St. Paul at Athens

    Paul’s sermon on Mars’ Hill affirms, against the safer method of denouncing pagan error, that the Athenians are already God’s offspring, near to a Father they have only dimly sought.

  6. Sermon IV. The Miracles

    Christ’s miracles, from the water turned to wine to the healing of the leper, are not proofs demanding blind assent but disclosures of the Father’s grace, to be tested by whether they bear his likeness.

  7. Sermon V. Casting out the Evil Spirit

    No law or chain can tame the Gadarene demoniac; only Christ’s direct claim upon his spirit heals him, a deliverance repeated in Paul’s conversion and in every reformation since.

  8. Sermon VI. Christ’s Parables

    Christ’s parables do not conceal divine mysteries behind homely images but unveil them, so that ordinary things such as seed, shepherd, and father become visible witnesses to anyone whose inner eye and ear are open.

  9. Sermon VII. Practice and Speculation

    Hoping for Christ’s coming manifestation is no idle speculation but the very ground of practical holiness, since knowing God as a calling Father, not a rulebook, is what makes obedience possible.

    1. Note
  10. Letters to a Theological Student Preparing for Orders on Mr. Mansel’s Bampton Lectures

    Before accepting Mr. Mansel’s argument that God cannot be known, count the cost: Thomas à Kempis, the Puritan divines, Augustine, and Luther must all be discarded, and the Prayer-book’s own words become dishonest.

  11. Letter I. The Cost of Confutation
  12. Letter II. Mr. Mansel’s Preface.—Sir William Hamilton

    Sir William Hamilton’s doctrine that the Unconditioned is unknowable, the philosophical ground beneath Mansel’s argument, is undercut by his own notes, which betray a hunger after the Absolute his logic condemns as madness.

  13. Letter III. Mr. Mansel’s Preface.—Butler

    Bishop Butler’s Analogy, unlike Hamilton’s theorizing, is a practical witness to a real moral constitution, able to guide the convicted sinner, the seeker of a true Church, and the scientist alike.

  14. Letter IV. Dogmatism and Rationalism

    Mansel’s charges of Dogmatism and Rationalism, tested against Anselm and Jowett, dissolve once God’s actual revelation of himself in the Incarnate Son replaces any verbal compromise between the two errors.

  15. Letter V. The Criterion of Truth

    Against Mansel’s claim that neither Mystic nor Rationalist can judge divine things, a common human faculty for truth, most alive in the plain and unlearned, discerns Christ from imposture.

  16. Letter VI. Struggle for Reality

    Mansel’s proof that the Infinite, the Absolute, and the Cause collapse into self-contradiction reveals only his own devotion to logical terms, not a true defense of the Bible’s plain, unsystematic grasp of fact.

  17. Letter VII. Philosophy of Consciousness

    Schleiermacher’s account of prayer as a feeling of absolute dependence, dismissed by Mansel as incompatible with the Bible’s urgent entreaty, is truer to prayer’s self-forgetting trust than Mansel’s own religious consciousness.

  18. Letter VIII. Appearances and Realities

    Contrary to Mansel’s claim that we know only appearances and must settle for rules rather than principles, English experience in religion, morals, trade, medicine, and art everywhere presses past phenomena toward abiding realities.

  19. Letter IX. Christian Mysteries and Miracles

    The name of Father, Son, and Spirit, which Mansel defends as merely an unresolvable puzzle, is the ground of family, nation, and church, and Christ’s miracles restore order rather than disturb it.

  20. Letter X. Morality and Evil

    Treating human morality and forgiveness as no true likeness of God’s own nature, and allowing that evil may last forever, Mansel undermines the gospel’s promise that Christ’s sacrifice has begun to overcome sin for good.

  21. Letter XI. Evidences of the Bible

    The Bible’s real evidence lies not in Paley’s one-sided external proofs but in its history of satisfying human need, obscured when Mansel treats the Canaanite wars as exceptions to God’s order rather than its expression.

About this digital edition

How the text was made, and the printer's errors corrected

The original book

What is Revelation? by Frederick Denison Maurice.

This edition

Produced 2026-09-26 from a page scan of the printed book, as part of a project to make F. D. Maurice’s works freely available in every useful digital form. The guiding principle throughout was fidelity to the printed text: Maurice’s own spelling, capitalization, punctuation, ligatures, and typography rule, except where a printing error was identified and deliberately emended (each such change is listed below).

How this text was made

The transcription pipeline combines automated tools, AI models, and page-by-page human review:

  • OCR and conversion. The scan was OCRed (ocrmypdf/Tesseract) and converted to structured text (marker), then cleaned by a language model (claude-haiku-4-5-20251001) working chunk by chunk.
  • Human review of every machine change. Every word the cleanup model altered was reviewed against an image of the printed line. Footnotes were verified against the printed page and renumbered uniformly.
  • Quotation marks were normalized to the printer’s own conventions and validated by a nesting checker; every remaining irregularity was inspected against the page image. The final text has fully paired quotation marks.
  • Italics were restored (the OCR text layer records none) by a vision model (claude-sonnet-5) cross-checked against a deterministic measurement of letterform slant on the page images, with per-word statistical calibration derived from the book itself. Every page was then reviewed by a human editor against the print. This edition carries 649 italic spans.
  • Small capitals and ligatures (æ, œ) are reproduced as printed.
  • A whole-book audit (claude-sonnet-5) checked structure, headings, footnotes, and Greek; its findings were reviewed by a human editor against the print before any were applied.

The text contains 26 footnotes and 0 small-caps renderings of the print.
