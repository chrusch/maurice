[Prefixed to the Second Edition, 1842.]

This work first appeared in 1838 as Letters to a Member of the Society of Friends. A controversy then dividing the Quaker Society prompted it. My main aim was to ask whether accepting the spiritual principles the Quakers professed meant rejecting Christian ordinances, or whether the one necessarily implied the other. This was almost the same question as another. The Quakers had tried to set up a spiritual kingdom in the world. Did such a Kingdom not exist already, and were the ordinances not its expression?

Among many smaller but serious mistakes, I found one that had made my purpose hard to understand. The early Quakers said the Spiritual Kingdom was bounded by no nation. Yet the Quaker Society has existed only in England and America. I wanted to show my Quaker correspondent a spiritual body in which he could find a home when the sect no longer gave him one. So in every Letter I naturally referred to the English Church, and to her sacraments, ministers and forms of worship. Many therefore took me to be writing a defense of that Church. If so, they asked, how had I kept my promise to show the Quakers a universal Church, such as they had dreamed of?

I also found that members of my own communion read the book far more than Quakers did. Some friends suggested that a new edition should become a Treatise on the Church, with every reference to the sect removed. I could not take this advice. If I have suggested to a Churchman anything he could not find far better put in a hundred other books, I owe it to my comparing our own position with that of those who seem farthest from us. Besides, if I lost sight of the Quakers, I would give up one means of repairing my error. By following their line of thought we may learn how earnest men have come to feel that they need a Catholic Church, and not merely a National one.

Still, though I cannot stop connecting my hints with Quaker principles, I must almost entirely omit the particular controversy that led me to address them. It seems to be over, and the Society is more likely to die by slow decay than by sudden convulsion. For this reason, and for some private ones, the form of Letters no longer fits, so I have rewritten and reconstructed the book, hoping partly to remove the fault I have described. I have also tried to remove some of the grosser errors, whether I found them myself or others pointed them out. Enough remain, no doubt, and my additions may have brought in more. I hope the book will be taken as nothing but a collection of Hints, which, if they lead the reader to deeper thought and greater reverence, may soon make him far wiser than his teacher.

The Dialogue that follows explains how the subject of the whole book connects with the history of the Quaker body. Its form is imaginary, but I have often voiced the same sentiments to members of the Society. The Quaker’s account of the trials, from within and without, that beset a thoughtful man who finds a sectarian position no longer tenable may, I believe, be called an “ower true tale.” I should add that the Archbishop of Dublin has lately published a work called “The Kingdom of Christ Delineated.” Out of respect for so distinguished an author, and for selfish reasons too, I would have changed my title once I knew it had been taken. But the description of my book is closely tied to its intention, and this edition fits the name more strictly than the former one did. So I must keep it.
