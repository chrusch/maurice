Notes to Vol. I

Six notes take up points from the text: early teachers reasoning with catechumens and heretics through love, John’s Logos against Tittmann, Socrates in the Clouds, Philo’s divine Word, the Greek Fathers’ indwelling Word, and the future of the Reformation churches.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Note (A), p. 12

1. In the text the Quaker says he believes that the catechumens in the primitive Church were not taught something wholly different in kind from what they learnt after baptism. He also believes they were not reasoned with on the selfish motives that their later initiation must have aimed to cure. Many passages may well be found in the Fathers to refute this, showing that they sometimes did use selfish arguments, on the plea that pure things are meant for the pure. The practice is so plausible, and flatters our spiritual pride so much, that it has prevailed in all later ages, so we must expect its seeds in the earliest. From the sound principle that milk is for babes and meat for grown men, an economist might easily pass to the notion that some constitutions are not even fit for milk, and that to them some liquid from the muddy streams of the world, or distilled from its poisonous herbs, may fitly be given. But I think Augustine’s short treatise “On Catechizing the Uninstructed” shows that this was not the habitual and deliberate opinion of those ages. He wrote it for a Carthaginian deacon who complained that he found it hard to keep his own mind cheerful and free while catechizing. What Augustine says on that subject deserves the attention of every teacher, in pulpit or schoolroom. But I cite the book for another reason: so that the reader may see in what spirit a bishop of the fourth century would have addressed a young heathen preparing for admission to the Church.

2. Augustine first sets out how the historical parts of Scripture should be shown to the candidate, and how Christ’s coming is the end they all point to. Then he continues: What greater reason is there for the Lord’s coming than that God might show his love for us and commend it strongly, since Christ died for us while we were still enemies? And this is so because love is the end of the commandment and the fulfilling of the law. We are to love one another, and as Christ laid down his life for us, to lay down ours for the brethren. As for God himself, who loved us first and did not spare his only Son but gave him up for us all, if it was a burden to love him, at least it should be no burden now to love him in return. There is no greater invitation to love than to love first, and the heart is too hard that was unwilling to give love and is unwilling to repay it. Even in shameful and sordid loves, those who want to be loved in return do nothing but show by every proof how much they love, and put on an air of justice, as if demanding a return from the hearts they are trying to win. They burn more ardently when they feel those hearts kindled by the same fire. So the sluggish mind is roused when it sees it is loved, and the mind already glowing is set more aflame when it learns its love is returned. Nothing begins or increases love more than this: that one who does not yet love learns he is loved, or that one who loves first hopes for a return or already has proof of it. If this holds in base loves, how much more in friendship! What else do we guard against in offending a friend, except his thinking we do not love him, or love him less than he loves us? If he believes that, he grows cooler in the love of mutual intimacy, and if he is not so weak as to grow cold to all affection, he keeps only the love that loves to be useful, not to enjoy. It is worth noticing, too, that although superiors also wish to be loved by inferiors, delight in their eager service, and love them the more for it, still the inferior is set on fire far more when he finds himself loved by a superior. For love is more welcome when it does not burn from the dryness of need but flows from an abundance of kindness: the one love springs from misery, the other from mercy. And if the inferior had despaired of being loved by a superior, he will be moved beyond words when the superior freely shows how much he loves one who would never have dared hope for so great a good. But what is higher than God as judge, and what more hopeless than man as sinner? Man had given himself over to be protected and ruled by proud powers that cannot make anyone blessed, all the more because he despaired of being cared for by that power which is exalted not by malice but by goodness.

3. Augustine goes on: If Christ came chiefly so that man might know how much God loves him, and knowing it be kindled with love for the One who loved him first, and love his neighbour at his command and by his example (for Christ became a neighbour by loving one who was not a neighbour but a wanderer far away); and if all the divine Scripture written beforehand was written to foretell the Lord’s coming, and whatever was written afterward under divine authority tells of Christ and urges love; then it is clear that not only the Law and the Prophets, which were the only holy Scripture when the Lord spoke, hang on the two commandments of love to God and neighbour, but so does everything divinely written since. Therefore in the Old Testament the New is hidden, and in the New the Old is made plain. Those who followed the hiddenness and understood carnally, then and now, were brought under the yoke of the fear of punishment. Those who follow the revelation and understand spiritually have been set free by the gift of love. In those days these were the ones who knocked in piety and found even hidden things opened to them; now they are those who seek without pride, lest even open things be closed to them.

4. Then he says: Nothing is more opposed to charity than envy, and the mother of envy is pride. So the Lord Jesus Christ, God and man, is both the sign of God’s love for us and the example of human humility among us, so that our great swelling might be healed by a greater remedy of the opposite kind. Great is the misery of proud man, but greater the mercy of a humble God. Then, addressing the deacon: “With this love set before you as the end to which you refer everything you say, whatever you relate, relate it in such a way that the one you speak to may believe by hearing, hope by believing, and love by hoping.”

5. These last words are tolerably decisive as to Augustine’s opinion on how God’s character and purpose, and the object of the Gospel, should be set before those not yet received into his covenant. I think his remarks matter chiefly to missionaries, who act directly on the minds and consciences of heathens. I would never have dreamed of arguing the question for people brought up in a Christian country. Such people believe they are struggling for a higher idea of Christianity than their countrymen generally hold. Their fathers, as I think, actually maintained a fundamental Church principle that was in danger of being forgotten, and passed on at least its form to their descendants. Much of its strength and vitality has gone out of it, I allow, but it is hallowed by old association and reverence. To treat such men as if they stood on the same ground as heathens because they are unbaptized seems to me a wilful denial of what God’s covenant has done in a society it has encompassed for a thousand years, and it is done under pretence of honouring the sign of that covenant. In quoting Augustine, then, I mean only to prove that even on this hypothesis, ancient practice does not warrant us in treating Quakers as if they must be baptized before we can speak with them about the principles and end of the Gospel. Whoever sets the maxim “holy things for the holy” against the way I have reasoned with the Quaker is attacking not me but Augustine.

6. Nor will he do better if he pleads that the heretic, who takes parts of the Gospel and rejects the rest while living under the shadow of the Catholic Church, is worse off than the heathen. On that plea he must be treated as one who has sinfully sacrificed all his powers of spiritual apprehension, and can be appealed to only by arguments addressed to his sensible experience. For then we shall produce Augustine’s two books, “On the Morals of the Catholic Church” and “On the Morals of the Manichæans,” written expressly to convince those seduced by what Augustine knew from his own experience to be the most fatal of all heresies. I mention this work the more readily because many passages in it might lead one to suppose that he would here have adopted the very maxim I am contending against. Speaking of how the Manichæans treated the Old Testament, he says in one place: “It must be said to you: it is not for you to understand these things. You are not unknown to me. You bring minds altogether coarse, sickly from the poisonous diet of bodily images, to judge divine things, which are far loftier than you suppose.” And again: “I could, within my modest ability, go through each point and demonstrate what I have received, things in whose height and excellence words mostly fail. But as long as you bark, it must not be done; for it was not said in vain, ‘Do not give what is holy to dogs.’ Do not be angry. I too barked and was a dog, when I was dealt with, rightly, not with the food of teaching but with the clubs of refutation. But if you had that charity we are speaking of, or should one day have as much as the greatness of knowing the truth requires, God will be at hand to show you that the Christian faith, which leads to the summit of wisdom and truth and whose enjoyment is simply to live happily, is neither among the Manichæans nor anywhere except in the catholic teaching.” And again: “Hence arises what we have striven for from the beginning: that nothing is more beneficial in the Catholic Church than that authority should precede reason.”

7. From these passages one might easily infer that Augustine would appeal only to such low hopes as these low minds could reach, would give nothing but fleshless bones to these barking dogs, and would insist on their submitting to the Church’s authority before treating them as capable of hearing any reason. How stands the case? At the outset of his treatise (chapter 2) he declares that he will appeal only to the scriptures they themselves recognise, and will try the Church by the moral signs they hold to be sound. In chapter 3 he says that although, in the true order of nature, authority ought to precede reason, he will nevertheless ask them to accept nothing for which he does not give a reason. He adds: “It pleases me to imitate, as far as I can, the gentleness of my Lord Jesus Christ, who put on even the evil of death itself, which he wished to strip from us.” And what kind of reasons does he appeal to? He asks what the highest good is, shows what an appetite man has for it, and proves that God must be the good, and that the knowledge and love of God must be the eternal life men crave. These are his starting points, and everything he says about the Scriptures and the Church is referred back to them. He does not say this in a cold, dry spirit, as if afraid to show deeper emotion before his hearers. His words often rise almost into rapture, yet he never loses for a moment his intellectual clearness or his human affection. See especially chapters eighteen to twenty-six of the first book, a noble piece of Christian ethical philosophy.

8. I think, then, that in abandoning the argument from safety and appealing to principles still acknowledged by those who have wandered farthest from what I believe to be the order of the Catholic Church, I am acting in the spirit of these passages. The other method seems to me an incongruous combination of the hardest maxims and precedents of early times with the most vulgar of our own. It is an attempt to graft Paley upon Chrysostom, and to establish ancient Christianity by the help of the Stock Exchange.

Note (B), p. 37

The following passage from Tittmann’s Meletemata Sacra (pp. 27–29) will explain the allusion in the text. “For a long time it has seemed to me, and still seems, that the view I mentioned in the place cited has the greatest marks of truth. I have since seen that Laurentius Valla put it forward long ago in his Notes on the New Testament, whose words I give below (he says that ‘the Word’ means that blessed seed of which the Lord spoke to Adam, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, David and all the elect, as if you were to call it God’s Speech, or Promise). Beza held it too, in his Notes on this passage. In our own times it has been set out at length above all by J. A. Cramer (Beyträge zur Beförderung theol. Gelehrsamkeit, Part 1, p. 232) and by Gabr. Chr. Benj. Mosche (Erklärung der Evangelien, Part 1, p. 289), and approved also by Jo. Aug. Ernesti (Bibl. Theol. Nov., the New Theological Library, vol. 3, p. 129 ff.). Briefly, and without repeating what I argued from the Old Testament, I judge that the word Logos means the promise. It is used in place of ‘the one spoken of,’ which is exactly equivalent to the more usual ‘the one who comes.’ Four things explain its use: the custom of those times in thinking of and naming the Saviour, the usage of the language, John’s purpose in writing, and the Lord’s own habit in describing himself. First, the custom of the time, arising from the nature of the case (the Saviour was still to come) and received from the Old Testament, was to call the Messiah ‘the one who comes.’ That is his pre-eminent name in the Old Testament, as Cyril of Jerusalem hints (Catech. xii. p. 261, ed. Prenot.) from Genesis xlix. 10. It is found very often in the New Testament too: in Matthew xi. 3, where John the Baptist bids his disciples ask, ‘Art thou he that cometh, or do we look for another?’, and in John vi. 16 and xi. 27. So the name could not have been unknown to Jews, and above all to Christians, nor unusual in common speech. Second, no one even slightly versed in the language of Scripture will be offended that the Evangelist says Logos for ‘the one spoken of.’ Abstract words are used for concrete ones a hundred times, and often in naming our Lord. He is called ‘salvation’ for ‘saviour’ (John iv. 22), and ‘life’ and ‘light’; Luke ii. 25 and 30 has ‘salvation’ and ‘the consolation of Israel.’ Nor can there be doubt that Logos can carry the force I describe. The Hebrew word it answers to, and the Greek word itself in Romans ix. 6, is said of a promise in countless places, and the Greek translators render it ‘message’ and ‘promise’ (for example Proverbs xii. 25). So ‘the one spoken of’ is, and can be, the same as ‘the one promised.’ Hence in Wisdom xviii. 15 the angel divinely promised and sent is called the Word of God (see Schleusner’s Spicilegium, Spec. i. p. 75). The word has plainly the same force in John’s other books: in 1 John i. 1, where the Lord is called the Word of life, that is, the promised author of happiness; and in Revelation xix. 13, where he is called the Word of God, that is, the one promised by God. Third, John’s aim in writing the Gospel was, as we saw, to show that Jesus is the promised and expected Messiah, and anyone can see how well the word suits that aim. Finally, and most notably, in all the discourses this book records the Lord habitually called himself the one sent by the Father, and the Father the one who sends. He described himself as sent by the Father, come into these lands, and about to return to him (John vi. 38, xvi. 28, xiii. 3 and so on). By this he plainly meant that he was ‘the one who comes,’ the promised and expected Messiah. This habit of the Lord seems the chief reason for the use of a word otherwise rather unusual here. So the sense of the opening words is: the promised Saviour existed before the beginning of all things, as if it had been written, ‘In the beginning was the Saviour spoken of.’”

These remarks would matter little if they were only the opinion of one critic, even one as respectable as Tittmann. But he can list a fair number of authorities, some going back to the Reformation, some among the scholars of the last age. All of them, with more or less reservation, accepted an interpretation that each would have rejected with indignation and disdain if it were applied to any passage in an uninspired author. There must be a reason. Men who had studied the laws of language could not talk this way about putting the abstract for the concrete. They could not offer such inapplicable parallels from other parts of Scripture, and then fall back on the insolent dogma that no one even slightly acquainted with the Bible’s language can be offended by such a departure from ordinary principles. Something deeply rooted in thought and feeling must be at odds with the simple rendering of the words. What it is may puzzle us for a moment, since the feelings of the Reformation writers were in many respects directly opposed to those of their successors in the next two centuries. But the earlier writers were eager to assert the Bible’s distinction from all other books, and above all to cut it off from philosophy. So we need not wonder that they wished to separate wholly the word on which John’s Gospel turns from the same word as Plato and Philo use it.

The early Socinian writers saw the fact that the orthodox Protestants had resolved to overlook. They used it to argue that if the expression meant something abstract and impersonal in either uninspired writer, it must mean the same in the sacred ones. Those who answered them naturally took fresh pains to assert its special meaning, and called any effort to connect it with another profane and heretical. Then came a time when the classical writers began to be spoken of with respect. The ordinary Protestant commentator was rather glad to see Scripture as a new and enlarged form of heathen morality, and the descendants of the Polish Socinians began to influence theological opinion. One might have expected then a reversal of the old decrees, and an excessive willingness to find analogies between St. John and the heathen or Jewish teacher. But by now Platonism had been drained of its meaning just as Christianity had. A heathen philosopher who laid down fine maxims about virtue was respected as a Christian minus the belief in Christ. When he uttered anything that savoured of a sense of mystery, the reverence vanished; there his paganism or false philosophy was thought to have intruded. The Gospel’s new merit was that it ended such dreams and brought everything within common sense. Since believers and deniers of the old Creeds shared this feeling, believers were glad of any expedient to prove that John, though he asserted a strange dogma, was free from all taint of mysticism. Deniers tried to evade his authority by alleging that he did show a tendency which the enlightened of all parties had agreed to denounce. In our own day the evangelical protest against mere heathen morality, and the alarm at new attempts to present Christianity as one among many forms of religious faith, have produced the same desire for an exclusive interpretation, which earlier periods wanted for different reasons. So Tittmann’s interpretation is likely to be popular among religious people both here and abroad.

But can we be honouring Scripture while we consciously look for strange and tortuous explanations of its most awful and characteristic passages? Should not every theological student suspect himself if he feels inclined to take refuge in this or any similar device for evading the ordinary force of words? I press this thought earnestly on those it most concerns. The recovery of a sound spiritual theology among us depends mainly on the temper in which we approach St. John’s Gospel. It depends on how we connect it with Old Testament history and prophecy, and on our willingness to acknowledge that it interprets to us God’s dealings with men in all time. The more I look at the directions men’s thoughts are taking today, the more this seems to me the subject we should give ourselves to. Right meditation on it may save us from a thousand confusions and help reconcile the most apparently opposite feelings. The three notes that follow may help explain my meaning, and help the reader understand the purpose of my other hints.

Note (C), p. 39

It has always been the impression, I believe, among ordinary readers and scholars alike, that Aristophanes meant to portray Socrates as a Sophist who was corrupting the old spirit of Greece with modern philosophical refinements. On this view Aristophanes may have entirely mistaken the man he was ridiculing, yet he still confirms what other sources tell us. Socrates was above all a moral teacher. He dealt with physical speculation only as far as it illustrated his principles about human life, and his great effort was to awaken men to self-knowledge. A teacher who said that a constant conflict goes on in man between one principle drawing him upward and another dragging him to earth could easily be shown as keeping a right and a wrong reason and making the wrong one win whenever it suited. One who urged pupils to study the meaning of words could be accused of inventing airy, over-subtle distinctions. One who wished to lead them through the crowding thoughts and fancies the sophists had stirred up, to a solid foundation, could be suspected of feeding self-consciousness and interfering with simple, practical life. Anyone must meet such charges who enters a corrupt society to reform it, not by preaching about the past but by entering the present and looking for a way out of its confusions. It is the best proof of Aristophanes’ talent that he saw so clearly the kind of work Socrates was doing. It is the best excuse for his character that he confused Socrates with people who deserved every condemnation, and whom Socrates was refuting and exposing by a very different course from the Comedian’s.

But a modern editor of Aristophanes, Mr. Mitchell, whose English notes, criticisms and earlier translations are likely to make his work very popular, has put forward an entirely new theory. He maintains that the idea of Socrates’ aims that we get from his two disciples, or from tradition, is utterly false. He says Socrates was primarily, almost exclusively, a physical philosopher, and that this is how he is ridiculed in the Clouds. The hero of the part of the play that seems to describe him otherwise is, he says, really not Socrates but Euripides!

This last hypothesis will, I think, refute the ingenious scheme better than any argument I could bring. To make his case against the philosopher, Mr. Mitchell has to destroy our respect for the genius of his own favourite poet. He must present the poet’s most elaborate play as wholly lacking unity of purpose or principle, a mere collection of incoherent lampoons on two men who apparently shared no common object or pursuit. I can hardly imagine a better confirmation of the common opinion, that Socrates was what Xenophon and Plato both describe, much as they differ in mind and in their ways of regarding their master, than this desperate effort to set it aside.

So far as the assertion in my text is concerned, these remarks might suffice. But I cannot help seeing that Mr. Mitchell’s remarks here accord with what he is trying to spread everywhere about Greek philosophy, and that he is, mischievously as I think, using Christian feelings and prejudices to support them. In his Preface to the Clouds he gives it as his opinion that the highest honour Pythagoras ever received was to be made “the hero and object of ridicule in one of Lucian’s unmatched dialogues.” He calls Pythagoras, Epimenides and Empedocles “impostors” and “charlatans.” Having assumed that Socrates belonged to the same class of thinkers and took much of his teaching from the Italian school, he leaves his readers to infer that Socrates was an impostor and charlatan too.

Our notices of Pythagoras are rather scattered, though they show that the greatest of the Greeks regarded him with respect and admiration. So my readers may want to know how Mr. Mitchell reaches his conclusions about him. Perhaps they suppose he gathers what Plato and Aristotle say, turns to Diogenes Laertius, and finally uses whatever hints Cicero can supply from Greek or Italian sources. No, our author disdains such vulgar methods. His authorities come from after the Christian era. He takes the life of Apollonius of Tyana by the sophist Philostratus. He finds that this mythical hero dressed himself in the cast-off clothes of the Samian, inventing some stories (as Mr. Mitchell fairly admits) but probably “finding others ready made to his hand.” The chief ground for that last opinion is that “the immortal Lucian” believed them, or pretended to. Out of these materials Mr. Mitchell constructs a Pythagoras, and from him, by the easiest process imaginable (granting his earlier hypothesis), a Socrates.

I urge my reader to check these assertions in the preface I mention, for I do not ask him to take it on my authority that any scholar acquainted with the laws of historical evidence has adopted such a method of judging an ancient teacher. It is needless to point out how the precedent might be applied elsewhere. If an Italian monk of the last or present century wrote a biography of some Father or Apostle, and had found his relics and worked miracles with them, that biography must now be accepted as genuine. On its strength the Father or Apostle must be pronounced an impostor or charlatan, provided only that Voltaire or some other immortal Lucian of the West has taken it up as a warrant for his jests. Nevertheless Mr. Mitchell’s statements will be eagerly received in many quarters. Some people seem to think that the proof of Christianity’s divine origin and worth depends on a low estimate of all the wise men Greece produced in its best days. If we can only wipe out respect for them, no matter how, we are left with a tabula rasa on which to write the feelings and principles of the New Testament. Alas, what a tabula rasa! One on which scorn, scepticism, indifference to every earnest hope and unbelief in any principle have already been written in the deepest characters. These are the preparations for the Gospel! These are the habits of mind we are to foster so that we may feel our own nothingness and our need of a Divine Teacher, and believe we have one! The facts are against this notion, and that is certain. In the ages after Christianity was proclaimed, the sceptics and scoffers were not the readiest to receive it. Those who most welcomed the messenger who brought good tidings and said, Behold your God, were those who held most earnestly and truly to what they had, and sought God by every light that Mythology or Philosophy gave them. Yet in the teeth of such clear evidence we suppose we prove Christianity to be what all men want, by proving that all who showed they wanted it were deceiving others or themselves.

The point on which I am most anxious to speak, however, is the ground of this suspicion. It is that whenever these heathen philosophers perceived a great moral principle, whose knowledge they believed might benefit ages unborn, they said they were inspired. I do not doubt that they were ignorant of the conditions under which spiritual life and wisdom are given. I do not doubt that the belief of being taught from above produced an intoxication that sometimes carried them into extravagance, and may in the end have bred a vanity that was the parent of real impostures. But I ask: is it safe, in spite of all this, to say that honesty of purpose and real, positive wisdom were the fruits of delusion and falsehood? All that is sincere in me revolts against it. Good produces good and lies produce lies. Whatever deceit there was in these men led to evil results and kept up a superstition that was eventually exposed and carried much sound faith away with it. But they also did some good, great good. Did that come from hollowness and trickery? May it never be! It must have come from deep and wise counsels, earnest meditation and loving feelings. And where did these come from? Are we, just because we are Christians, to say it was pretence and cheating to claim that they came from the source of all truth and good? We know that not only rains and fruitful seasons but all holy thoughts, good counsels and just works have a Divine Author. Are we then to say that the dim recognition of this truth by older men was actually a crime, and that they would have been better and wiser if they had taken the honour to themselves? Yet we must charge this humility upon them as a sin if we are determined to adopt the Pelagian doctrine: that the good in these men had its root in human nature, existing apart from God, and not in the teachings and impulses of the Divine Word.

Note (D), p. 42

The reader will find in Mr. Coleridge’s “Aids to Reflection” a valuable passage on Mysticism. It distinguishes accurately the two senses of the word I mentioned in the text. A deep grasp of the law and mystery of our own being may come from simply studying the word of God, or from that study improved by reading and cultivation. It may then attach itself to notions and allegories suggested by the fancies of particular minds. Coleridge’s examples of this are Böhme and Fenelon, well chosen as the most perfect types of Protestant and Romish mysticism. But Philo is perhaps a more striking and important instance than either. No one who looks into his writings can think he was not a laborious student of the Bible. The charge often made against spiritualists, that they undervalue the Scriptures and put in their place wisdom drawn from elsewhere, applies to him least of all. He plainly valued his sacred writings above all other books together, and traced all his thoughts and discoveries to the light he had received from them. Yet his readings of Scripture would utterly confound anyone used to reading the text simply. Such a person would not say, as nine out of ten would who bring the artificial notions of their own time, “All this is mere stuff and nonsense.” He would see that those words do not fit. He would see a continuous stream of thought through every part of the exposition, which convinces us that the records have an inner unity. But I am sure the best and truest reader would also feel great restlessness and impatience on seeing the records of a simple, practical life turned into high-flown conceits and allegories about the moral virtues and the sensible and rational parts of man.

How, then, are we to tell the solid and real from the fantastic in these books? I think Philo’s position supplies the answer. Nowhere could a man better learn than in Alexandria what the thoughtful men of the heathen world felt and needed, what the philosophers were seeking, and what it would take to satisfy them. Nowhere could he better find something to interpret the plain domestic life and national feeling of the Jewish people. Bringing that knowledge to his Scriptures, Philo found everywhere hints of a Divine Teacher guiding men out of the ways of sense and out of slavery to visible appearances, into the acknowledgment of an unseen Being. He knew how man’s reason had struggled against the impressions of sense and tried to realize an object answering to itself. Here he found the other side of the picture: the Reason of God leading the higher faculty He had given His creature to seek Him and find Him. But when he brought his ignorance to the same books, the real conflicts, human deeds and outward crosses of the people they describe became to him mere pictures of mental feelings and operations. He could see the world of human relations only as an image world, and he was wholly unprepared for the mystery of the Word made Flesh. His first class of thoughts connects him with the most thoughtful men of all times, in positions very unlike his own. The second connects him with them only in this: that they too have been prone to read their own circumstances and states of mind into the universal book. But because those circumstances differ from his, their speculations offer little support to his, and his little excuse for theirs.

My object is not to give an account of Philo’s scriptural allegories, or to show how the Divine Word is woven through all his thought. I wish only to illustrate how he represents the Word as a Teacher, connects that teaching with man’s reason, and describes it as universal. His hints about the relation between the Word and the Absolute Being are more important in the strictly theological sense. I will allude to them only so far as they bear on the point belonging to the history of Quakerism. So I pass over his assertion that the Divine Word was both the framer of things and the image after which they were framed (see the book on the Mosaic history of Creation, from the passage beginning “For God, having foreseen” to the one ending “good in accordance with truth”). I pass over all passages on the physical universe, even when they speak of an intellectual universe as its type. I will not dwell on his comment on “the Lord God took the man whom he had made, and set him in the Garden, to till it and to watch over it.” There he distinguishes the mind that was merely formed from the one that was created: “a pure reason, unparticipant of corrupt matter, enjoying a purer and more untainted constitution, which pure reason God apprehends, not suffering it to go from Him, but placing it as the watchman and governor of the different virtues which He has planted and made to bud around it.” (Allegories, Book i. § 28.) I will hardly more than mention his remark, which is an important limit on those words, that Cain and Abel stand for two opposing opinions. One refers everything to the reason as the guide of thought, feeling, motion and power. The other refers it to God as His workmanship. The same soul travails with both, but once they are born they must be divided, since such enemies cannot dwell together. (On the sacrifices of Cain and Abel, § 1.)

More directly to our purpose, and a key to the others, is the following passage from a treatise on the “Husbandry of Noah,” allegorized in the author’s usual way.

“Our Reason, like a goatherd, cowherd, shepherd or keeper of cattle, needs to rule, choosing what is convenient over what is pleasant, whether to itself or to the lower creatures. Yet that the parts of the soul are not left ungoverned, but have the blessing of a faultless and altogether good Shepherd, under whom the council of thoughts cannot be disturbed and scattered, we must ascribe finally, and we might say exclusively, to the oversight of God Himself. The soul is shown to be under one order because it looks up to one Overseer, since having to serve many different rulers is an intolerable calamity. So good is the Shepherd’s office that it is rightly ascribed not only to kings, wise men and souls purified for the highest mysteries, but to God, the universal Ruler. Our authority is no insignificant person but a prophet and psalmist, whom we may trust: ‘The Lord is my Shepherd, and I shall not want.’ Let each man say the same in his own part and measure, for this is a song fitting for every godly man to sing over to himself, and it also belongs to the whole universe. Water, air, fire, and all plants and animals in them, things mortal and divine, the heavens, the courses of Sun and Moon, the movements and harmonious dances of the other Stars: God guides all of these as Shepherd and King, as if they were one sheepfold, according to order and law. Over them He sets His true Word, His first begotten Son, ready like the viceroy of a great king to receive and give back the care of this holy flock. Let the whole world, that greatest and most perfect sheepfold of the true God, say, ‘The Lord is my Shepherd, I shall not want.’ Let each say it individually, not with the voice of tongue and mouth, which spreads through only a little air, but with the far-reaching voice of the mind, which touches the boundaries of the universe.”

I do not quote this for its style, which is perhaps inflated, but as a fair statement of the idea running through the whole treatise. That idea is that the living Word is the shepherd and teacher of the inner man, ordaining the whole constitution of the mind and holding direct, continual converse with it. The following passage from another work is in precisely the same spirit.

“It is fearful for the soul to ascend into the contemplation of The Being by itself, not knowing the way and lifted up by ignorance and boldness. Great are the falls that come from such ignorance and foolish daring. So Moses prays to have God Himself as his guide on the way to Him: ‘If thou wilt not go with me, lead me not up from hence.’ Every movement without Divine direction leads to evil. It would be better to stay here below, leading this confused, beggarly mortal life like most men, than to lift ourselves up to heaven and, through boasting, be turned back and confounded. That has happened to multitudes of sophists, who fancied wisdom lay in inventing persuasive words rather than in the most true faith of things. Perhaps this is also signified: ‘Do not lift me up on high by giving me wealth, fame, honour, power or any of the things called good, if Thou wilt not Thyself accompany them.’ These things often bring the greatest curses and blessings: blessings when God is the guide of the mind, curses when He is not. To multitudes the so-called goods have caused intolerable evils. But he who follows God uses as fellows and comrades on his journey those Words (Λόγοι) whom it is customary to call angels, for it is written that Abraham went with them, leading them on the way. O beautiful equality, when the guide is himself guided, giving what he receives, not one thing in place of another but the very same thing reciprocated! While he is not yet perfected, he uses the Divine Word as the leader of the way. For the oracle says, ‘Behold I send My angel with thee before thy face, that he may keep thee in the way, and lead thee to the land which I have prepared for thee; give heed to him and hearken to him, disobey him not, for My word is in him.’ But when he reaches the highest point of knowledge, running on vigorously, he will match his pace with the one who led him before. Both will become followers of God, the universal Guide.” (On the Migration of Abraham, § 31.)

Whatever one thinks of the startling words that end this passage, reading them makes clearer the purpose of the Epistle to the Hebrews, especially its first chapter. They also throw light on the form of Gnosticism that attached itself to Jewish ideas. The Jew could recognise these Λόγοι, or angels, ministers of God and to man, in his Scriptures. Might not Jesus be one of them: neither absolutely Divine nor absolutely human, but belonging to a middle race between Godhead and Humanity? The writer of the epistle meets this opinion. He proves that the Scriptures speak of one who is not one of the Λόγοι but the Λόγος, not a Son of God but the Son of God. He then shows how this Son took hold not of angels but of the seed of Abraham. Students of theology know this remark well, but it may help younger men to see it as one proof that all the Scriptures become unintelligible and incoherent once the idea I am dwelling on is lost sight of.

The following extract from the treatise “On the confusion of tongues” has the same kind of value. Philo is discussing the words “The tower which the sons of men have builded.” He imagines a jester asking why this phrase is used, since who but sons of men would build cities and towers? He answers that the words are not careless. They indicate that the builders were pursuing a multitude of objects and had lost sight of the one Creator and Father of all. He then adds: “The truly wise are fittingly called sons of the one God, as Moses confesses: ‘Ye are the sons of the Lord your God,’ and ‘God that begat thee.’ This belongs to those who have fashioned their souls to think that what is in itself pure and beautiful is the only good. That belief is set up as the opposite fortress to that of pleasure, to overthrow it. Even if a man is not yet fit to be called a son of God, let him strive to fashion himself after God’s first begotten Word, the eldest of the angels, the archangel of many names. He is called ‘Beginning,’ ‘Name of God,’ ‘Word,’ ‘The Man according to the divine Image,’ and ‘He that looketh upon Israel.’ Though we are not yet reckoned sons of God, we may be called sons of His eternal Image, the most holy Word, for this most venerable Word is the image of God.”

In a very cabalistic commentary on the fifteenth chapter of Genesis, on the words “he divided not the birds,” he says: “The word of God, solitary and single amid the multitude of things that have come to be and will perish, remains uncompounded, ever ascending upward and seeking to be the companion of the One. So there are two undivided natures: the Reason in us, and the Divine Word over us. Being undivided, they divide innumerable other things. The Divine Word has divided the things in nature and distributed them all. Our reason distinguishes, in innumerable ways and into innumerable parts, whatever things or bodies it has taken in, and never ceases its dissections. This comes about through its likeness to the Creator and Father of the whole. The divinity, unmixed, uncompounded and without parts, has been to all the world the cause of mixture, combination and apportionment. So it is fitting that what is like it, the reason in us and that above us, being without parts and undivided, should be able with mighty force to divide and discriminate each thing that is.” (Who is the Inheritor of Divine things? Sect. 48.)

There is a striking passage early in the first book on Dreams, which I recommend to the reader. It shows how Philo connected Science, or Knowledge, with submission to Divine guidance. Far from setting Science directly against Faith, he treats it as the direct opposite of Sense. This is a remarkable thought, which he might have taken from Plato, but which seemed to him in strict harmony with the teaching of his own lawgiver and prophets.

The evidence for Philo’s belief in the universality of the Divine teaching comes less from particular passages than from the whole spirit of his books. In all the language I have quoted, the Word’s teaching is constantly referred to man, and there is no attempt to confine the blessing to his own nation. That would have been natural in an indifferent Jew who set little store by his Scriptures and thought the heathen writers more profound than they were. But Philo admired and studied the Jewish writings deeply, and that admiration implied the greatest thankfulness for Israel’s original separation and for the ordinances that distinguished it. So one might have expected here and there some of the feeling characteristic of his countrymen. Yet it is evident that he held wisdom and knowledge of God to be the great object and reward of human search and the highest gift the Creator can bestow. This led him to see the Jew, who had every facility for the pursuit, as better off than if he had had the right to treat other men as outcasts and aliens. And we need not rely on inference. There is a special treatise on the thesis that “every virtuous man is a freeman.” In it he lays down as a maxim, supported by a line of Sophocles, that “he only is free who hath God as his guide.” (§ 3.)

Having set out the conditions and marks of freedom, he meets the question: where have such men ever been, or where are they now? He answers that men who excelled in virtue, however rare, are to be found. They followed God as their only guide and lived by the right law of nature, and they were not only free themselves but inspired their neighbours with free thoughts. He finds such men in Greece among the “seven wise men” and some of the Eleatic philosophers, in India among the gymnosophists, and in Persia among the magians. His express words and the habitual tone of his thought show that he did not think them wise or good because of any intrinsic quality, or because each man might be saved by his own religion. He thought so because, amid whatever perplexities and contradictions, they followed the one Guide and Shepherd who had revealed himself to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob: that unseen and everlasting Word who had entered into covenant with the Jews and brought them out of sensible idolatry into the light and liberty of His children.

In some recent English attacks on the Fathers (I do not mean Mr. Taylor’s, which took a somewhat different line), the charge of holding the Quaker doctrine of the Logos was pushed to the front. But these writers did not know how strong their case was. They imagined the Greek Fathers were advancing this notion as one among many. They should have said boldly: “This heresy was not taken up by accident. It is woven into the very tissue of their thoughts, and their minds were infected by it to the core. You could as easily take Justification by Faith out of Luther as take this doctrine out of them.” That would have been perfectly true, and it would have brought the matter to a plainer issue. They gave a number of extracts, all to the point, but still leaving room to hope that the general tone of thought in the first three centuries might be sound. Suppose they had instead given an analysis of the Pedagogus of Clemens, or of any of his other writings, or had used those which the present Bishop of Lincoln introduces into his lectures. Their readers would then have had no doubt that these Fathers’ very conception of Christianity involved acknowledging the Divine Word as man’s invisible teacher in every period. And if that acknowledgment is heretical, then Clemens was heretical, and so was the Church generally, which did not condemn him but sympathised with him and counted him among its greatest lights. They would have been habitually, wilfully, radically heretical. Let the opinion be stated in plain terms, together with the usual defence of it, that the doctrine sets at nought the authority and inspiration of the Scriptures. We believe this principle is involved in every part of Scripture. We can see no grounds for Scripture’s authority and inspiration if it is denied. We attribute much of the Church’s lifelessness in our day, much of the feebleness of our theology, and much of our strife and division to the habitual disregard of it. So we will be able to state the grounds of our belief frankly and, by God’s grace, to meet any consequences that follow from professing it.

In speaking of Philo, I explained why I think one consequence is not the necessary adoption of all the theories about interpreting Scripture and the symbols of nature which he, and others who acknowledged an invisible teacher, put forward. I cannot inconsistently deny that the same principle applies to the study of the Fathers. I have said elsewhere in these volumes why I suppose they, like Philo, lacked the means of entering into the practical life of the Hebrews. I do find in them a very different tone from his. They show more tenderness, a consciousness of evil, a readiness to call themselves sinners rather than saints, and more sympathy with men than with philosophers. That is what we might expect from those who acknowledged a teacher who took human flesh and who, as Clemens puts it, “assists us in all things, both as man and as God; putting away our sins as God; training us not to sin as man.” Above all, I find in them a sense of unity and fellowship, as members of one body, inhabited by one Spirit, worshipping the one Name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. That contrasts with the isolated, esoteric temper of the theosophist. But in the outward relations of life and society their knowledge seems to have been much on a level with his, so it is no surprise to find the same habits of thought in both. A late publication suggests that the cabalistic interpretations of Scripture among the early Fathers may have been communicated by special revelation. This is surely one of the most gratuitous hypotheses a theological writer ever put forward. It seems plausible to students used only to “modern Protestant divinity” because they feel: “This is so unlike anything others say that it must have come directly from Divine authority.” But look at a sage of an earlier time who apparently never received the Gospel at all and has never been counted among the Church’s teachers. We find in him precisely the same tone of thought, and probably many of the very thoughts that are supposed to have been specially imparted to the Christian sages, or to be sacred traditions of our Lord’s own words. We also find a tendency of the same kind, and often curious resemblances to the actual cabbala of the first ages, in the writings of those whom our patristic schools most despise, Protestants and Quakers. We may surely ask, then, whether such thoughts belong to human nature under certain circumstances and conditions, though of course they presume that nature not to be untaught from above.

The Fathers, I think, would have disdained the kind of honour their admirers are trying to give them. They believed their hearts and reasons were under the teaching of the Divine Word, and that they were created and constituted in him. So they would have been much more inclined to regard their thoughts about nature and Scripture as spiritual intuitions than as authoritative traditions. They may well have been tempted to treat these intuitions as sacred and to speak of them as certain. That is very true. Everyone who has such intuitions feels them, for the time, to be certain; he cannot feel otherwise. Hence the value of the corrective set against this dangerous but inevitable conviction: only what belonged to the whole Church, not what was uttered by individual minds, was stable and everlastingly true for man. These two principles, balanced and harmonized, seem to give the true witness and protection against ancient and modern fanaticism, and against ancient and modern formality. The Divine and Everlasting Word is each man’s teacher, his guide through the sensible into the spiritual, and through the individual into the universal. In each man, according to the temper and habits of his age, there is a continual tendency to mix the sensual with the spiritual, and what belongs to the peculiarities of his own mind with what is permanent and for all. These utterances are not meant to be stifled. They remain as witnesses of what each age is, and the truth which that age was especially to defend and develop could not be seen if they were withdrawn. Then certain men of each age say that what belongs to them is true and perfect, and others rise up to show how much deeper the wisdom of a former age was. Each would stamp with sacredness what belongs to the passing accidents of a particular period. But He remains who is the Father of the everlasting age, the perpetual Guide of the spirits of all who will obey Him. He remains in the fixed records of His own revelation, the fixed ordinances of His own Church, and the order and succession of His own natural universe, to teach His servants to tell apart what belongs to the constitution in which He has placed them and what belongs to their own or other men’s apprehensions of it. Take away belief in His presence, and the teachings of antiquity, even the written Scriptures themselves, become oppressive restraints on the spirit. They cannot raise it above its own modes of thought, and only give it a miserable sense that it ought to rise and cannot. Having lost the conviction that the whole Church is under His guidance, we at one moment declare we will believe nothing but what antiquity tells us, or nothing but what the Bible tells us. The next moment we are the sport of every dreamer who claims to have the Spirit the Bible and the Fathers spoke of. For he speaks to something within us which tells us we are meant to follow a living voice and not a dead one. Because we refuse the truth implied in that witness, it becomes to us a mischievous falsehood. We do not acknowledge the Word, the great distinguisher between light and darkness, truth and falsehood, “the discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart,” who unrolls the volume of experience and binds times and seasons together. So the Spirit, who knits hearts together, who would bind us into one family and lead us into the knowledge and enjoyment of the Divine and ineffable Unity, seems to us a Spirit of division. He seems to enter our minds only to exalt us in our own conceits, to lift us up against one another, and to make us heady, high-minded, “lovers of ourselves more than lovers of God.”

Note on Chap. II. Sect. iv.

When I speak of the final result of the experiment of pure Protestantism, I do not mean that the Protestants of Germany, Holland and Switzerland have lost the blessings they had before the Reformation, or those claimed for them at the Reformation. I mean only that the systems called Lutheranism, Calvinism and Zuinglianism have had their day, and that their extinction is near. Germans are the most aware of this. That is why they are eager to unite the followers of these systems in an “Evangelical Church”; why they want to reconcile the Lutheran and “Reformed” ideas of the Eucharist by mutual concessions; and why they are willing for a time to submit to the State, if it will only deliver them from a sectarian position. Anyone who is not spellbound by some system must see in these feelings a proof that the Reformers’ descendants have not been deserted by the Head of the Church. He may be preparing for them blessings that the great men of the sixteenth century longed for and could not reach. I will not anticipate the later part of my book by saying what these blessings are, or how they may cure the evils Germany was groaning under long before the Reformers arose to help and purify her. Still less will I take up the practical question of how they may be recovered. One thing is clear: those who feel the unhappy ecclesiastical position of Protestant Germany are also the most determined not to give up the principles of the Reformation. Some, perhaps touched by Austrian or Bavarian influences, may dream of returning to the position before Luther; but all who are in earnest and know their own minds will reject the thought as both folly and sin. The German mind is full of the idea that there must be progress, not retreat, and I would be very sorry to drive it out. The only question is what kind of progress. The Reformation began in the spirit and has, in the most grievous sense, been made perfect in the flesh. Its principles have found no clothing except a system, which has stifled them, or a State organization, which stifles the minds and energies of those who profess them. The progress Protestants should want is surely toward an organization that is a vital expression of the nation’s faith, not an artificial one. Suppose the ecclesiastical organization Germany once had, though corrupted and deadened by the denial of Christ’s direct superintendence over it, proves to be of this vital kind. Then recovering it will be no less a growth than acquiring a newer one, and far more so: the old one belongs to the land’s own history, while a newer one would be a fantastic dress, made like Napoleon’s institutions to the maxims of an age, and so an intolerable burden to all who look beyond that age and feel they have a share in their fathers and their posterity. Their Protestantism will make the old Catholicism new and living; the Catholicism that has this quickening element will gradually extinguish the Romanist counterfeit of it; and the States, which no civil arrangements have been able to unite, will become one through unity of faith. Then the poet’s words will be fulfilled: wherever his tongue is spoken and God in Heaven is praised in it, there the German will find his fatherland.
