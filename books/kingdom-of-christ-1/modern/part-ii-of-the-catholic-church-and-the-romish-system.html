Part II. Of the Catholic Church and the Romish System

Though every historical church system decays, the principles behind Quakerism, Lutheranism, and Unitarianism endure, pointing toward a real spiritual and universal kingdom, founded on a divine Person, that Scripture can show still exists on earth.

Chapter I. Recapitulation

Our conclusion seems to be this. The principles asserted by the religious societies formed in Europe since the Reformation are solid and imperishable. The systems that embodied them were faulty from the start, have fulfilled their purpose less and less as they aged, and now show clear signs of approaching dissolution. When I spoke of modern philosophical movements, I mentioned popular statements that at first look much like this conclusion. One is the doctrine that systems, religions and churches are dying, but that they were the clothing of important ideas which will outlive them, and which wise men should note, preserve and perhaps re-clothe. I cannot hold that doctrine; if this book means anything, I must oppose it. Still, a conscientious reader may find it hard to see where it and mine part ways. To make the link between what I have said and what I am about to say intelligible, I must relieve him of that difficulty. The best way is to remind him of the evidence we have met along the way.

I. On the authority of the old Quaker books, and against one of their modern teachers, I held the fundamental doctrine of Quakerism to be this. Man is a twofold creature. He has inclinations toward sensible things, but by trusting in the divine Word, to whom he is united, he can rise above them to spiritual life and communion. The people most unlike the early Quakers are modern philosophers, artists and politicians. Yet we found people of these classes, by various routes, adopting the very maxim that was the great obstacle to believing Fox’s principle. Religious people said it cannot be true, because man as such, apart from a special religious calling and impulse, is not spiritual. The philosophers of the last generation said it cannot be true, because man is not capable of such a calling and impulse at all, being simply flesh and blood. Those who speak for the enlightenment of our time would disown both opinions. They say man as man has spiritual powers and is a spiritual creature.

Many who use that language would present it as a doctrine stripped of its old form and reduced to its pure essence. “In acknowledging a spiritual life in man,” they would say, “we keep Fox’s meaning and only remove the phraseology that earlier religious tradition put on it.” I am not arguing whether that is so. I am showing that our observations led to the opposite conclusion. A man who believes he has spiritual capacities is just the man who must ask under what conditions they exist. That was Fox’s problem. The sense of upward tendencies in himself, constantly restrained and resisted, gave him not peace but torment. We may talk in general about our spiritual power. But the moment we realize it practically, amid the contradictions it lives under in this world, it gets tangled with awful responsibility, with the vision of an unknown world, with the certainty of moral evil, and we are glad to escape into materialism. Since that escape is now closed, we must ask where these desires and impulses come from, what upholds them, and where they tend. Those questions lead straight to the principle Fox asserted. A system may omit it or substitute another, but every man really engaged in the world’s conflict must face it. Modern thinkers constantly show they lack it, especially when they speak of self-consciousness and its necessity, misery and falsehood. The words “Not I but Christ in me” answer these perplexities, and we may search heaven and earth before finding another answer. If I am asked why, then, I say the Quaker system has proved inadequate, my answer is this. It has failed to give Fox’s idea a clear, definite expression. It has reduced it nearly to a vague synonym for the notion that we have spiritual capacities or feelings, and it has not shown men the object on whom Fox said those capacities and feelings were to be exercised.

The ordinary philosopher knows little of Fox. He is more anxious to show that he has kept all that is precious in the great Reformer’s teaching. Our mere men of letters revere Leo, think Erasmus protested as much as the abominations of his time required, and regard Luther, who knew nothing of statues and wrote poor Latin, with positive aversion. The more earnest men, especially those who believe European society has been progressing from darkness to light, speak of Luther as one who worked mightily to overthrow opinions resting on mere authority and tradition, and who prepared the way for thoughts he himself would have rejected with horror. I am not speaking of the ignorant declaimers who praise Luther for exalting the understanding over faith. I mean those who actually know his writings, and who know he deserves that praise about as much as Brutus deserved canonization by the French sansculotte for slaying the aristocrat Julius Cæsar. They would think it an ill compliment to say a man lacked faith and wished to destroy it in others. They readily admit that “A man is justified by faith” is more characteristic of Luther than his opposition to popes or masses, and that the acts of his outward life all referred more or less directly to that principle. But, they say, taken out of the swaddling bands of the sixteenth century and allowed to move freely, the principle means this: a man’s inward state, not his performance of prescribed acts or even his worthy outward conduct, entitles him to be called good. Not what he seems, nor even what he does, but what he is, makes him a right and true man. The Church of that day had set this truth at nought, and by the vehemence with which Luther declared it and made men listen, he earned his chief claim to mankind’s gratitude.

I am well convinced that the Romanists had practically denied this principle, and that Luther was the most powerful instrument in reasserting it. But we saw reason to believe that George Fox held the doctrine of inward righteousness as strongly as the German, and yet that there was a marked difference between them. Fox urged his disciples to exercise faith in a spiritual Being, the Lord of their spirits. Luther delighted to declare that this Being had actually taken human flesh and died a human death, and had by these acts redeemed us from a curse and justified us before his Father. Luther’s admirers constantly say he was straightforward and practical, impatient of abstractions, and fond of plain homely images. Here, it seemed to us, lay the secret of those qualities. He was not speaking of a fantastic Being or a mere idea, nor even merely of an object for spiritual apprehension, though he was that in the highest degree. He spoke of one who had identified himself with men and, by the series of outward acts the creed announces, had established his human as well as his divine character. It struck us that this, the strictly Lutheran characteristic, is needed to complete the Quaker doctrine, which without it soon turns to vapour. Whether or not that is so, this peculiarity of Luther’s preaching enabled him to overthrow existing superstitions and so, as we are told, to precede greater deliverances. The Romanists certainly recognised the fact of the Incarnation. But their notions of inward and inherent righteousness, and the practices built on them, had stripped it of significance. It had ceased to witness to any man of what he was. By affirming the reality and significance of this fact, Luther got rid of the impositions on understanding and conscience that practical unbelief in it had made possible. So it seemed to us that we do not merely strip Luther of his dress, but destroy the man, when we make him witness for a principle and not for a fact. We do not keep the quality that made him a reformer and discard what he shared with those he reformed. We take away the very source of his reforming power, and leave ourselves, if all history is not a delusion, in danger of falling back under the sensible tyrannies he freed us from. If I am asked again what my objection to the Lutheran system is, I answer: only this. It does not bear witness to the all-importance of the fact Luther said was all-important. It teaches us to believe in justification by faith instead of in a Justifier, and substitutes for Christ a notion or scheme of Christianity.

Quakerism and pure Protestantism belong chiefly, if not wholly, to individual life and experience. Unitarianism, we found, was altogether different: it took men from self-reflection to thoughts about nature and God. Yet it has changed its complexion as men’s views of themselves have changed. It applied material standards to the Infinite while the habit of the time was to regard men as purely material. When that habit ceased, it began to decry ordinary theological language as too earthly and definite. Here, perhaps, is a system that exactly answers the philosophers’ demand, readily dropping one period’s dress for another’s. But will it be said that this is merely a change of dress? Those who just now called the acknowledgment of man’s spiritual powers the very essence of religion cannot so change their note as to call it an accident. By their own showing, Unitarianism has not kept its identity, and changing popular opinion has abolished its very substance. But it seemed to us that this was not so, and that it has a principle which is constantly and invariably true. Its hold on earnest minds came from declaring the unity of God, the absolute love of God, and the existence of a good and pure state for mankind to be primary truths that no experience or dogma can alter or set aside. These are eternal principles, not subject to changes of costume or fashion, and needed by man at all times. Our objection to the Unitarian system was that it represented these truths feebly and miserably, and even contradicted them in practice as no other system ever has. That charge applies to the modern scheme even more strongly than to the old. The whole virtue of Unitarianism lay in asserting God’s existence as distinct from men’s thoughts and apprehensions, whereas the later teachers keep drifting toward confusing our own spiritual “nature” with the Being it acknowledges. From there the step to worshipping the separate feelings and moods of that “nature,” and then to prostration before them as idols, is very short. A strange apotheosis for Unitarianism!

I contend, then, that the principles of Fox, Luther and the Unitarians are too strong and vital to bear the imprisonment the various systems have put them in. But I do not think they will become more true and vital when they lose their religious and personal character and are translated into terms of a philosophical theory. I believe they will then lose all their preciousness, and reach the perfect impotence and insignificance to which so far they have been only partly reduced.

II. Even in their strength, though, these principles have shown a disposition to clothe themselves in some form. Only in times of great weakness do they rest content as notions or opinions in individual minds. So we must ask what this tendency means, and how it may be satisfied when the systems that arose from it no longer satisfy it. To answer, we must turn to another class of facts. The second distinguishing Quaker tenet was that a spiritual and universal kingdom is established in the world. We can conceive, though with great difficulty, that the doctrine of the Indwelling Word might have been received merely as a doctrine, the governing law of individual lives. But this tenet ceases to be a principle for individuals if it is only for them. That there should be such a kingdom, and an honest man who believes it not ask what the conditions of citizenship in it are, is incredible. We traced the origin of the Quaker society and system to this conviction. By joining the society, Fox’s disciples sought a place in the kingdom. By adopting the system, they interpreted its nature and laws to themselves and others. So our main question about the society was whether it answers to this character, or even still claims to. About the system we asked what explains the failure to which the body’s history so clearly witnesses. I will not dwell on the answer, beyond noting that a resolute avoidance of forms was a main characteristic of the Quaker system, and a tendency to formalism one of the most striking characteristics of the Quaker body. My present aim is to remark on the faith that made Quakerism necessary, not on its good or evil features.

Was this faith new, and was Fox the first to proclaim it? We found a very strong and distinct acknowledgment of a spiritual kingdom among the Reformers as well as the Quakers. It worked so powerfully in Luther’s mind, and was so tied to his recognition of a divine Person, a divine Man, as the object of all trust and allegiance, that he was most reluctant to introduce any theory or scheme of doctrine that might become a substitute for it. Yet we found that it finally drove him and the other Reformers to set up little Churches or kingdoms of their own, since they could not imagine how else God’s purpose could be accomplished. And Fox’s protest in the next age was provoked partly by the unspiritual character of these bodies and their evident inadequacy to express Christ’s spiritual kingdom. It was provoked partly also by the importance the Reformers and their followers gave to national societies, and the confusion that seemed to have arisen between them and the universal body.

This idea of a spiritual and universal kingdom was not new in the seventeenth century, but may it not have become obsolete in the nineteenth? The history of Unitarianism was an important link in the evidence. In the last century the idea was distinctly and formally repudiated by those most admired for wisdom and enlightenment, and man was excluded from any concern in such a kingdom, having no faculties to take cognizance of it. At the same time the idea of a very comprehensive world, including all nations, systems and religions, became prevalent and was set against the sects of Christendom. So one half of the belief of earlier ages, the half Protestants had regarded least, was trying to assert itself under new conditions, to the exclusion of the other half. If the Roman empire from Augustus to Diocletian was not a sufficient type of this all-tolerant, all-including world, the French Empire, which inherited and carried out the speculations of the last generation, was a fair specimen of what it must be. While that Empire spread philanthropy through Europe, we saw the other element of the idea of a kingdom for mankind, which philanthropy had cast aside as unnecessary, slowly reappear in several quarters. First came a religious awakening: men became strongly convinced that a spiritual power is at work among them. This brought a greater value for personal religion. Then it brought a desire among those who felt it for combination and fellowship in spiritual objects. Finally it raised the question whether such a combination must not have a spiritual foundation and be connected with belief and worship. Next, various philosophical men showed a new habit of thinking about the constitution of our race. They tended to see man as essentially spiritual, and so to conclude that his highest acts must be spiritual. With this faith grew another, that there must be a region for those acts, a world adapted and formed for them, since they cannot merely turn in upon themselves. We could find no clear account of this world except that it was the universe around us, which our eyes and ears take in. And though that universe was proclaimed the great inheritance of mankind, we could not learn that more than a few gifted poets and sages had any right of admission to its meanings and mysteries. The philosophers had another difficulty: does a distinct spiritual world exist at all, or is it only created out of this common world by a class endowed with faculties for the purpose? Another set of deep and earnest thinkers decided this peremptorily. They seemed to have proved that there is something man did not create, to which he must in some way refer all his acts and thoughts, and which must be assumed as their ground.

Meanwhile, the most eager and passionate demands for a universal constitution that men as men could enter came not from religious or philosophical men but from labourers, handicraftsmen and serfs. The nature of this constitution had been discussed again and again, and its settlement had not been left to discussion but put to the most severe practical tests. Three inferences seemed to follow. First, every modern attempt to construct a universal society had been defeated by men’s determination to assert their own wills. Second, the true universal society must neither overlook those wills nor treat them as an inconvenient accident, a friction to be regretted and allowed for, but must assume them as the very principle and explanation of its existence. Third, men cannot be content with a spiritual society that is not universal, or with a universal society that is not spiritual. This doctrine, then, cannot be called obsolete or turned into a mere philosophical notion. Time has strengthened it, and there is more need now than ever that it be satisfied in practice, not in theory.

III. These observations suggest that the spiritual and universal society must be involved in the very idea of our human constitution; or rather, that it must be that constitution, by which we realize there is a humanity and that we form a kind. But, it will be said, may we not suppose that this constitution has been making itself known gradually as civilization advances? When it is more widely diffused, each man will feel his place in it, rightly and harmoniously exercise the spiritual powers that fit him for it, and let his neighbour exercise his without molestation, even helping him. Strifes of opinion that have hindered cooperation will gradually cease, and peace and good will become general. One means to this end may be giving up the notions of the early ages, kept alive by the religious sects and systems since: that the character of this constitution has been revealed in an inspired Book, and that it is ruled in some incredible manner by a divine Person. This is the last hint I shall consider, and it leads to another class of facts we have reviewed.

The Quakers, we found, disparaged what they called the outward letter. They feared reverence for the Bible would interfere with belief in a Spiritual Invisible Teacher. Yet it was in the Bible that Fox learnt clearly that there is such a Teacher, and from the Bible that he preached it to others. From the Bible Luther, torn by inward conflicts, learnt not merely justification by faith but, still more, its personal form and its conception as a vital truth rather than a scholastic dogma. Calvin received the belief of Election, in its highest and purest form, from the same source. The Unitarian prized the Bible as the great witness for the Divine Unity, for God’s absolute and universal love, and for the fact that mankind is under a better condition than a curse. So whenever a man has held one of these precious convictions, he has revered the Scriptures as its teacher and authority, and whenever he has carried it home to his brethren, the Scriptures have been his instrument. This is the more remarkable beside another fact the study of the systems revealed: as any of them has consolidated, the Bible has been deposed from its real dignity, even if nominally held up to admiration or worship. The Quaker turns it into a system of conceits and allegories under pretence of honoring the Spirit. He has treated it no worse than the Lutheran or Calvinist who cuts it into texts to confirm dogmas or serve as mottos for sermons, or the Unitarian who would reduce it to a collection of moral maxims. So, rather than doubting the value or authority of the book all these appeal to, because the systems are unstable and helpless, may we not say boldly this? The book revealed to each founder of a sect the aspect of the spiritual economy he was called to present. And it has been a perpetual and most embarrassing witness against the effort to compress that economy into the rules and formulas he and his followers devised to state their opinions.

But this is not enough. It is alleged that whatever may be true of religious bodies, the greatest light on our spiritual constitution has lately come from people who did not draw their knowledge from the Scriptures, and who even doubted their value and authenticity.

I have not affected to disparage the labours of philosophers, now or in the past. I have the highest respect for those who have brought to light precious truths about faculties in us that were supposed not to exist. But I have also suggested an opinion, which I am anxious to have sifted and, if false, exposed: that this is precisely the limit of their work. They have proved that we have faculties that take cognizance of spiritual transcendental objects, but not what those objects are. They have shown that we must have a spiritual constitution, but not what it is. I do not deny that we have learnt what our forefathers did not know, or knew far less well. I do not deny that experiments, failures and contradictions have made us better acquainted with what we are and want. For mankind’s sake I am thankful these men were permitted to make their discoveries, apparently without seeing the truths that I think answer to them, and which show that Tantalus is not the one type of humanity. But nothing I see or hear of these writers leads me to think they have found any substitute for a Revelation of what man’s highest necessities require. I am convinced that their teaching, honestly received, will make his cries for one more passionate. I am convinced it will be seen at last that the book which has always met its readers’ cravings, and given them the glimpse of the mysterious world they needed, contains the full declaration of the state God has established for us, which we have toiled all our lives to find.

The second part of the question is bound up with the first and is perhaps the most important for our purpose. Is not the idea of a spiritual King, an actual Person superintending and ordaining the movements of the universal and spiritual society, the dream of a past age? Would not a sensible man who was also honest, and used words in their simple straightforward sense, be reluctant rather than anxious to bring it forward? Is it not obvious that every step of thought and discovery has carried us further from such a notion and left it as the proper possession of old wives and children? I have perhaps already treated this point implicitly. But I am so anxious to consider it directly that I will, at the risk of repetition and tedium, return to my former method of proof.

A belief in a direct spiritual government over the life, thoughts, acts and words of those who submit to it was, we have seen, the third distinguishing peculiarity of Quakerism. It produced so many more outward results than the other two that modern Friends think it has absorbed them. The society’s system seems expressly devised to express this belief. Did it seem to us that the system was decaying because it had borne too decisive and consistent a witness to this bygone notion, or had kept it from the changes that increasing light and civilisation ought to bring? On the contrary, Quakerism’s essential feebleness lay in showing the doctrine of spiritual superintendence in an inadequate, inconsistent, shrivelled form. It testified that sudden thoughts, acts and speeches, often obviously trifling, came from divine teaching and inspiration. It virtually excluded from the spiritual sphere what even Quakers must acknowledge as the most significant part of our life, the calm, orderly, continuous transactions. The Quakers thought education most important, yet on their system’s terms education could not be a spiritual work.

Turn from Quakerism to its opposite, Calvinism, and we were forced to the same inference in another form. Belief in the will of God as the only spring of Good, Order and Happiness was the earnest, life-giving principle in Calvin and his disciples, and all their brave acts and good thoughts sprang from it. Had they pushed it too far? Had their system riveted the notion of a ruling Will in their minds and perpetuated it into an age when it ought naturally to have been abandoned? We were led to exactly the opposite opinion. By setting aside the idea of a human will, their system left the doctrine of a Divine will barren and meaningless. The idea of a personal Ruler disappeared, and those most anxious to assert the government of the living God became the great propagators of an atheistical Necessity.

But it may be said that these Quaker and Calvinist opinions, about the Spirit working in man and the absolute Will of God, may involve or be involved in the idea of an actual King of men, yet are not identical with it. That idea turns on the doctrine of an Incarnation: it asserts that one who is Son of Man as well as Son of God is Lord of the world and, in a higher sense, of the spiritual Society. This doctrine, it is said, is so connected with the oldest fables of the world that it must give way before the light of truth. The Unitarian of the last century thought so, and the question we discussed was how this opinion, the root of their system, affected the principles that really formed the faith of their better men.

It seemed to us that the unity of God was sacrificed, because the person acknowledged as the great object and centre of human admiration was denied to be one with the Father. The love of God was sacrificed, because it was denied that he had personally interfered on behalf of his creatures. Our being children of God was sacrificed, because nothing gave the name Father reality or showed it to be more than a loose and almost blasphemous figure of speech.

Accordingly, in the new Unitarianism Jesus of Nazareth is coming to be recognised as merely one of the world’s heroes, perhaps the most conspicuous, perhaps not. But this belief, the last and highest discovery of the nineteenth century, takes us back to a stage of history when universal fellowship was impossible. There was a Grecian Hercules and an Egyptian Hercules; he who repealed bad laws was the hero of a country, and he who drained a marsh of a neighbourhood. Men were crying and sighing for someone to be head and prince of all these, the Lord of their race, who would rescue it from the evils it suffers as a race and connect it with the absolute Being their consciences witnessed. Is not this a strange and melancholy relapse under the name of progression!

We have, then, reasonable excuse for asking whether there is on this earth a spiritual and universal kingdom that the religious systems have not been able to supersede or destroy, that is likely to show itself when they have all perished, and with which we of the nineteenth century may have fellowship. As a preface to this inquiry, it seems fitting to ask whether there are traces of a spiritual constitution in the early ages of the world, and whether the books of Scripture help us to interpret them.
