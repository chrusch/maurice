To the Rev. Derwent Coleridge, Stanley Grove, Chelsea

Dedicating the book to Derwent Coleridge, Maurice traces his debts to Samuel Taylor Coleridge’s poems and prose, while admitting where he departs from him on baptism, ministry, and the sacraments.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

MY DEAR MR. COLERIDGE,

In a note to your volume on the Scriptural character of the English Church, you mentioned the first edition of these Hints. You meant to correct one of my many inaccuracies. That was a kind service, and you made it more welcome by approving my general design. I would have valued such recognition from anyone who had thought so seriously about the subject. I valued it more from you, because your work had qualities that might have made me fear you would be less tolerant of mine. Its calm, scholarly tone and careful English style contrast strongly with the crudeness and haste of my Letters to a Quaker. Yet I was delighted to find that neither you nor the accomplished Editor of Mr. Coleridge’s works was kept by these defects from taking an interest in my thoughts. You both saw in them one of a thousand signs of the influence your father’s writings are having on this generation.

Everyone who has felt this influence must want to acknowledge it, so you may well be surprised that in a book of some length I referred to it so seldom. Twenty years ago you might have put this down to a cowardly fear of being linked with an unpopular name. But when I wrote, no one could have had such a motive. The English parties that in Mr. Coleridge’s lifetime differed only in how much they disliked him were now scrambling for a share of his opinions. The only danger of another reaction seemed to me to lie in his admirers’ ambition to make him answerable for their own statements of his views, or for their inferences from them. I did not want to contribute to that. I never had the privilege of knowing him, so I had no means of correcting the impressions I took from his works. I was liable to great mistakes in interpreting them, and to the moral temptation of bending them to my own purposes. I thought it better to seem ungrateful and a plagiarist, even to myself, than to risk using his name to support sentiments he might have disapproved. I also did not want to keep anyone from profiting by his wisdom because I had taught them to connect it with my follies.

This caution did little good. The only two reviews that, so far as I know, paid my book any attention, one treating it with extreme kindness and the other with unbounded contempt, both put my name beside Mr. Coleridge’s, flatteringly but undeservedly. I could not help fancying that one critic would gladly have had readers credit the master with the monstrous absurdity, self-sufficiency, love of priestcraft, hatred of the rights of conscience, and preference of Fathers and Councils to Scripture that were declared characteristic of the disciple. Anyone spoken of in this way should explain, as well as he can, how far the charge is true that he took his method of thought from his supposed teacher, and, if it is true, how far that teacher answers for his use of the method. I want to make that explanation now, to ease my own mind and to rescue your father’s memory from any injury I may have done it. I might have made my confession to many dear friends who admire his writings. I choose to make it to one of his family, for two reasons. First, those who have profited most by what he taught do not and cannot form a school, and the English public, with its party notions, should not suppose they do. Second, my feeling toward him, though not based on personal acquaintance, is so strictly and vividly personal that I cannot bear to think of him chiefly as a writer of books, and I am always glad to connect him with a living representative.

Some people cannot feel affection for a book unless they can associate it with a living man. I am not sure I share that incapacity, but I admit that Mr. Coleridge’s books interest me mainly as the biography of a man who passed through the struggles of the age we are succeeding, and who, after great effort and sorrow, found a resting-place. There are the juvenile poems showing him seeking in Unitarianism a refuge from the flatness and falsehood of a mere State Christianity. There is the fierce, magnificent ode in which he sees the old European world of convention and oppression falling apart and rejoices. There is the noble recantation of his hopes from republican ascendancy. And there is his ode to Despondency, which so perfectly gives the feelings of a man who, after all practical hopes failed, sought deliverance in meditation, and on returning to the actual world found its glory gone and his capacity for enjoyment dead. These poems seem to me so intensely and painfully real, and so expressive of what thousands of minds must have felt in different measures, that I doubt I have ever judged any of them merely as art. I do not think this conflicts with the belief that in him, as in every great poet, creative power implied self-forgetfulness and the ability to pass beyond personal experience. No one can utter the thoughts of others as well as his own, or be in any degree the spokesman of his time, without this quality. But I should imagine it nearly always goes with much inward suffering, since the person who enters most into what many others experience does, in the liveliest sense, experience it himself. I have no right to speak on these points, and if I speak ignorantly you must remember that I only report my own impressions, not a standard for other readers. Your father’s greater poems, such as the Ancient Mariner and Christabel, belong undoubtedly to pure imagination. But I question whether I would be as interested even in these if I did not find many veins and fibres connecting them with his personal being, and if they did not help me read the history of his mind and so the history of our time.

Just as I have never learnt to separate his poetic genius from himself, I fear I cannot appreciate him formally and abstractly as a philosopher. In his “Friend” I seem to find the same man I had known amid the storms of the revolutionary period. He is no less impatient of mere rules and decrees than he was then, but the impatience has taken a new form. He has become convinced that society is a reality, and that unmaking and reconstructing it would not make it more real. So he asks what the grounds of its reality are, and how we may be kept from turning it into a fiction and a falsehood. I do not say this inquiry is complete and satisfactory. I am glad it is not, since I believe a more complete one would not have been half so profitable as this has been and will be for generations. Its merit is that it is an inquiry: it shows us what to seek and puts us in the way of seeking. That is why it has offended more than one class of persons. The mere Destructive complains that it recognizes the worth of what ought to be swept away. The mere Conservative is indignant that it will not take existing rules and opinions as an ultimate basis, but tries to find their meaning and foundation. The man of Compromises is most bitter, because it assumes the statesman has some law of conduct other than sailing with the wind. The mere Englishman is angry that taxes, libels, and the bombardment of Copenhagen are not treated as his favourite journals treat them. The man of Abstractions cannot see what such topics have to do with a scientific book. This combination of enemies, each able to call the book “neither one thing nor the other,” is enough to explain any unpopularity it has met. Explaining its power despite these disadvantages, and others I need not hint at in writing to you, is harder. How has a book said to be utterly unpractical changed men’s minds on the most practical subjects? How has a book said to have no sympathy with the spirit of this age affected the most thoughtful of our young men? I hope some Reviewer will one day undertake it. I am not trying to solve such problems, only to account for its influence on my own mind. That influence is owed mainly to the very peculiarities that seem to have lowered its worth in the opinion of wiser people. For this I am at least thankful: the book has not lessened my interest in others on the same subject, nor tempted me to set Mr. Coleridge up as the one teacher on it. It has enabled me to honour others, very different, from our own and earlier times, whom I might otherwise have misunderstood and, through ignorance and self-conceit, undervalued. Above all, it taught me to reverence the facts of history, and to believe that the least perversion of them to extract a moral is both a folly and a sin.

I have found similar help, in a different department of thought, in that still more irregular work, the Biographia Literaria. A young man today who is tormented by the puzzles of Society and the many systems for dissolving them is haunted almost as much by the problems of Criticism. He feels the connexion between his own life and the books he reads. He meets theories about that connexion, authoritative dogmas on the worth or worthlessness of particular poems and paintings, and paradoxical rebellions against those dogmas. He asks what authority antiquity has and what province belongs to our own time, tries to find permanent laws of art, and hears indignant assertions of its independence of all laws. Anyone who has watched himself or his contemporaries, or even read my rough list of these difficulties, will feel how tangled they are with our thoughts on the highest subjects. To say we need not understand ourselves on these questions, or that principles about them do not matter, is just to say we should not meddle with them. Someone never brought into contact with such topics need not think about them; but if he is, he will find that having no thoughts about them hinders him more than he expects in matters directly touching his personal life. Now if anyone reads Mr. Coleridge’s literary life expecting a great poet to supply the principles of his art ready made, for immediate use and export, he will be disappointed. I cannot find a system here any more than in his political work. I have lately heard there is one, taken whole and alive from a great German author. I speak only of what I saw myself, and it escaped my notice. I seemed to see a writer feeling his way into questions that had puzzled me, and explaining his own progress out of the belief that all things depend on association, into the acknowledgment of something with which they are associated, and the discovery of a keynote to the harmony. From him, by practical illustration, I learnt how to enter into the spirit of a living or departed author without presuming to judge him, and how to learn what he means without imputing our meanings to him. I learnt that beauty is neither accidental nor artificial, but is to be sought as something in both nature and the mind of man, which by God’s law binds us to nature. But all this comes out in a natural, experimental method, through tests and trials in which a man may be greatly helped by another’s successes and failures, as Faraday may be helped by Davy. He cannot simply adopt them from another; we can adopt them from him only by catching his spirit of investigation and applying it to new facts.

The “Aids to Reflection” is a book of a different character, and I feel much deeper and more solemn obligations to it, though of the same kind. I want a politician or critic who has worked his own way through the region where he acts as my guide. I would be most dissatisfied with someone who wrote moral and spiritual aphorisms without showing he was himself in conflict with an evil nature and a reluctant will, and had received the truths he shares with me not at second hand but as the needed supports of his own being. I know no book that gave me clearer tokens of this than this one. Admirers and objectors alike describe it as dealing with religion philosophically. In whatever sense that is true, and in an important sense I believe it is, I can testify that it delivered me from many philosophical phrases and generalisations that attach themselves to the truths of the Creed, even in minds that think they receive Christianity with a childlike spirit. It helped me see that the deepest principles are ones the Peasant can grasp and enter into as well as the Schoolman. I value his philosophy mainly because it led me to this discovery, and to the practical conclusion that teachers must cultivate their understandings. They must do so in order to tell what is factitious and accidental, belonging to our artificial habits of thought, from what is fixed and eternal, belonging to man as man, which God will open the eyes of every humble man to see. In this way I learnt the preciousness of the simple Creeds of antiquity, and the inward witness that a gospel of facts has and a gospel of notions must lack. I learnt how the most awful and absolute truths, which notions displace or obscure, are bound up in facts. Through facts they can be held by those who cannot compare notions and have a blessed incapacity for resting in them.

The first person to apply this principle to religious questions will inevitably be caught sometimes in the obscurity he is trying to free us from. Whoever starts to challenge notions and theories will himself be counted the greatest notionalist and theorist. To clear away crudities and confusions he will sometimes have to adopt or invent a nomenclature. His strict adherence to it will be called pedantry. His followers will deserve the charge if they repeat his words instead of carrying their meaning into their studies and lives. His enemies will have a plausible pretext for saying he has made simple truths complex. The “Aids to Reflection” has suffered all these misfortunes. Yet I have heard it denounced as unintelligible by people I found very hard to understand, who kept perplexing me with hard words to which I could find nothing corresponding among actual things. They tried to explain mysteries by the very events that were most mysterious to me, and which, being nearest to me, I most needed to understand for my practical life. On the other hand, I have heard the simplest, most childlike men and women express an almost rapturous thankfulness for being allowed to read it, since it helped them understand their own hearts, their Bibles, and the connexion between them. I believe it has offended, and always will offend, many, not for its theories but for its essentially practical character. Its manly denunciation of the sentimental school must pain many today who have in practice adopted the Rousseau cant with slightly altered words. They praise men for being good though they do monstrously evil acts, and think it a vulgar worship of decency to say that one who is the slave of his own passions, and enslaves others to them, may not be a very right and true man after all. Yet those who really exalt decency above inward truth and conformity to a high standard will not own Mr. Coleridge as an ally just because the school that pretends to oppose them rejects him. The whole aim of his book is to draw us from a merely worldly and external morality to what concerns the heart and the inner man. Here again he is unfortunate, for those who have turned “heart-religion” into a phrase, and put the feelings and experiences of their minds in place of the laws those feelings might, rightly used, lead us to, will be sure to see him as peculiarly their enemy. If everyone in the land belonged to one of these classes, and many had not tried them all and grown weary of them all, it would be hard to understand how this volume has found its way into so many studies and so many hearts.

The idea of the first “Lay Sermon,” that the Bible is the Statesman’s Manual, is less developed than any of those I have mentioned. But the bare announcement of it has been worth more to me than any lengthy exposition I know. No topic in these volumes has engaged me more than the national history of the Bible, and I have said very little that did not have that thought as its germ.

The little book on Church and State you will suppose, from the title and character of these volumes, I have studied more attentively still. If you watch me closely, you will probably find many more thoughts I have stolen from it than I am aware of, though I am conscious of obligations in superabundance. The doctrine I have tried to bring out on the relations between Church and State seems to me nothing but an expansion of Mr. Coleridge’s remark on the opposition and necessary harmony of Law and Religion. Here, as elsewhere, I have departed from his phraseology and even adopted one he might not sanction.

The robberies I have confessed are such in the truest sense: conscious and deliberate. If someone found twenty or thirty pages in my book traceable to some English or foreign author, common sense, even without charity, would make him hesitate to charge me with wilful fraud. It is far likelier that I mistook for my own something long mixed into my compositions than that I took such a stupid, blundering way of earning a reputation that a few years must destroy. A court of justice, on the mere evidence, would lean to the tolerant side. If it hesitated, it would be either because an insignificant author can do with impunity what a famous writer with enemies on every side would be mad to try, or because someone who had merely forgotten would have had a vague impression of reading the passage elsewhere. Being honest, he would then have dropped some hint, even a wrong one, of where it came from, making the reader curious to see what the other writer said. If I had done that, any competent judge who did not deliberately pervert his judgment would drop all suspicion of evil design. But my use of your father’s writings is of an entirely different kind. It could not be proved by collating paragraphs, so if I wished to conceal it I would be really, not apparently, dishonest. And this holds even though the main subject of my book is one he has not, so far as I know, distinctly treated. The thoughts he scatters about it, though deeply interesting, do not always satisfy me, so I have commonly been without his guidance and have sometimes wilfully deserted it. I will not fulfil the purpose of this letter if I do not show how these two apparently opposite statements are reconciled.

No man will be of much use to his generation who does not give himself mainly to the questions occupying those who belong to it. An antiquary, I daresay, has an easier and quieter life than one who interferes with his contemporaries and joins their speculations. But his quiet is his reward, and those who seek another must give it up. Every man is often tempted to rest in some little nook of thought where others will not molest him because he does not molest them. Those with work assigned to them are soon driven by an irresistible power out of such retirement into the dusty highways of ordinary business and dispute. This, I think, was your father’s peculiar merit and honour. The subjects he addressed were not those his poetical or metaphysical leanings would have chosen. They were exactly the questions of the time, those other men were discussing in the spirit of the time. As we of a younger generation have inherited these questions, we inherit also the wisdom that dealt with them. But there are questions we have not inherited, which belong more expressly to us than to our immediate predecessors. I suspect we must humbly study these for ourselves. It will make a great difference whether we invent a way of investigation or try to walk a path that better men have cleared of rubbish and marked with footprints and signposts.

One such question is the one your father was led, by the soundest wisdom I believe, largely to banish from his consideration after the French Revolution taught him the unspeakable importance of a distinct National life. I mean the question whether there is a Universal Society for man as man. I have given reasons in these volumes why everyone today must be more or less consciously occupied with this inquiry, and why no other topic, however important, can keep it from taking nearly the most prominent place in our minds. Another question, apparently from a different region of thought but touching this at more than one point, is how all thoughts, schemes, systems, and speculations may contribute their share to one that shall be larger and deeper than any of them. If I am indebted to your father on one account more than another, it is for showing me a way out of the dreadful vagueness and ambition such a scheme involves. He led me not merely to say but to feel that knowledge of The Being is the object we must strive after, and that all pursuit of Unity without this is the pursuit of a phantom. Yet I cannot help believing there is a right meaning hidden in this desire, that it will haunt us till we find it, and that we cannot merely denounce or resist it in ourselves or others. We shall do far more good, perhaps the very good we are meant to do in this age, if we steadily consider it. A further question thrusts itself on us continually and stirs more party feeling just now than any other. It concerns the doctrines expressed in the old Creeds about the nature of God himself. Are they to be taken on trust from the early ages, or treated as matters for our own inquiry, acknowledged only so far as they accord with what seems to us the declaration of Scripture or the verdict of reason? In preparing to consider this great subject I have felt, with many others, that Mr. Coleridge’s help has been invaluable. Nearly every thoughtful writer of the day would have taught us that the highest truths lie beyond the limits of Experience, and that the essential principles of the Reason cannot be proved by syllogisms. The evidence for them is the impossibility of admitting what falls under the law of experience unless we recognize them as its foundation, and the impossibility of believing that we or anything else exist except on these terms. The atheism of Hume has driven men to these blessed discoveries. It was your father’s honour to assert them to an age and nation that had not yet seen the need of them, but he did not pretend, and no one should pretend, that he was their first reviver or expositor. The application of these principles to Theology, however, I believe we owe mainly to him. He taught me that by the very law of the Reason the knowledge of God must be given to it, that the moment it tries to create its Maker it denies itself, and that the most opposite kind of Unity to the one Unitarianism dreams of is needed if the reason’s demands are to be met. I must acknowledge I received this from him, or prove ungrateful to the highest Teacher, who might have chosen another instrument but has been pleased, in many cases I know of, to use this one. This instruction seems to me a most precious preparation for the inquiry that belongs more strictly to our age, but only a preparation. Reading the profound and, to a theological student, invaluable hints on the Trinity in Mr. Coleridge’s writings, I cannot help feeling: “This is not enough.” If reason is, as he said, expressly the human faculty, belonging to rich and poor alike, then not only the truths belonging to each person’s condition but this highest truth, which he presents as demanding the highest efforts of thought and abstraction, must belong to the humblest man. It must be a sacred part of his inheritance, and must somehow be capable of being presented to him. Anyone who entertains this thought will find this theological subject soon tangled with the other two. The hope that some day

“Wisdom may teach her lore
In the low huts of them that toil and groan,”

grows much brighter if we can really believe the deepest lore is the most universal. The hope that diverse sides of thought may be reconciled can begin to part from the dreary vision of a comprehensive System from which all life is excluded, if the central Unity is that of the living Being.

But how can such a dream ever be realized? To me the promise of it came in sounds that belong to our nursery, in the words by which our infants are baptized. Here it seemed to me lay the assurance that this truth belongs to no esoteric region. It is one of those all-embracing mysteries that surround us at every moment and gradually draw us into themselves, and it becomes most truly ours when we gain most of the privilege of men by becoming most like little children. So I was led to consider Baptism as a key to the nature of ordinances generally. I found they had been much prized by Luther and by the most earnest of those who, like him, regarded Christianity almost solely in its reference to their own personal life. They felt the extreme danger of substituting their belief for its object, and so destroying the reality of both. Their testimony was of the highest practical value, and the experience of those who had rejected ordinances to reach a more spiritual state of mind abundantly confirmed it to me. Still I could not see how someone starting from their point of view could ultimately escape the conclusion so generally adopted by the Reformers’ disciples: that he who first reveres inward Truth and then comes to revere outward Signs begins in the spirit and is made perfect in the flesh. I could entirely sympathize with Mr. Coleridge’s feeling that those who exalt Ordinances by turning them into Charms are not harmlessly adding to what was sufficient before, but destroying its meaning and reality. But suppose they are signs to the Race, signs of the existence of that universal body we were inquiring after. Then they take on a very different importance. They become indispensable in a higher sense than their champions dream, who seem to value them chiefly as means of exclusion. They are the very voice in which God speaks to his creatures, the witness that their fellowship with each other rests on their fellowship with him and both on the mystery of his Being, and the means by which we are meant to rise to the highest blessings he has given us. So there rose before me the idea of a Church Universal, built not on human inventions or human faith but on the very nature of God and the union he has formed with his creatures. It is a Church revealed to man as a fixed and eternal reality by means that infinite wisdom itself devised. Its tokens and witnesses, it seemed to me, must be Divine. But the feeling of its necessity, and apprehensions of its different sides and aspects, must, if it is a reality, be found in all the schemes that express human thought and feeling. No amalgamation of these can create a real harmony, but each may find its highest meaning in the harmony God has created, of which he is himself the centre.

These are the leading thoughts I have tried to express in this book. So you will understand me when I say I may have uttered innumerable sentiments for which your father would not have chosen to be responsible, even while I wished to study and apply his lessons. I conclude he would not have agreed with my views on Baptism. He would probably have thought I over-exalted the Ministry, would not have accepted every statement of mine on the Eucharist, and would have judged me wrong in some opinions about the Scriptures. On all these subjects I have deviated from what I think would have been his judgment, without losing any reverence and affection for his memory, and perhaps without coming any nearer than he did to the sentiments of any party dividing the Church. I am sure I would not have had courage to differ from them or him if he had not helped me believe that Truth is above both, most of all above myself and my petty notions, that it is worth seeking and loving above all things, and that He who is Truth is ready, if we obey him, to guide us into it.

I have been so occupied with a subject I am sure interests you above all others that I have left myself no time to express as I would wish my gratitude for your personal kindness and for the benefit of my opportunities of intercourse with you. But I cannot conclude without wishing you God-speed in the noble undertaking you are engaged in. If you are permitted to raise up a body of wise and thoughtful teachers out of our trading classes, you will do more for the Church than all those who write treatises about it. The trials and discouragements will be in proportion to the importance and novelty of the work. In these I trust you will be sustained by the highest consolations a Christian man and priest can know. But there will be times when you need lower helps too, if they are of the right kind. I can think of few more cheering than the recollection that you are carrying into effect principles your father urged on our countrymen years ago. You are doing what you can to prove that one who was called a theorist and a dreamer was in truth labouring to bring the most practical benefits to his country and to mankind.

Believe me, My dear Mr. Coleridge, Yours very sincerely, F. MAURICE.
