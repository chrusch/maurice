Part II. Of the Catholic Church and the Romish System

Chapter IV. Signs of a Spiritual Society

Baptism, examined against Quaker, Anabaptist, Evangelical, philosophical, and Roman Catholic objections, is defended as the sign that admits every person, without regard for age or feeling, into the same universal, spiritual kingdom founded in Christ.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

We have seen the traces of a spiritual constitution for mankind. Its two parts, united by family ties and by locality, depend on a third part that is universal. A universal society can take two possible forms. One destroys the family and national principle; the other expands it. Scripture calls the first this world and the second the church. The principles of the world exist in the heart of every family and nation. They are simply men’s natural tendencies and inclinations, and they are always threatening to become dominant. When they do, men are no longer recognized as related to a Being above them, or as sharing a common humanity. The Church is the witness for these facts, which it is natural for us to deny, so it must be a distinct body. If it loses its distinctness it loses its meaning, and, though the words may at first sound paradoxical, it loses its universality for all practical purposes. The question to examine, then, is this: are there any signs today of a spiritual and universal body on the earth? Do those signs identify it with the body Scripture speaks of? And are they an effective witness against the world?

Section I.—Baptism.

For the last 1800 years there has existed a rite called Baptism. It is not derived from the national customs of any of the peoples among whom it is found. Tribes of very different origin and character adopted it, and once they had received it they believed themselves members of a common society. That society was supposed to be connected with an invisible world, and with a certain worship and government. An immense proportion of the children of Europe are admitted to the rite very soon after birth, and it is generally performed by a particular class of officials. These are facts that need no proof. The only question is whether they have a meaning, and what it is.

The idea of the Scriptures, as far as we have traced it, is that Jesus Christ came to reveal a kingdom. That kingdom is founded on a union established in his person between man and God and between the visible and invisible worlds, and ultimately on a revelation of the divine Name. If the setting up of this kingdom, and the adoption of men into it, are not connected with baptism in the New Testament, then the facts just noted do not concern us, whatever their import. Even if our Lord Himself enjoined baptism, if He did so in terms that let us suppose it merely incidental to the purposes of His coming, we cannot prove that the universal society which acknowledges it now is the one He founded.

So let us see in the Gospels how far this is the case. One of the first events they announce is that John the Baptist came preaching in the wilderness of Judea, telling people to repent because the kingdom of heaven was at hand, and that Jerusalem and all Judea went out to him and were baptized in the Jordan, confessing their sins. The narrative is at least singular. Baptism is tied to a spiritual act (repentance), to a spiritual promise (remission), to the announcement of a kingdom, and to a hint that the kingdom would not be made up only of Abraham’s children. Suppose the ceremony, as some imagine, was unknown until then: it was introduced at the very moment the kingdom began. Suppose, as others say, it was already used to receive Gentile converts into the outer court: then giving it to Jews signified that they were henceforth to stand on universal human ground. Either way this baptism was the preparation for the Gospel, though for all that appears so far it may have been only a preparation. But Jesus Himself goes down into the water, and as He comes out a voice from heaven proclaims Him the well-beloved Son, and the Spirit descends on Him in bodily shape. So the announcement that the Divine man, the king of men, had appeared was, according to the Gospels, connected with Baptism. They also treat it as the beginning of His public ministry and of all the acts attesting His descent from above. Yet this might have been needed only to mark out the leader, with no application to his disciples. But Jesus preached, “Repent, the kingdom of heaven is at hand”; He appointed Apostles to declare that kingdom; and these Apostles baptize. Even so, the nature of their message might mean that they were only continuing John’s dispensation, and had nothing to do with that higher Baptism of the Holy Ghost and of fire which John said would supersede his own. Our Lord tells Nicodemus that he must be born again to see the kingdom of heaven. What is born of the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the Spirit is spirit; the fleshly man cannot understand even earthly things, much less these heavenly ones, which only He could reveal who had come down from heaven and was in heaven. This declaration of the transcendent character of the kingdom is joined to the words that unless a man is born of water and of the Spirit he cannot enter it. Yet perhaps even here the reference is to the spiritual eye in man, which this ordinance, like the earlier Jewish ordinances, might open, rather than to the actual gift of the promised Spirit; for it is expressly said that the Spirit was not yet given, because Jesus was not yet glorified. After He had risen, He appears to His disciples and says that all power is given Him in heaven and earth, and that they are therefore to preach the gospel to all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost. This language is certainly strange, for it seems to look forward only to a spiritual kingdom. One piece of evidence is still wanting: after Christ’s glorification and the descent of the Holy Ghost, did the Apostles still baptize with water? Peter stood among the disciples and told the Jews that God had made this Jesus, whom they crucified, both Lord and Christ; he told them to repent and be baptized and they would receive the gift of the Holy Ghost; and that same day three thousand were baptized. This may be enough to show that the writers of the Gospels and Acts believed baptism to be the sign of admission into Christ’s spiritual and universal kingdom, and therefore that everyone receiving it was by that very fact a member. A son or servant of the Roman commonwealth, once freed, entered at once on the rights of a citizen, and all its immunities and responsibilities became his. In the same way the young Christian convert, taught from the Scriptures, could not doubt that from his baptism he was free of the brotherhood whose head was his Lord. However lofty the language describing that brotherhood, it described his state. If Christ came to make men sons of God, he was a son of God; if to make them members of His body, he was a member; if to endue them with His Spirit, that Spirit was given to him. His baptism said to him: this is your position, and you are to live according to its conditions. The life will not be easy but a perpetual conflict, a battle not with flesh and blood only but with principalities and powers, the rulers of spiritual wickedness in high places. But understand the battle. Your foes are not keeping you from obtaining a blessing; they are keeping you from enjoying one already obtained for you. They will laugh at you for pretending it is yours and tell you not to claim it. In the strength of this covenant you must claim it, or your life will be a lie. I ask anyone to read the Epistles calmly and say whether baptism could have borne any other sense for those who exhorted men, because they were baptized, to count themselves dead to sin and alive to God. The same holds for those who addressed men they mostly did not know personally, some known to be inconsistent and unholy, as being in Christ, elect, children of God; and for those who urged their disciples not to doubt that they had been admitted into the communion of saints, and warned that they would sink into apostasy if they did. But then I must also ask this. If Christ’s kingdom was declared everlasting, and this sign was fixed from the first as the admission into it, and the sign still exists among us, why are we who have received it not in the same position, with the same privileges and responsibilities, as those who lived eighteen hundred years ago? Is Baptism not the sign of a spiritual and universal kingdom?

Objections.—1. The Quaker.

To this question various answers are given. I will consider first that of the Quakers.

I. The Quakers find it utterly incredible and monstrous that a spiritual fact or operation should be shown by a visible sign. Their argument runs: “Either men are livingly united to the Divine Word, or they are not. If they are, the sign is useless; if they are not, it is false. If Christ’s kingdom depends on outward ceremonies, how does it differ from the Jewish? What do the words mean, that John baptized with water but Christ with the Spirit and fire, if both baptisms are equally outward?”

These positions are so self-evident to the Quaker that Scripture is not allowed to contradict them. Quoting texts and commandments is useless, because these are primary truths that override every text and decide how it is read. If the Apostles acted against them, that only showed the Apostles were still ignorant and Jewish in outlook. Be it so. If these notions are good for anything, and do not contradict the leading positive truths of Quakerism, let them be upheld at all risks. But that is the point I want to examine.

We have seen that Fox did not think it the work of the Gospel to reveal the fact of men’s relation to the Divine Word. He believed that fact was made known to Heathens and to Jews alike, and to the Jews by a sign. By this means the Invisible Teacher told the children of Abraham that there was a union between them and Him. He warned them of the tendency of their fleshly natures to separate from Him, and promised to uphold them against it.

Fox acknowledged this to be the Divine method in the Jewish dispensation. He never claimed that the union made known to the Jew was a material union, for then there would have been nothing to signify, and so no sign. Nor did he claim it was a variable union, depending on the feelings of the people sharing in it, for then the thing signified would change every day, and again there could be no sign. So the fitness of this method, its very possibility, came from the fact that a spiritual and permanent relation was being made known by it. Yet the Quaker’s only reason for abandoning the method is that the Gospel dispensation is spiritual and permanent, not material and transitory! This inconsistency needs a better justification than vague declamation against carnal practices, and angry denunciation of all Christendom, from the Apostles downward, for committing them.

“But it is false to give the sign to anyone who lacks the reality.” What does lacks the reality mean? Does it mean the relation is not real? Then Fox was wrong, for he affirmed it was real, for all men. Or does “real” refer to the man’s feeling and acknowledgment of the relation? Then the proposition says it is false to tell a man a truth because he does not believe it. We are certainly guilty of that falsehood, and so was the whole Old Testament dispensation, and so are Fox and the Quakers themselves.

“But the sign is useless to a man truly united to the Divine Word.” This implies two opinions, both important and both illustrating Quaker feeling and history. One is that it is nothing to a man that a thing is true in itself and universally, so long as he feels it to be true for him. The other is that union with the Divine Word is all men need. Every earnest word of Fox testified against both. First, as I have shown often, the truth of the thing was the ground on which he urged men to base their feeling of it. Second, he declared that union with the Divine Word did not satisfy the Heathens or Jews who perceived it, but made them long for something more, a kingdom of Heaven. Here is evidence for Baptism that all the history of Christendom could not have given, freely offered by those who reject it. Their whole preaching is against Judaism and the old covenant, yet they are thrown back on Judaism and cannot rise above its great doctrine. And they cannot keep even that doctrine. They cannot keep the faith that we are related to the Divine Word, and can only put in its place certain individual feelings and impressions.

With this in mind, let us compare for a moment our reading of John the Baptist’s words with theirs. We say John came baptizing with water unto repentance, for the remission of sins; that was the spiritual meaning of his Baptism. Our Lord’s baptism, we hold, includes that meaning but has a deeper one. It does not merely show that the heart has turned to God and so been delivered from sin. It gives the spirit and power from which repentance and every right act must flow, and it brings its subject under the discipline of the purifying fire that is to consume the old and evil nature. This reading seems literal enough, and it agrees exactly with the promises and anticipations of the prophets and the expositions and retrospections of the Apostles. How does the Quaker improve on it? He makes it the great mark of John that he baptized with water, and of Christ that He would not. So the voice crying in the wilderness said: “Hear, O Israel! Rejoice, O ye Gentiles! The glorious time is at hand which your fathers expected and the whole universe has groaned for: the time when signs are to be abolished. The great Prince and Deliverer is at hand, who will make the things of earth no longer pledges and sacraments of a union with Heaven! This is the consummation of all mankind’s hopes, this is what is meant by the Tabernacle of God being with men, by His dwelling with them, and their being His people, and His being their God.”

2. The Anti-pædobaptist.

II. The Anti-pædobaptist (one who rejects infant baptism) is in many ways the opposite of the Quaker. He values the baptismal sign very highly and believes it is meant to witness to a spiritual kingdom. He generally holds firmly, as the Quaker does not, that God chooses men for their place in the Divine Economy. But he thinks that admitting people with no spiritual consciousness or capacity destroys the ordinance’s meaning. As it exists in modern Europe, he says, it has nothing to do with the Kingdom of Christ.

I would answer this objection with great care, since it touches my whole argument, if it had not already been so fully considered. The question between us and the Anabaptists is not whether a sign points to a kingdom, but what kingdom it should point to. I have maintained, on the authority of Scripture, that the Catholic Church is a kingdom for mankind, grounded on the union between God and man established in Christ. I have maintained that it grew out of a family and a nation, and proved itself the proper and only foundation of both. If this notion is false, it may be reasonable to treat a child, an embryo man, as no citizen of this kingdom. To anyone who believes it true, such a doctrine must seem monstrous. Take a member of either class from which the early Church was formed, first a Heathen. The sins of the Roman empire have alarmed him with coming judgment: the divorces, adulteries, incests and parricides of its leading men. He has seen how little his countrymen’s idea of the gods restrained such atrocities. A Christian Apostle’s preaching has shown him that the evil nature behind all these crimes is in himself. He hears of deliverance from it. God has revealed himself as the enemy of all unrighteousness and also as men’s Father. His Son has come to dwell among men, made himself the brother of our race, claimed its members as members of his body, given them a sign of admission, and promised them his Spirit. Could the man who received this joyful message and obeyed it doubt that he had been received into the true human family, out of a hateful, anomalous, inhuman world? Could he then say, “This child I have begotten belongs to that world. He has a human form and face, the form and face Christ bore, yet the accursed nature I have renounced is his rightful master, the evil society I have fled is his home, and I leave him to the evil spirit who, I believe, has spread his leaven through that nature and society”? I am not reasoning now with someone who thinks little of Baptism, but with one who believes it truly the sign of the redeemed covenant family. I ask him what less than this a Christian convert could understand from being told not to baptize his child because he could not be sure the child was included in Christ’s redemption.

This is not the whole contradiction in such a prohibition. The Gospel reveals truths that are expressed in the forms of family life and seem expressible in no other. The convert reached them through feelings awakened by his home relations, or by his awareness of having violated them. All links between human relations and Divine would seem to him destroyed if he could not speak of his child as joined to him in a spiritual bond. And the Gospel promises a Spirit who awakens all consciousness, conviction and affection. That promise is equally trifled with by the doctrine that these must already exist before anyone can be admitted to the Gospel Covenant.

On this last point the perplexity of a Hebrew Christian told not to baptize his child must have been still worse. His own covenant had been emphatically with children. What replaced it was in every other respect wider and freer, referring every act of the creature more directly to the love and good pleasure of the Creator. Yet Christ is not recorded as saying a single word to this effect: I command you not to follow the analogy of God’s earlier dispensation, and not to suppose that in my kingdom of grace infants count as human and moral beings, as they did under the law. Many recorded acts and words point the opposite way, that infants were a most honoured part of the race he came to seek and save. The convert confesses daily that the Redeemer of humanity entered childhood as well as manhood. Yet he must give up all the habits of thought and feeling God himself had taught him, not because they were too narrow, but because they were too comprehensive for his new position.

So the Anti-pædobaptist cannot plead, and this is his only plea, that baptizing infants is a strange departure from the admitted sense and purpose of the ordinance. I rather suspect the danger lies on the other side. It was so reasonable and inevitable a consequence of the baptismal principle that infants should be received into the Church, and this so clearly explained the Church’s propagation and reconciled it with God’s ordinary providence, that it is no wonder another equally necessary truth was lost sight of in the eagerness to press the practice. It might be forgotten that we baptize children not because they are children but because they are embryo men; that a spiritual blessing needs a receiver as well as a giver; and that Baptism is not a momentary act but a perpetual sacrament. Before I finish this section I may show that some or all of these errors have arisen in the Church, and the rise of a sect of Anti-pædobaptists is no doubt to be attributed to their prevalence. But in every country where Baptism is practiced there stands beside it an institution that bears far more complete witness against such perversions than those who reject the principle itself have been able to bear. This institution does not displace Baptism but confirms, as its name says, the authority and pledges of that sacrament. It declares to the child that the One who has guided it through infancy will be with it in the conscious struggles of manhood, and that it has been made free not only of one congregation but of the Universal Church.

The Anabaptist doctrine, then, like the Quaker’s, strongly supports my position. It shows that the more Baptism’s operation is restricted, the more belief in a human society becomes impossible.

Some will say the Baptism now found in Christendom is invalid because it is usually done by sprinkling rather than immersion. Since this notion is accidentally connected with the Anti-pædobaptist theory, I add a few words on it here. “The practice of the early ages, so far as we can tell, was to immerse. Sprinkling destroys Baptism’s emblematic character as a burial. And if we admit an outward ceremony at all, we cannot then pretend the mode of performing it is indifferent.” There is truth in each of these propositions. I admit, with probably most Churchmen, that early times very probably practiced what is least likely to have been introduced later, and that most known facts confirm this. I admit that “buried with him” in the sixth chapter of Romans is a better argument for immersion than St. Peter’s “sprinkled from an evil conscience” can ever be for the modern custom. And I admit that having received one form and not another as the sign of a certain thing, we have no business making light of the particulars prescribed. But here is the distinction. Our Lord prescribed no particular mode of baptism. He said that water shall be the sign, used with certain words. He did not say, Use it thus and in no other way. However high the probability that a present practice differs from an earlier one, it does not, I contend, become illegitimate if it answers to the terms of the law that ordained it. This applies especially to a Universal Institution. We cannot tell that the old practice, however right, was not shaped by the circumstances of a particular country or time, or that the liberty to alter the mode was not contemplated in the terms of the enactment. A sign divinely instituted for mankind is too serious a thing to be settled by guesses about antiquity. These may be most useful as suggestions and may recover meanings we have lost, but they cannot condemn what the One who appointed the sign has not condemned. Least of all may we suppose that a sign is not big enough or does not involve enough self-sacrifice. That sets aside the first principle on which every sign’s validity rests: the one the Ruler has fixed, great or little, is the right one and must denote what he meant it to denote.

I hope this is plain. If not, the ring in marriage may illustrate it. If a law prescribes a ring for marriages, a ring is indispensable. But the strongest evidence that wedding rings had commonly been gold, and the absence of any evidence that they had been anything else, should not void a marriage concluded with some other ring. This also bears on the alleged loss of the baptismal emblem through sprinkling. Suppose someone impeached a marriage celebrated with a brass or copper ring, arguing that the nobler metal implied purity, and even proved that this notion had attached to it and given rise to the custom. A judge would not listen for an instant, though he would grant most strongly that marriage implies purity and that all marriage institutions aim to preserve it. He would declare at once that the emblem, however interesting, useful for personal meditation, or lawful as the basis of a custom, is altogether distinct from the sign. The prescribed sign testified that the union was complete and final. A purely arbitrary sign with no emblematic value would do this, and its force as arbitrary and appointed must not be lost through considerations of wisdom or propriety. This is especially needful here. A divinely appointed institution will carry profound wisdom and many adaptations to the creatures it is meant for. A symbol like water suggests numerous hints and analogies, and more come to an earnest thinker, even one not fanciful. But the main fact Baptism witnesses to, our adoption into Christ, must be the key to these analogies, not they the key to it. That adoption must be received as a fact on the authority of the sign, without regard to any apparent likeness between sign and thing signified. Otherwise the sign loses its universality and counts for nothing to those who, through a weak faculty of comparison or any other cause, cannot see the resemblance.

These remarks only aim to show that nothing warrants the notion that our present Baptism is not formally the baptism Christ ordained, and so not the sign of admission into his universal and spiritual kingdom, merely because its mode may differ from the Apostles’ practice. Whether any power, and what power, has the right to prescribe a mode of Baptism for the whole Church or any branch of it is a quite separate question, to be considered in its proper place.

3. The Modern Protestant.

III. Next to the Anabaptist comes the self-styled disciple of Luther and Calvin, the modern Protestant or Evangelical. His doctrine is that Christ has two kingdoms, one real and spiritual, the other outward and visible. It is highly desirable, perhaps necessary, that the young as well as the old be admitted into the outward one, and Baptism is the appointed way in. What privileges belong to this “Gentile court” they do not precisely settle. Perhaps some grace is given in Baptism. If not, there is great benefit in being allowed to hear preaching and receive a Christian education, which may be put to greater use later. But the important thing is to press on men that until they are actually and consciously converted they are not members of Christ or children of God. Some in this school believe these words may be applied to baptized people “in a sense.” Ask in what sense, however, and the answers are so vague that they leave a painful impression. The language is awful and significant, yet it seems it may on occasion be played with, or used with a secret reservation.

But those who say this answer that they want to get rid of equivocations, not invent them. They resort to the hypothesis of a double kingdom because plain observation shows that a baptized man may be very evil. Being evil, he seems to have nothing to do with a kingdom of righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost. They also say: “Let people devise whatever theological scheme they please, we know that we, baptized in infancy, passed in manhood from death to life as truly as any heathen in the first age could have done.” It is neither fair nor honest, they contend, to suppress either fact: the one is obvious to every man’s common sense, the other they know in their own consciousness. Yet it is suppressed, and must be, if we take Baptism to be what it is sometimes called, a New Birth, the actual introduction into a spiritual world.

I am as unwilling to deny the melancholy truth that Christian men are not living Christian lives as the Jewish prophet was to pass over the fact that God’s name was blasphemed through his countrymen in all lands. I believe the precedents of Scripture apply strictly to us. The preacher’s function now is what it was then: as the prophet in Jerusalem rebuked sin and called men to repentance, so must we in London or Paris. The question is what sin we are to rebuke and what repentance we are to call for. The Jewish prophet charged his people with forgetting the covenant of their God, and he traced all their sins to that one. The Jew was guilty because he did not claim a Jew’s privileges and did not act as a Jew. Are we to follow this precedent or not? Do we follow it when we say, “This covenant is, I will be to you a father, and you shall be to me sons and daughters; you are acting as if you were not in it, you are forgetting it”? Or do we follow it when we say, “These titles are not yours, or are yours only in some formal imaginary sense,” which in plain English means in no sense at all?

I am just as unwilling to deny or explain away the other assertion, that baptized men who have lived without God in the world are converted to him by his grace. I believe St. Bernard, Tauler, and à Kempis held this as strongly as any modern Methodist, and I might add Loyola and Xavier. They did not confine it to open profligacy, though they did not exclude that. They applied it to laymen and priests who, under a respectable exterior, had sought the praise of men more than the praise of God. Whether we may restrict the word “conversion” to a particular act or crisis is a matter for serious reflection. So is whether every act of repentance is not a conversion, a turning to God, and whether we forget that each such act is as much the work of God’s Spirit as recovery from habitual thoughtlessness and sin. But however these are decided, they do not affect the opinion that someone who has received Christian Baptism may undergo an entire change of feelings and aims. By what words, then, is such a revolution to be described? I believe the answer comes from comparing approved records of conversions, which differ greatly. In some, a man speaks with horror and loathing of his past years and his youthful companions. Another turns that loathing against his own evil nature and himself for yielding to it. He shows the deepest affection for all he has known, owns them more righteous than himself, believes God cares for them as for him, and is certain that what is true for him is true for them. The first talks much of his new start, his new heart, his purified affections. The second rejoices that, having found his own heart feeble, he has been led to see another in whom he ought to have trusted before and may trust now. The first speaks of the grace bestowed on himself; the second of being taken under the guidance of a Spirit he has resisted too long. These ways of speaking may be intermingled, there may be true feeling in those who mostly use the first, and there may be errors and confusions in records where the second predominates. Yet does not everyone recognize a characteristic and most practical difference between them? Would not any experienced Evangelical feel that the one language shows a healthier, more genuine character than the other? Then ought he not to ask whether both kinds of language are incompatible with Baptismal Regeneration, or only one, and if only one, whether the false or the true? Suppose “then I was awakened” does not imply “I had been asleep.” Suppose “then I came to the knowledge of the truth” does not imply that what I knew was true before I knew it. Suppose “I ceased to strive against the Spirit” does not imply earlier resistance to the Spirit. Then these are mere cant words, good for nothing and utterly detestable. But if they do imply all this, they imply just what the believer in Baptismal Regeneration is charged with fiction and falsehood for maintaining. They presume a state that is our state, whether we are conscious of it or in conformity with it or not.

So it is not necessary, to vindicate these two facts, to adopt the notion of two kingdoms, one earthly, formal, and fictitious, the other heavenly, spiritual, and real. Neither fact can be calmly examined, even in the reports of those who insist on them most, without suggesting that there is a heavenly, spiritual, real kingdom. All evil men, just so far as they are evil, are rebelling against it, and all converted men, just so far as they are converted, are brought into subjection to it. Therefore whatever evils have flowed and are flowing from the notion of two kingdoms are not justified or offset by a single practical advantage. How practical those evils are, let the history of Christian Europe since the Reformation attest. I have spoken of the difference between Luther and the Lutherans, and between Calvin and the Calvinists. I have spoken of how Justification by Faith has been turned from a living principle into an empty shibboleth, and how Divine Election has lost its force except as an excuse for doubting our own awful responsibilities. If we trace these miserable fruits to their root, I believe we shall find it in this notion. This at least is certain, as I have had occasion to remark again and again: Luther did not hold Baptismal Regeneration alongside Justification by Faith, as he might have held some doctrine belonging to the natural philosophy of his age. He grounded the one on the other. “Believe on the warrant of your Baptism. You are grafted into Christ; claim your position. You have the Spirit, you are children of God; do not live as if you belonged to the devil.” This was his invariable language, and with it he shook the Seven Hills.

What, I ask, have those done who have abandoned this language? While they talk of Luther, they would denounce as heretical and papistical anyone who used it. The children of Protestant families are told they have no right to call themselves children of God. They grow up in that conviction, and in maturer years they carry it to its logical consequences. They feel they have no right to use the Lord’s Prayer, or to pray at all. They feel there is no power near them to keep them from temptation, and no bond of fellowship with anyone except liking and taste. As their understandings ripen and their feelings decay, they come to regard Protestantism as a halfway house between Popery and Infidelity. Whether they go back to one or on to the other depends mainly on circumstances, and on whether fancy or intellect prevails in their constitution. I speak of the more courageous; in the majority, dull indifference, incapable of either resolve, becomes the ruling habit of mind. Thanks be to God, the exceptions in all Protestant countries are innumerable. But I believe they will almost universally be found where parents acted on the principle I am maintaining while disavowing it in words. They treated their children as if they possessed all Christian titles and privileges, in utter disregard of their own theory. I can say with some confidence that even in such cases the contradiction has not been harmless. A sense of perplexity and half-sincerity clings to the minds of those who most long to keep a clear heart and a free conscience. They do not dare to call themselves by a name which they feel they must claim if they are to serve God or do any right act. So their conduct becomes uncertain and their thoughts unmanly, and in place of humility they cultivate a false shame, in which they are conscious that pride is a large ingredient. There are hundreds of young men who will understand my meaning, and there are others, ministers of the Gospel, to whom I wish I could make it intelligible. At all events, those who feel as I do will have bitter cause for self-reproach if they do not protest, in season and out of season, against a notion which, unless I am greatly mistaken, is doing more than any other to undermine the Christianity of the Protestant nations.

4. The Philosopher.

IV. Last come our modern Philosophers. Their view of Baptism is usually put something like this. Baptism cannot be the sign of a universal society, because it shuts out Pagans and Mahometans, everyone but the members of one religious sect. It cannot properly be the sign of a spiritual society, because it makes no distinction between the stupidest and the most cultivated, the most brutal and the most humane. By its very words it implies acceptance of a doctrine that many Christians deny, few think about, and none understand. Judging by the words and ceremonies that everywhere go with it, it presumes belief in an evil spiritual agent, a belief that belongs only to the darkest ages. Baptism was certainly a bond of fellowship in certain periods, and it meant something to those who lived then. But its significance is gone and it has become a worthless symbol. It may be tolerated so long as it claims to be nothing, but the moment it tries to recover its obsolete importance, wise men will reject it altogether.

To the first objection, that Baptism is exclusive because it is only for those who profess faith in Christ, I reply as follows. Against theologians who regard Christ merely as the great teacher, the argument has great force. If they are right, to baptize men into Christ’s name is to receive them into the school of a man who appeared in Palestine 1800 years ago. We may prefer him to one who appeared in Arabia about 1200 years ago, but our taste, which more information may change entirely, is no true foundation for a human fellowship. But this is not the idea of Baptism expressed in any formulary recognised anywhere in Christendom. That idea assumes that Christ is the Lord of men and that men are created in him. This is the constitution of our race, and so every attempt of men to break themselves into separate units is contradictory and doomed to fail. Say, if you like, that this view is utterly false, that it does not explain how men are related or what their history means, that mankind has no spiritual constitution, or that it cannot be known, or that it is not this. But you cannot say that, if it were this, a society founded on it would be a party society and not a society for mankind. By our doctrine we must tell Jews, Pagans and Turks that there is a fellowship for them as well as for us, and that we have no spiritual privilege to which they have not an equally indefeasible right. We protest against the Jews because they deny this and maintain that there is no fellowship for mankind. We protest against the Pagans because, by giving us different objects of worship, they divide us by circumstances, customs and places. We protest against the Mahometans because, by affirming the greatest man to be merely a man, they destroy the communication between our race and its Maker. They suppose that communication to exist, if at all, only for certain sages and not for every human creature. They set absoluteness against relationship, when each is involved in and depends on the other. So they make it impossible for Islamite nations to have any feeling of a common humanity, or to be anything but slaves.

Next it is said that our baptismal fellowship is not spiritual, because it takes no account of spiritual differences among men: the dullest clod has the same place in it as the man who sees furthest into the meaning and life of things. Here again I must recall the objector to the baptismal principle. He may think we are using a mere form of words when we say that a man or child is actually adopted into union with a Being above himself, and that the Spirit of Life, Power and Wisdom is given to him. It may seem to him sheer absurdity. We do not think it so. And if it were a truth, the accusation would have no ground. We would then be bearing witness that the great distinction of all is between the man who has an eye for spiritual things and the man who sees only the ground at his feet. And we would be doing more: taking men into a position where they might, if they would, overcome their downward tendencies and reach the highest insight. Certainly we do not look for intellectual or moral aptitude and expose the children in whom we do not find it. We believe there is an eye in all men that can be opened if the evil will does not keep it shut, that all special faculties are subordinate to this, and that they will be best awakened when it is most exercised. Philosophers of this day cannot object to that doctrine on principle, however little they may habitually act on it.

The third complaint is that this universal sign is inseparably tied to belief in an incomprehensible dogma. When we examined the Unitarian creed, we noticed as its most striking feature the assertion that the unity of God is a great, primary, unchangeable truth on which all others rest, and whose acknowledgment must be the foundation of unity among men. This seemed to us of the highest importance, and we said that no theory or dogma, whatever its authority or arguments, could destroy its force. But we found that the so-called Unitarians had turned this awful, everlasting truth into a mere notion. It was a notion deduced from material considerations and so self-contradictory. It was purely negative, telling Polytheists “You ought not to worship many gods” without declaring the one God they ought to worship. It led not to the adoration of a Living Being but to a superstitious reverence for the number one, and so it could never be the symbol of a human fellowship. If this unity is at the root of all union among men, the foundation on which the pillars of the universe rest, then we should look not for a crude notional announcement of it but for a gradual discovery of it through the forms and relations of human society. I have urged that such a discovery is found in the Scriptures. The name revealed to the father of the faithful was its first step, “Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God is one Lord” the second, and the name into which we are baptized the final and perfect step. I do not say this is a progress from the obscure to the intelligible, or from the remote to the near. Like all science, it advances from what the Senses or Affections can apprehend to what is deeper and within reach only of the Understanding or Conscience. It then goes on to those amazing abysses which the Reason seeks, delights in, wonders at, and is lost in. Otherwise it would not be a revelation of God. As it is, we believe it is not the cold denial of everything men have dreamed in all ages, but the sweet reconciliation and harmony of all past thoughts, anticipations and revelations. No Pagan mythology could exist without acknowledging a Something behind all its conceptions of the gods, too awful to speak of, almost to think of. Each mythology also had its Heroes of divine and human race, whom men could admire and sympathise with. And even that was not enough without the dream of an Inspirer, Life-giver, not removed from men, not merely an object to behold and adore, but the source of their deeper thoughts and longings. I need not tell how dark and sensualized this faith became: the absolute Being seen as a dreary fate, the Heroes as products of earthly passions and full of earthly crimes, the Inspirer as the god of folly and drunkenness. Nor need I tell how these beliefs jarred even at their best, always seeking a unity and not finding it. Still, the feelings were really there. Poets and sages expressed them, but only because it was their gift to utter what was in the hearts of poor men, who were obscurely feeling or dimly acting it out. Now suppose the Name into which we Europeans have been baptized for so many centuries is, as we believe, what brings all these thoughts together, separates them from their hateful additions, raises them to Heaven, and yet ties them more directly to the daily business of this earth. Are we then guilty of fencing men off from our Communion by a strange dogma they can know nothing of, when we tell them that this Name is to go with them from cradle to grave? Are we guilty when we say that its grace, love and fellowship will be with them as a charm against every peril, a light in darkness, a comfort in sorrow, binding them to what was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be? Or when we say that every homely duty, every act of self-sacrifice, every deed of mercy will make the vision of it brighter, and every sin, proud thought and uncharitable feeling will cloud it? Or that the more they cleave to this name, the less they will dream of selfish rewards and the more they will long for the day when the sunlight of God’s countenance will gladden the whole creation? And that the communion of the Father and the Son in one Spirit, which has been the ground of all their thoughts and hopes, will be the consummation of them all for those who wake in the same likeness and are satisfied with it?

But, it is said, Baptism implies the acknowledgment of an evil spiritual agent, and this belief is at war with all civilization and philosophy. I no more shrink from this charge than from the others. Baptism has certainly been connected with this doctrine ever since it was practised, and I think it must be a meaningless ceremony to anyone who denies it.

I ground this opinion on what has just been said about the Baptismal Name. Baptism asserts that each man is taken into union with a Divine Person, and by that union freed from his evil Nature. This rests on another assertion: that there is a society for mankind constituted and held together in that Person, and that whoever enters it is freed from the World, the society bound together in acknowledging and obeying the evil, selfish tendencies of each man’s nature. It further affirms that this unity among men rests on a yet more awful and perfect unity, expressed in the Name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost. Lose sight of this last principle and the other two perish. It is impossible, and has always been felt impossible, to believe in a Truth, Unity and Love existing under certain forms without believing in an absolute Truth, Unity and Love from which those forms get their excellence and existence. But is it not equally impossible to Reason, and has not mankind’s Experience proved it impossible, to contemplate the antagonist forms of evil without rising to belief in an evil that has pervaded those forms and can exist apart from them? This conviction has been so necessary, not to a few but to all, that the imagination has continually pictured a horrid notion of an evil that is not only the source of all evil in the actual state of things but the source of those things themselves: a primary, original power, a rival Creator. This Manicheeism, though only at certain seasons hardened into a system, has haunted men’s minds from the beginning and haunts us still. Where does it come from, and what chiefly props it up? Surely this. We cannot deny the facts of misery and evil that force themselves on us, and we feel we must refer them to a cause. But we do not dare look steadily at the idea of an evil will in the light of a perfectly pure and holy will. So we imagine that powers used for an evil end are themselves evil, and that things turned to an accursed use are themselves accursed. Hence the doctrine of Oromasdes and Arimanes, and hence all the superstitious notions that have filled nature with malignant influences and objects of dread. How are such dark dreams to be dispelled? Not by setting facts aside, nor by outraging mankind’s innermost convictions. Nor by separating men from one another, as you most effectively do when you teach them that the evil each must fight in himself, the evil he sees in his fellows, and the evil he notices in the world’s history have no common derivation. So-called philosophers have committed these outrages on reason and conscience again and again, but what have they achieved? What dark fear have they removed from our path? What new bond of affection have they created in the human family? The mockery went out, was listened to, admired and adopted, and in the next age all the old superstitions returned. The imagination took its revenge for the denial of an evil Spirit by turning all the forms of Society and Nature into evil. Meanwhile Baptism has been testifying to high and low, to men of every country, language and custom, that they have a common friend and a common enemy. But it says the enemy has been vanquished and declared to have no right or property in any human creature or any corner of the universe. His power is conferred by our faithlessness, and while we claim our true position we may despise and defy him. It is only by making a lie that we come under the dominion of the father of lies. Yet it testifies just as strongly to the monstrous fact that men may be making lies, living in a position utterly anomalous and unreasonable. Nothing but entire dependence on the righteous and holy Being rescues anyone from it, so everyone has a devil to fight as well as a world and a flesh. Get rid of this contradiction, if you can, by the philosophy of denials. We shall go on, with God’s help, seeking deliverance from it by declaring to men their true state and adopting them into it.

How far the general charge that Baptism is a bygone symbol is made out can be judged from the particular arguments it rests on. Suppose nothing belongs to more than a particular age, nothing is permanent or real, and nothing connects the parts of mankind separated by space and time. Then the sign is obsolete, because there is nothing for it to signify. Suppose, on the other hand, that all men in all times have sought, and the men of this age above all profess to seek, some common human bonds that were not created to suit a particular period and place. Suppose they do not change when the notions and theories of that period prove futile or the customs of that place no longer apply. Then I ask where is the proof that this sign is not as fresh now as it was 1800 years ago? I willingly admit that in times of sensuality, luxury, religious exclusiveness and philosophical pretension, men grow impatient of it. What better argument do I need than that it is a true thing, a witness for what is spiritual, real, simple and universal? Thanks be to God that he has not left eternal truths that concern all men to the custody of the wise and prudent of the earth. He has embodied them in forms that from generation to generation have witnessed his love to the humble and the meek, and which all the contradictions of pride and self-will only help to illustrate and interpret.

The Romish System.

I have explained why I regard Baptism as the first sign that a Catholic Church, or Kingdom of Christ, exists in the world, and I have considered the objections to that view. Along the way I mentioned a class of people who are very earnest in proclaiming that such a Church exists, and equally earnest that Baptism is the only way into it. I have said that I differ widely from them, and that the Reformers of the sixteenth century defended the dignity of Baptism against them. So I am bound to examine the Romanist doctrine of Baptism and give my reasons for not adopting it.

The common phrase that the Romanist treats Baptism as an opus operatum (a work that works by being done) is easily misunderstood. An intelligent defender of the system would protest against some things it seems to imply. He would say that it is absurd to suppose we think a baptized person cannot sin or lose heaven. That would contradict everything our doctors have written and our priests practiced. Followers of the Reformation actually complain that we are too vigilant and self-suspicious, and our strongest conviction is that abandoning baptismal privileges is both very possible and terrible. Having said this, I think he would readily accept the phrase and explain it this way: “In baptism we receive the benefits of Christ’s redemption and become new and holy creatures. The work is finished; we have the highest blessing God can give. Our business now is to keep ourselves pure by every means the Church prescribes. We shall not keep that purity entirely; we shall commit frequent venial sins, which we may hope will be forgiven after confession and penance. But by constantly using the Church’s prayers and communion we can guard against mortal sins, which would rob us of God’s blessing altogether. If a mortal sin has been committed, or is feared, we may still hope for restoration, and that hope encourages us to seek it by the appointed methods with unabated diligence.”

The reader will see at once that this explanation matches mine at certain points. First, on the effect of Baptism: I have argued that it affirms a man to be in a certain state, and affirms the presence of a Spirit who is able and willing to uphold him in it and bring his life into line with it. Second, on sin: I have argued that it consists in voluntarily refusing the blessings of God’s covenant. Third, on the means of being kept in the right way: like the Romanist, I would say it is by abiding in the ordinances through which we keep communion with our brethren and with God. Where, then, does the difference begin? At the threshold of these very statements. A man is brought into a certain state, but what state? I say, and the Romanist would not deny in words, a state of union with Christ. But this state rules out the idea that goodness, purity, or holiness belongs to any creature considered in itself. To be something in himself is man’s ambition and man’s sin, and Baptism is the renunciation of that pretence. So a man does not, by Baptism, faith, or any other process, acquire a new nature, if “nature” means, as it usually does, inherent qualities and properties. Nor does he become a new creature, unless that means he is created anew in Christ Jesus, grafted into Him, and made heir of Christ’s life and not his own. That, so grafted, he receives Christ’s Spirit I of course believe. But the Spirit’s work in him is to draw him continually out of himself, to teach him to disclaim all independent virtue, and to bring him into the knowledge and image of the Father and the Son. On these grounds I have argued, against modern Protestants, that the sin of a baptized man is acting as if he were not united with Christ: setting up his own nature and will and obeying them. His sin is doing self-contradictory acts, claiming to be what he is not and never can be, and denying what he is and always must be. What follows? That faith in this union is a duty, the greatest of all duties, and that it can never cease to be a duty. A man has no right to believe a lie. Sin leads him to believe one, and brings him into a state of mind where a lie seems truth. It may bring him to where lying becomes the element of his being and truth is wholly closed to his eyes. I cannot doubt that this kind of mortal sin is possible, whether I reflect on the tendencies to atheism in every one of us or read the Epistle to the Hebrews. But if that condition had actually come upon a man, it would not alter the fact; it would confirm it. Is the writer of Hebrews any less earnest than other New Testament writers in urging faith, or in inviting men into God’s rest? Does he separate those exhortations from his warnings about the danger of apostasy? Or does he not make that danger one of his main arguments for why each of his readers should claim his privileges as a citizen of the New Jerusalem?

For the same reason, I tried to show that the Evangelical or modern Protestant notion makes repentance impossible. If we may not call ourselves children of God, how can we be told to arise and go to our Father? And if we may not do that, what does our repentance mean? It can only be a selfish, sinful struggle to regain the corn, wine and fatness of the earth we think we have lost, and not a humble confession that we have made light of our birthright and are no longer worthy to be called sons. The world’s repentance may come from the desire to be assured that we belong to God’s redeemed family. The repentance that leads to life must be a confession of the unbelief, ingratitude and hardness of heart that made us slight a love freely given, which has never stopped watching over us and struggling with us.

The doctrine of the opus operatum leads, I think, by a longer but surer road to the same practical results that make our Protestant systems so dangerous. When it is said that a baptized man loses his baptismal state, it is necessarily implied that this state was one of independent holiness and purity. As I have urged again and again, we do not stop being children because we are disobedient children. If Baptism were understood as adoption into the state of children, and its virtue lay in that, the notion of losing it would be impossible. But it is supposed that the man acquires something for himself at the moment of Baptism: that he is endowed with heavenly virtues and is in himself, separately considered, a new creature. The Romanist thinks this exalts Baptism. To me it degrades it and robs it of meaning. He turns a sacrament into an event. He makes Christ’s redemption exhausted by a single gift, while the Bible presents it as bringing men into an eternal and indissoluble friendship. He thinks he is encouraging a safer, holier, more watchful spirit. But as far as this opinion governs our lives, it undermines holiness, watchfulness and safety, because it turns the whole of life into a struggle to recover a lost good. Pursued honestly, that struggle has no holiness in it, for it is purely selfish and cannot be prompted by love. In most men, though, a cruel sense of contradiction arises. They are commanded to repent, yet they feel they cannot, since conscience tells them that grief over the consequences of sin, present or expected, is not repentance. Hence come a craving for indulgences, a habit of unbelief, and despair of holiness. Which of these states is safe for a human being? The Romanist still thinks he at least honors the Church by this notion. To me he is destroying the very idea of the Church and denying its necessity. For he makes the blessing of Baptism not that it receives men into the holy Communion of Saints, but that it gives them individual blessings and an individual holiness. How then are self-renunciation and fellowship as members of one body possible? And if they are not, what is the Church?

I hope it will be admitted that I have not charged Romanists with any mere excess or exaggeration of their creed on this subject. This doctrine is an integral part of a system. But do I mean that it is found only in Romanist writers, or that the doctrine I defend is not found in them? Anyone who claimed that would be easily refuted. Extracts from the Fathers could show that the doctrine of an opus operatum mingled in their minds with that of being grafted into Christ. And extracts from Anselm, Hugo de St. Victor, Bernard, Aquinas, and even eminent Romanists today could show that the very idea I have tried to express was unfolded by them, with far more eloquence and unction. I believe all this fully. Far from discouraging the theological student from noticing it, I would press it on his attention. I would try to convince him that whenever a great spiritual principle has been strongly revealed to men, a material counterfeit of it has appeared as well. The two have lived together in the minds of the best and wisest men. If we look for the true one, we must turn to their devotional exercises, the times when they were cultivating fellowship with God and forgetting themselves, which are the parts of their writings their disciples often study least. The counterfeit we shall find in elaborate controversial treatises, the ones that supply the best material for theorems, ready formulas, and convenient weapons for argument and ridicule against opponents. The first remain for the delight and consolation of humble Christians in every age; the second gradually harden into a definite system. They are supposed to be bone of each other’s bone and flesh of each other’s flesh, until some great crisis comes in which God shows the difference of the causes by the difference of the effects, proving that one came from Him and the other from the devil. So let the reader not be dismayed if the highest authorities are cited for the opus operatum, or surprised to find it in any age or part of the Church (especially one that greatly undervalued sacraments), reappearing and claiming to be the Scriptural and Catholic idea. Let him not be frightened when told that, in distinguishing between the two, he is setting his own judgment against doctors and antiquity. Let him say boldly: I am doing no such thing. I am only determining not to believe the doctors against themselves. I will not be cheated of a transcendent truth they taught me, one evidently dear to their inmost hearts, which they felt came from Christ’s own teaching and bound them to the Apostles and Martyrs of all times, and which they admitted was contrary to all their carnal apprehensions and preserved in them only by God’s Spirit. They gave a plausible explanation of that truth elsewhere, while arguing with adversaries, while trying to make a principle tell on the hopes or fears of men who could not grasp its meaning, while drawing conclusions from Scripture by private judgment, and while defending some fungus that the maxims of their age had confused with the tree it grew on. That explanation is forgotten in every moment of higher inspiration, and the more it is tested by its fruits, the less divine it proves. It is easy to accuse of pride those who have the courage to act on this determination. I believe it is the proud, system-seeking, system-loving intellect within us that inclines us to embrace the doctrine of the opus operatum, while the humble and contrite heart craves a deeper principle and, finding it, must part with the other for its sake.
