Part I. On the Principles of the Quakers and of the Other Religious Bodies Which Have Arisen Since the Reformation, and on the Systems to Which They Have Given Birth

Chapter II. Pure Protestantism

Luther’s justification by faith, Calvin’s election, and Zwingli’s supreme Scripture are traced as facets of one Protestant principle, yet Lutheran, Calvinist, and Puritan churches repeatedly harden into dead formulas or bare feeling.

Section I.—The Leading Principles of the Reformation.

Justification by Faith—Election—The Written Word—Authority of National Sovereigns.

1. Martin Luther’s inward struggles were at least as terrible as George Fox’s, and they have left far more remarkable witnesses in the history of Europe. The conflicts in the mind of the Drayton shoemaker fixed the character of Quakerism, and in the same way those that tormented the Monk of Wittenberg explain the character of the Reformation.

The two men were alike in some respects. Anyone who reads Luther’s first document against the sale of indulgences carefully will see how deeply he felt that he was a twofold being. Something in him had to be crushed and destroyed, and something in him was meant to enjoy life, peace and freedom. A man who had not experienced much of what Fox describes could hardly have reached that conviction, or put it in such language. Yet the history of their minds was altogether different. The contrast is as striking as we can expect between two men who were equally sincere and brave.

Luther knew as much as any Quaker could have told him about a light speaking to the conscience, warning him of the evil he had done and of the temptations within and without that drew him back to it. But for him that light brought tumult instead of peace. It spoke of a Being of absolute power, wisdom and righteousness with whom he had no sympathy. It told him to seek reconciliation with that Being at any cost, and to count every trial light if it promised such a blessing. But it also told him of a strict, irreversible law that allowed no departure and no dispensation, so that every effort to heal the breach became a new witness that the breach was permanent. Then came the dream of deliverance from the curse of this law, in words he had heard from infancy but had never been able to attach any meaning to. He had been told of a Mediator between Creator and creatures, who had offered a sacrifice for men. He had been told that men are united with Him, grafted into Him, and have in Him a righteousness they do not have in themselves. The doctors and schoolmen he studied had said such things again and again. But they mixed them up with strange perplexities about cases of conscience, the kinds and degrees of repentance, mortal and venial sins, and the nature and mode of justification. If scholastic obstructions stood in the way of escape from a state he knew to be evil, the practical obstructions seemed even more monstrous, for they cut off all intercourse between the soul and its Deliverer. There were indulgences that soothed the conscience, and penances whose promises were never fulfilled and which tormented the spirit more than the flesh. There were all the notions of intervening mediators begging for the removal of a curse that had already been borne by Him who alone could bear it and alone could fully sympathise with those for whom He suffered. These were so many bandages and fetters on the soul, leaving it content with the sin it loved or hopeless of deliverance from the sin it loathed. The Bible freed Luther’s mind from the perplexities of scholastic logic, and the creeds and sacraments of the Church enabled him to disentangle himself from the web of papal inventions. From beginning to end the written word of God seemed to him to witness that a man is justified by faith. It used no school phrases for this, but every act of trust in a Divine Person who had revealed Himself as the object of trust was an example of it. So he could see what St. Paul meant when he said Abraham was justified by faith: Abraham trusted God’s promise and word, and that made him godly and righteous. In the same way the Psalms were all acts of faith, in which a man crushed by evils inward and outward rose up, claimed the relation to God that God’s covenant had given him, and shook off the sins he had fallen into by forgetting it. Properly speaking, these were acts of trust in a Mediator, recognitions of one to whom the suppliant was himself related, who was a bond between him and the absolute God, and in whom alone he dared to call on Him. So all these justifications were foretastes of the justification the Son of God made for all who would trust Him, when He offered up His body as a sacrifice and rose from the dead. Luther believed that the great end of St. Paul’s life was to announce this work as accomplished, and to tell men that they become righteous by believing it and so entering into union with their Lord. He believed it was his own appointed office too. In every age the preacher’s business is to tell men this truth simply, in the direct personal language of the Bible and not the formal, dogmatic language of the schools. And the man was not the only witness. The Creed preached it and the Sacraments preached it, and the truly instructed doctor would find the deepest wisdom in them and labour to make them carry home in practice what he could only say in words. This, it seems to me, is Lutheranism according to Luther. In it lies the germ of all the doctrines that peculiarly belong to the Reformation, though other minds than his might have to develop them distinctly.

2. The chief of these doctrines is the one Luther proclaimed so vehemently in his controversy with Erasmus. Yet it was evidently not as habitually present to his mind as it was to the Genevan Reformer, John Calvin. In Luther’s heart and reason, the idea of an object a man might look to and rest in came before all others. Unless some dogma like that of Erasmus drove him to it, by seeming to threaten a revival of the papal contrivances for reconciling man to God, he troubled himself little about the origin of the feelings and acts by which a man lays hold of the One who offers Himself to his faith and hope. But it is clear, from this treatise1 and from the very character of his doctrine, that the question must arise and must receive some such answer as he and Calvin gave. The idea of an absolute will, to which man must be reconciled by a Mediator, lay at the base of all Luther’s thoughts. Anyone meditating steadily on those thoughts must ask: who devised this whole scheme of reconciliation and redemption? Who leads men to use it? Who determines the working of their minds and where it leads? The schools had always dealt with such questions, and Augustine, who seemed to settle them as Calvin did, had always been counted one of their highest oracles. The difference was the same as before. The principle that man must look up to God as the direct source of his acts, thoughts and purposes was presented to men’s faith in the real language of Scripture, not to their understandings in the abstract language of the schools. Those who grasped their relation to Christ were to speak of themselves as God’s elect people, as Samuel, David or the Israelites did, and to believe they would have been miserable and accursed if God had not elected them. They were not to trouble themselves with questions about the will, or look for any reason for their blessedness except that it was God’s good pleasure to give it. This belief was also to be the decisive barrier against the impostures of Romish priests, who tried to persuade men they must win by their own efforts a position that ought to be received as God’s gift. This, I think, is the Calvinistic side of Protestantism. Some may think I have given it, like justification, too little of a scholastic character, and spoken of it too much as opposed to the logical systems of the previous age, when Calvin, Melanchthon and some of the German Reformers were notable for their devotion to logic. I still believe I am right. I may explain presently how the scholastic tendencies of the Reformation later developed. Here I only remark three things. Reformers trained by the schoolmen would naturally keep many of their characteristics. Men with a strong bent for dialectics are often the ones who most feel the want of what is practical and popular, and look for a practical and popular language. And those who have most often commented, in praise or blame, on the scholastic qualities of the Reformers’ controversial writings have always also held that the Reformation itself was an appeal to the feelings and sympathies of common men.

3. If Luther’s justification by faith is the first characteristic of Reformation theology, and Calvin’s formally asserted election the second, most people will agree, I think, that a certain peculiar estimate of the Scriptures is the third. But there are one or two questions here. No one who knows the Reformers’ writings would say they were more scrupulous about their Canon of Scripture than the doctors before them. Luther’s language about the Epistle of St. James and the book of Revelation would be conclusive against that, even if it was retracted later and even if nothing similar appeared in his contemporaries. Nor can it be said that Luther or Calvin thought anyone could pick up the Bible without previous initiation and naturally draw light from it. They saw it rather as a divine witness to men who were already consciously struggling with their evil nature, telling them about that struggle and the means of deliverance. This, I think, was the view most present to Luther. The Bible was the preacher’s book, from which he was to tell men how people of the same flesh and blood had fought the world, the flesh and the devil before them, and what strength and help God had given them in it. At the same time it was a fixed and permanent authority, above the notions and experiences of particular minds, which let them find solid grounds of peace and comfort even against those notions and experiences. So Luther’s veneration for the Bible, high as it stood, was subordinate to his zeal for the doctrine of Justification. He looked on it mainly as the witness for that doctrine, and for that reason he loved it with all his heart and would have given his life that men might hear it in their own language. The same was true, though less so, of Calvin: the Bible witnessed to him of divine Election, and mainly for that reason it was precious to him and no labour in studying and expounding it was wasted. But if Scripture was valuable as the announcement of one or other of these great principles, was it not in itself a great principle that there is such a book as a Bible, a book speaking directly to men’s consciences, a fixed and permanent utterance of the divine will? To some (I should think to Zuinglius) this seemed the cardinal idea of the Reformation, to which the others were subordinate. At any rate, a body of men gradually began to be separated by important peculiarities from the other Reformers, and for this body faith in the Scriptures, with less distinct reference to the principles taught in them, seems to have been the most striking positive characteristic.

4. These three principles seem to me positive principles in the strictest sense. They are no less so for having been brought forth against certain popular notions and current practices. That is rather the very test of their positive character. When Luther appeared, many abominations were prevalent that Romanists abhorred then as they do now. Some were corrected or softened at the Council of Trent, and some disappeared when the infidel temper of Leo and his court gave way to the more earnest spirit of later popes. Great as the disgust was, and clear as it was that it had reached the people of the countries of Christendom, among whom a class had arisen that wanted a position independent of both hierarchy and aristocracy, it was still a very solemn question for wiser and better men how far it was possible to remedy, or safe to denounce, even the most crying abuses. The building is tottering: ought we to touch it under pretence of repairing it? Sir Thomas More and other men as good as he may have asked themselves this, and may have been unable to find a theoretical answer, though they answered it in practice by sitting still. Were they wrong? I would not dare say so. If they saw these corruptions merely as the excess of something good, they were clearly right. Even if they felt, as I have no doubt they did, that the loss of faith was in some important sense the cause of these superstitions, and still more of the contrivances to make them profitable, they could not see that any great truth was hidden or contradicted by these parts of the popular system. In that case they would have run the great hazard, or rather committed the sin, of taking away something that held the affections of men without giving them any substitute. This argument must have been strengthened in the minds of those who had the wisdom and opportunity to observe the change taking place in the mind of Europe and the kind of cravings that threatened the Church. The feeling “I do not merely belong to a great Christendom, I have a distinct individual position” had clearly developed in the members of the new class. It made them eager for novelties, ready to follow particular guides, and impatient of systematic authority. In some countries wise observers might see that this feeling was linked to another they could count more wholesome and worth encouraging. The German or English tradesman, along with his Hussite or Lollard notions, had a sense of belonging to a particular soil and speaking a particular language, often far weaker in the nobleman. But this conviction interfered just as much with submission to Church authority and affection for Church ordinances. It produced strange questionings about the dominion of the Roman Bishop, stories of how kings and emperors had resisted him in former days, and a dislike of the universal language. Was it not clear, then, that the age had a violent inclination to infidelity and irreverence? Did not every admission of an error sanctioned or tolerated by Churchmen make that inclination irresistible? And was not the only duty of men who wished well to society, and to truth, to uphold the entire system as best they could?

It seems to me that God’s Providence led the Reformers to the only possible escape from this fearful dilemma. They were led to see that certain great moral principles, involved in the very idea of a relation between God and His creatures (say rather, the belief in that relation itself), were outraged by the existing Roman system. Its abuses were not excesses but essentially evil, rooted in a great denial and unbelief. They set at naught great facts about man and God, facts announced by an express revelation from heaven. Here was a standing-point. I maintain, and would earnestly press, that Protestantism has a standing-point of its own; that it is not merely condemnatory or negative; and that, so far as it keeps within its own appointed province, it denounces only what is itself negative and sets at naught something needful for the life and being of man.

What that something is, and so what the appointed province of Protestantism is, I have already indicated. The feeling most strongly awake when Luther appeared was each man’s feeling that he was an individual, not merely one of a mass. Luther did not create this thought; it was there. He struggled with it in himself and would gladly have overcome it, but it was too strong. He had to find some interpretation of it, and he was not at peace until he found one that told him the only safe, free, true position for a man is not rebellion but allegiance, the subjection of the whole soul to a righteous and divine government. The clue that led him out of his own perplexities was what thousands besides him needed, and they received it and rejoiced. It is easy to say that he was a minister of sedition, or raised up such ministers, because it is easy to misrepresent history and blame those who were God’s instruments in preventing the evil consequences of certain states of mind from becoming universal. But those who look at the facts steadily and impartially, not wishing (and I think I have shown I do not wish) to represent them to the disadvantage of Protestantism’s opponents, will, I believe, be more and more convinced that the Reformers did not call forth the rebellious activity of their period. When it was seeking refuge in infidelity, they taught it to find refuge in faith.

The three principles I have described gave a religious satisfaction to the sense of an individual position that men of the sixteenth century were feeling. I have hinted that closely connected with it was another sense, that of a distinct national position. The fourth principle of Protestantism was the recognition of this feeling too as true and as having a religious basis. The protest against the usurpation of the Bishop of Rome was not mainly grounded on the idea that it interfered with Christ’s prerogatives over the whole Church. I do not say the Reformers never put this idea forward, or that it never dawned on them as the principle the Papal usurpation invaded. But I do not think it was constantly present to their minds, or ever fully developed in them, or that when they used language implying it, the language meant to them precisely what it means to us.

They may also have spoken with displeasure, even reprobation, of one bishop assuming authority over others, but I cannot persuade myself that this was a sin that would have led them to reject the Papal authority. Their contempt for it grew as they became more convinced that what an infallible wisdom had pronounced erroneous and mischievous was true and necessary, and as they saw how it had interfered with the power and functions of National Sovereigns. The peculiar theology of the Reformation was linked in many ways with asserting the dignity of this office and the national distinctness it represents, and one link is very obvious. The Reformers had turned to the Scriptures not merely for their authority but for their practical character. That practical character shows especially in the Old Testament, where every truth is brought out in relation to the events of a national history. Their own time interpreted the Scriptures to the Reformers, and the Scriptures in turn interpreted their own time.

Section II.—Objections to the Principles of the Reformation Considered.

I have already noted one primary objection to all these doctrines, which I think would be fatal to them: that they are merely negative, the mere contradiction of what Romanists believe. But there are also particular objections to each of them, and these must be examined.

1. One charge against the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith belongs especially to the Quakers. They say that justification by faith either means the same as the doctrine of the Indwelling Word or, if it means something different, one of the two is false. Either the light dwelt with men or it did not. If it did, following that light is justification. If it did not, Fox’s whole doctrine, which I have defended, cannot stand. A man must be justified either by the agreement of his mind with an inward principle or by outward acts done on his behalf. It is impossible, they say, to hold both that a man is in his right state when he submits to the Indwelling Word and that he is justified by acts Christ performed as his representative. I readily admit that the temper which leads a man to assert one of these doctrines vehemently is not the temper which leads him to assert the other, and that the two are seldom found together in great strength. The Quaker and the Mystic (I use the word here neutrally, not in the evil sense I gave it in my first chapter) habitually contemplate a divine presence in the heart. They may link that presence with the life of our Lord, but if they do, they give His acts and words an import different from their plain historical one. The Lutheran habitually contemplates a Divine Person with a real, distinct life. He rejoices that this Person entered into ordinary human relations and circumstances, and he realises his own connection with Him through them. Anyone who has watched himself, and knows how different were the feelings that drew him in these two directions at different times, will admit that Quakerism and Lutheranism are curiously antipathetic. But I think the same observation will lead him to hold that each doctrine is the complement of the other, and that, despite their apparent opposition, neither can exist with real strength if the other is denied.

To explain, consider the actual wants and anxieties of men in the old world who went through the struggle between light and darkness that Fox speaks of. Must not thoughts like these have been constantly in their minds? “Two powers struggle within me, one good and one evil. Sometimes the darkness seems about to scatter, sometimes the light seems almost quenched. But who am I in the midst of this struggle? Do I belong to the light or the darkness? Whose child have I a right to call myself now, and whose shall I be for ever? My consciousness of evil and rebellion testifies against me. My belief in the graciousness and might of the Being on my side speaks in my favour. But then outward facts seem to confirm the worse conclusion: all the sicknesses, sorrows and troubles of the world seem to condemn me, or to prove that my unseen Friend is not omnipotent, or that His forbearance with my repeated disobedience will have a limit. And what is that limit? May not Death decide the struggle against me, as God’s permitted minister?” These thoughts do not imply any unbelief in a future state. The heathen’s anxious question, as all their mythology shows, was not whether there is one, but what he would be in it. Some ethereal particle in him might rise and enter into rest, even be united with the Divine Essence, but would it be himself? He could not believe he would die as the things around him die. Whenever he feels that he is at all, he feels he is immortal; he may lose the thought while speculating, but never while acting and living. The point is this: will good or evil, light or darkness, be that to which I am united, when all the spiritual energies by which I seemed to assert a connection with something better than myself are crushed by pain, weakness and death, as the energies by which I eat and walk are crushed? The Jews felt precisely the same difficulty, with greater power and reality, but with a brighter hope of solving it. They felt the struggle in themselves. But taking hold of the covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, they could believe that the righteous Lord who revealed Himself to their hearts was their Lord for ever. Coming with the appointed sacrifice, at the appointed time and place, to the appointed priest, they could believe that the covenant had not been destroyed by their iniquity, that they still had an inheritance in the King of their nation, would see His face in righteousness, and would share His glory when it was manifested to the whole earth. Yet it was still only a hope, and the doubt at times gained a dreadful ascendency: “Is this evil and accursed nature my own self? Are its evils not imputed to me and counted a part of me? Will Death not destroy that nature, and when he does, shall I be spared?” These questions occupied men not because they lacked the light that lights every man coming into the world, but because they had it, and in proportion as it was revealed to them or they followed it. Surely some answer was needed. It is a mockery to say the light itself was the answer. If we accept Luther’s doctrine, the answer is clear. The Word was made flesh and dwelt among us. In this flesh He passed through the conflicts and trials of men, died a real death, and brought back a real body from the grave. This was the voice from heaven answering the voice from earth. The man asks, “What am I—a child of light or of darkness?” Christ, dying and rising, declares: “Your nature is accursed, your person is justified. Married to your evil nature, you are sinful and under the curse. Claiming your portion in me, you are holy, righteous and redeemed.” Is this merely the doctrine of an Indwelling Word? Does it contradict that doctrine? Or does one prove the necessity and reality of the other?

It is true, as I said, that one side of the Lutheran doctrine has nothing corresponding to it in mysticism. Many an earnest mystic has recognised in his later years that our Lord’s outward acts in human flesh are needful parts of a spiritual economy, as assertions of the right of creatures who bear that flesh to rise above themselves and claim a portion in Him. But Luther’s root conviction, that these acts were mediatorial and propitiatory, aimed ultimately at satisfying the will of the Father, has generally been received by mystics with coldness if not disgust. They should understand from the start, since they must discover it sooner or later, that they cannot connect this faith with any of mysticism’s ideas or reduce it under them. If those ideas include all truth, the Lutheran doctrine is not true. Its very assumption, to which everything is referred, is an Absolute Will that is the ground of all being, life and thought. That forms no part of mysticism, however mystics may have grafted it into their faith. The Divine Word is the only real subject of their meditation. They may think awfully of a vague gulf of being beyond, but they dare not speak of it in human language, bring it within personality, or dream of it as the ground of human relations. I shall presently have to admit that the mystics have reasonable complaints against the systems that Lutheran theology produced, on exactly this ground: that they have stripped the idea of God of its fearfulness and grandeur and reduced it to human notions. But the question here is whether evidence like that which compelled us to acknowledge the mystical idea of an Indwelling Word also compels us to acknowledge a truth beside and beyond it, involving in some terms the belief in Mediation, Sacrifice and Satisfaction. Suppose we valued the discovery that the doctrine of the Indwelling Word is not merely asserted in a few texts but gives coherence to the whole course of Scripture history: it gives a sense to the word Idolatry, shows why that was treated as the sin of mankind, and explains the lives and language of those who kept free of it. May we not observe a parallel line of proofs for these other principles? Does the Lutheran depend on certain texts to show that human Mediation is in Scripture? Is it not worked into the very tissue of the history, and into the constitution of the Jewish commonwealth? Can less be said of Propitiation, Atonement and Sacrifice? May it not be more correctly said that what gives continuity to books written in such different circumstances and at such wide intervals is this: lawgivers, psalmists and prophets, each in their function, from inward promptings and for the needs of their times, were gradually drawing out ideas already embodied in the institutions and life of the Jewish nation?

Again, it seemed a remarkable witness for Fox’s principle that a great part of the Gentile records is scarcely intelligible without it. Have we no witness for these principles in another part of the same records? The philosopher found a divine light or wisdom to cry after and follow. But did not the whole people believe in an invisible power to be propitiated, reached by mediation, and offered sacrifices? Even when all actual faith had gone, did not the wisest statesmen still regard these thoughts as strange and mysterious, yet something the nation must acknowledge if it was to be a nation, though they might dispense with them? Was philosophy ever able to rise above these ideas or merge them in its own? Many philosophers laboured hard, and the best of them felt most strongly that the popular faith on these matters contradicted truths he held most sacred. Yet he was the least disposed to attack that faith and the most inclined to see it as something the philosopher needed all the more because he was a philosopher.

In this view he was, as I have hinted before, almost alone. Nearly all others wanted to extinguish the existing theology by philosophical notions, to translate it into them, or to give philosophy the mysterious, miraculous character of revealed theology. The records of each experiment are preserved, and the more they are studied the better. The fact has outlasted them all: these ideas, in one form or another, have been and are the most characteristic and fundamental ideas of humanity, the very proofs that we constitute a Kind. Explain them as you will, but remember that an explanation is not the thing. If these ideas are not delusions, there is a reality corresponding to them, and that reality, could we know it, might be expected to explain both them and the partial, false and mischievous notions that have surrounded them. If they are delusions, it seems all humanity must be a delusion, with no common principles for a groundwork. So I cannot think the mystical objection to the Lutheran doctrine tenable, on whichever side we view it.

2. Another class of persons oppose the doctrine of justification as Luther stated it, saying that it exaggerates a mere crisis in the history of individuals into a fixed, permanent law. “At a certain time,” they say, “a man who has been careless of religion becomes convinced of his error. He sees he has lived a faithless, godless life, as if there were no Lord and Saviour to trust and love. He begins to recognise such a Saviour and to believe in Him. It is a new feeling, the beginning of a different class of feelings, and he rightly invests it with great sacredness. But it is only the first of a series of spiritual acts. His belief, unless stunted by the notion that it is all-sufficient, grows into love and good works. And it might have done so without any sudden discovery that he calls Justification. From baptism onward he might have led a faithful, pure life, and then his baptism would be as rightly called his justification as that first conscious act of faith. Luther was led by his own circumstances or his age’s to dwell with delight on the transition-moment of his spiritual history. Yet even he often speaks of baptism as if it deserved the credit of his justification. So there was a confusion in his mind, which might not unfit him to be an active reformer but must make us suspicious when he claims to announce a great principle. Everything in his words and the history of his doctrine heightens the suspicion. Why did he dwell so much on a formal release from guilt and a formal imputation of righteousness? What man needs is a real deliverance from sin and a real righteousness. Give the other notion its best form and strip it of the fictitious character that must offend both God and man, and it still only points to a position offered to us, which we may realise if we please. To speak of that position as independent of our realising it, while saying it is granted to faith and faith is the realising principle, is to give us shadows for substances, a dream of food to satisfy hunger.”

I hope I have stated the argument fairly, avoiding only one or two favourite phrases, such as “forensic,” which have become catchwords and embarrass everyone on either side who uses them. I acknowledge its great plausibility, the admirable piety of those who have brought it forward, and the difficulty of showing why I think its substance fallacious without seeming to reject parts of it I believe true and important. But by going at once to the heart of the question we may relieve it of many perplexities. Everyone is struck by St. Paul’s words: “That I may be found in him, not having my own righteousness, which is of the law, but that which is by the faith of Christ; the righteousness, that is, of God by or upon faith.” What is remarkable is their connection. Paul speaks of some very high attainment, the consummation of all his strivings. And what is it? Not having an individual righteousness, but precisely the not having it. The highest perfection this saint and apostle could conceive, and which he dared not say he had reached, was ceasing to be anything in himself, acknowledging his whole moral and spiritual life as in another.

I do not believe the excellent men who argue against the Lutheran doctrine on these grounds have any difficulty with this passage, or wish to explain away its force. I am persuaded they would say: “This is one of the paradoxes of divinity, but every new fact in our spiritual history throws light on it. It is so involved in Christianity and in our own moral constitution that few people fail to confirm it by unconscious expressions, even if they see little of its meaning. Everyone knows that just so far as he contemplates self in his acts as an object of congratulation, those acts contradict themselves, and just so far as he can renounce self and see his acts as beginning and ending in another, he has fought the great fight of life and reached his true state.” Writers who strongly dislike Luther commonly use such language. Let us look at it steadily for a moment.

It is admitted that this self-denial, in its highest and fullest sense, is the true state of a man: no fantastic, imaginary state, but something every good man should desire and strive after, sorrowing that his vanity and pride keep checking him. So we should not assume there is something necessarily fictitious in the idea of abandoning one’s own righteousness and acquiring another. In one sense that idea is the reverse of fictitious: it is deliverance from the perpetual fictions and counterfeits of our selfish nature. Nor can it be maintained that renouncing our righteousness and receiving another belongs only to the lowest stage of a man’s progress; in some sense it belongs to the very highest. But, it is said, this cannot be the Lutheran sense, which is, as clearly as language can put it, that a sinner pursuing an evil course becomes by an act of faith in another’s righteousness a righteous, justified man. No one who does not wish to confuse essentially distinct, even opposite, things can identify that assertion with one that relates to the most advanced period of Christian life. Now the special charge against Luther is that he mistook the phenomena of a crisis for a law applying to the whole of life, and that he treated this doctrine as the key to a man’s entire spiritual existence when it merely describes its first conscious feeling. May we not suggest that the objector may himself be falling into this error, and mistaking Luther just because he was free of it? May there not be a law which is expressly the law of a man’s being, conformity to which is his perfection, which from his first hour to his last is his law, which does not depend for its reality on his recognising or denying it, which will judge him at the last day, and which must therefore never be concealed from him but announced to him as that against which he is rebelling? If Paul’s words are taken simply, must this law not be the law of union with another, the law of self-renunciation? Suppose I see a man on an utterly wrong course, a course of indulgence in the commonest sense. Have I not a right to tell him it is an evil course, and, if he asks why, to answer that it is a course of selfishness and that he was not meant, as his conscience tells him, to be selfish? And if he replies, as nine times out of ten he will, “But how can I be otherwise? Everyone is selfish; it is our nature and necessity; God made us so,” am I forbidden to assert God’s ways against man’s blasphemy in words like these? “I care not whether you call selfishness your nature. If it is, your nature is a contradiction and a lie, for it makes you do what you cannot do without being at war with yourself. Then you are not meant to live according to your nature but to rise out of it, above it. There is One who has come to redeem you out of your nature and unite you to Himself. In Him you may believe and live; in yourself you cannot.” In saying this I have preached the Lutheran doctrine of justification to this sinful man. I have told him that there is a state belonging to him with which he is living at variance. I have said that union with another is his law and separation his transgression, and that this union is spiritual, not natural, maintained by faith, so that unbelief renounces it and is an anomalous, sinful condition. There is a fiction here, certainly: it is a fiction to have a state and not enjoy it, to possess the conditions of a spiritual being and act as if they did not exist. But it is the fiction of an evil world, and I do not know how to get rid of it except by declaring the fact it opposes.

It was certainly not Luther’s dream that because this is announced as a fact it will be at once received, that there will be no fierce conflict with the selfish nature before it is acknowledged, or that the struggles will not recur every day of a man’s pilgrimage. Every page of his writings, like every hour of his life, witnesses to a tremendous struggle. The question he thought all-important was this: is the struggle against the evil heart’s too great proneness to believe and trust, or against its reluctance to do so; against its overeagerness to cleave to its Lord, or against its passion for selfish independence?

His conviction was that when he distrusted Christ he was a bad man, incapable of any right act. So trust must be a duty; a man who did not trust could not be righteous, and so far as he trusted he must be. Asked whether nothing must precede this trust and warrant it, he would have answered that God’s word and promise must precede: the declaration that this state is yours is your warrant for claiming it. The words of the Bible generally, and the assurance of baptism to you in particular, give you the right to believe. To seek the right in anything else, in outward acts or inward feelings of yours, is a contradiction, for such acts and feelings, if lawful, imply trust and express it. A man’s repeating his Credo does not give him a right to trust God’s mercy, but if he repeats it as he should, it is a form of trust in God. A man’s comfortable feelings are not reasons for confidence; unless they are mere physical sensations, they are effects of resting in his true Friend. So, for Luther, faith could not be treated as a grace we contemplate in ourselves. By its nature it is the act of going out of self, of entering into union with another from whom all our graces come. He firmly asserted that the power to do this is given by God and so is a grace. But it makes an immeasurable difference whether grace is given as so much stock a man may call his own, or leads him to disclaim all property in himself and live wholly in Christ. That is why he resisted the Romanist argument from the relative excellence of faith and love: love, they said, is a higher grace than faith by your own St. Paul’s testimony, yet you make faith, not love, the ground of justification. He would answer: I do not make what you call the grace of faith the ground of justification. I do not tell a man to ask how much faith he has and, if he has so much, to call himself justified. I tell him precisely not to do this; that is the trick he has been practising on himself under your teaching. He is not to think or speculate about his faith at all. He is to believe, and by believing lose sight of himself and forget himself. So I cannot allow that he is justified by love, though I admit it is the highest grace. Trust is the beginning of love, the way to it. A Being who shows He cares for me, and in whom all love dwells, proposes Himself as an object of my trust; I trust Him and so enter into knowledge and sharing of His love, and that love works in me to will and to do of His good pleasure. I do not say Luther fell into no inconsistencies on these points. Those who search his writings for inconsistencies will be amply rewarded. Those who look for a strong, steady current of thought running through his perplexities and contradictions, often made more evident by them, will find that too. In support of my view I make one remark. It is rare for the character of the doctrine a man spends his life proclaiming to stand in direct contrast to his own character; usually the one takes colour from the other. Readers of Luther’s history would expect him to throw more of his own personality, not less, into any principle he defended. Now Luther is often condemned as coarse, rude and impatient; did anyone ever say he was not plain-spoken and substantial enough? Is there not then a strong à priori improbability that his doctrine, whatever charges it may be open to, is guilty of just this one, of merely pursuing or fighting shadows? Might one not be glad to find an escape from a supposition that must seem utterly startling and inexplicable to any ordinary person who is not a theologian?

3. With one other objection I shall have met, I believe, all those likely to perplex people today. Many students cannot see how the doctrine of justification by faith differs from the general doctrine of atonement, which the Romanists recognised, in words at least, as strongly as Luther.

They say: we can understand that many practices sanctioned at that time interfered with full acknowledgment of our Lord’s sacrifice, and that it may have been important to reassert the principle strongly to protest against those abuses. But the doctrine was there, and the Romanists insisted on faith in it; what more have we to do? If Protestantism has got rid of harmful outgrowths of the older system, let us be thankful. But why maintain this particular mode of expression, which was apparently adopted for a temporary purpose and has accomplished it?

I think my statement of Luther’s doctrine is to some extent an answer. He did not call on men to acknowledge a new doctrine or an old one, or to believe an opinion about justification or about the atonement. He called on them to believe in God the Father Almighty, in Jesus Christ His only Son our Lord, and in the Holy Ghost. He said again and again that the Credo was justification. He told men that union with Christ was deliverance from sin and condemnation, that this union is claimed and maintained by faith, and that faith is therefore justification. The assertion is either true or false. If true, it cannot have ceased to be true. The circumstances that called it forth may have passed, but the law it proclaimed is as much a law for us as for those to whom Tetzel sold his indulgences. And those who raise this objection seem to think that we can substitute faith in a notion or dogma for faith in a living person, though the men of the sixteenth century could not. I rather think that is a temptation we have yielded to more than even the Romanist did, and from which we almost need a second Reformation to deliver us.

But I do believe the objector really means something important. I suspect the difficulty about the relation between justification and atonement is a very important one, which did not force itself on the Reformers but does on us. People are expressing it to themselves in various ways, some of them confused, tending to increase our perplexity and rob us of distinctions and principles hard-won for us. But it must be earnestly considered, and it will receive some practical resolution, a very mischievous one if not a satisfactory one. The question is this. Can the doctrine of justification, if it keeps its Lutheran meaning, as the assertion of a man’s personal position and duty, and does not degenerate into the most lifeless of formulas, hold the position it holds in much Protestant divinity? Can it be put forward as the truth at the foundation of the Christian Church? Does this view honour the doctrine, or only kiss it in order to kill? We shall consider this when we inquire into the Protestant systems and their workings. The reader may be less unwilling to join me if he sees, as I hope he does by now, that I desire as much as he does to assert the principle in its integrity and fulness.

II. 1. The mystics, who oppose chiefly the side of justification that connects it with the Divine Will, must dislike seeing that idea so prominent in Calvinistic theology. It is not hard to guess, from their earlier arguments, what substitute they would offer for the doctrine of Election. Each man, they say, stands in a certain relation to light and darkness. Following the light and submitting to the Divine Word, he enters an elect state; preferring darkness, he becomes reprobate. In the first case he acts according to God’s purpose, in the second he resists it. But this does not make man the author of his own salvation, for the nature of his act proves otherwise. It is more properly an act of submission than of choice, though it involves choice: the surrender of his own will, whereas the opposite act is the assertion of his own will, a declaration of independence.

I have already recognised the principle in this statement so strongly that I make no exception to it. I accept it as a satisfactory explanation of the practical conditions under which every man acts, conditions that remain true whatever other truth is involved in them. The only question is whether this doctrine of the Divine Word can set aside or make unnecessary the distinct, formal belief in a primary, absolute, originating Will. I have said I think it cannot. That belief seems to lie deeper than the one about our relation to the Divine Word, and to be its necessary ground. Take that ground away and I cannot see that we keep any acknowledgment of God in Himself; we contemplate Him only in reference to us and His operations on us. The mystical doctrine may explain the position of man; but these very circumstances, if the doctrine is true, imply a theology, and that theology is what mysticism, it seems to me, lacks.

2. Again, those who complain that the Lutheran doctrine of justification leads to belief in a fictitious righteousness see in election an arbitrary dispensing with all righteousness. “A person receives eternal life because God is pleased that he should. Grant that the obedience is decreed as well as its reward, yet where is freedom, the essence of any obedience acceptable to a perfect Being? You can make the doctrine reasonable only by admitting that divine election is the foresight of human obedience.”

I would admit that proposition, or any other however startling, rather than acknowledge the great primary contradiction that the source of all being is self-will. But one contradiction is no escape from another. The idea of an obedience in man with no ground to rest on, foreseen by God but not derived from Him, a good that cannot be traced to the Fountain of good and exists independently of it, under what we usually consider the very condition of evil, is an agonizing contradiction. And what need have we of it? Only do not suppose the Being you worship to be a mere power. Acknowledge Him to be in reality what you say He is in words, essential truth and goodness. Suppose the absolute will to be a will to good, and how can we imagine that happiness, obedience and freedom originate anywhere but in Him, or that misery, disobedience and slavery mean anything but revolt and separation from Him?

3. The last complaint against this doctrine parallels the last against Luther’s. Does election not mean the election of a body? Has it anything to do with individuals? I make the same answer as before. Every individual must be in some state, and every individual ought to know to whom he should ascribe it. The Reformers were dealing especially with the circumstances of individuals, and meant to explain to whom each should attribute his election. But what each man’s true state is, how each stands in relation to a body, and whether the election of an individual can be viewed apart from the election of the Church, are questions forced on us now. They may be resolved in a way in which some Reformers and most of their disciples would not have resolved them.

III. 1. The Reformers’ language about the Bible was probably more offensive to the Quakers and mystics generally than their doctrines of justification and election. The notion of a book to which men who have the Inward Light and are guided by the Spirit must defer as an absolute authority puzzled and confused them. Yet they did not deny that the more they walked in the Light and submitted to the Spirit, the more sympathy they had with its words and the less they cavilled. So here, as in the two former cases, they tended to translate the Reformers’ language into their own, and to call it false in any other sense. The spiritual man can discern spiritual truths under the letter of Scripture, so for him it has meaning and great value. But to call it divine in itself, as words and records of facts, and to hold it up as an object of reverence in that character, was to turn men’s eyes from the true light and, so far as possible, quench it.

I have already admitted the truth of this statement so far as it describes the faculty Scripture addresses, and argued that those who seem most startled by it implicitly recognise it. Most people, in some form of language, will confess that all revelation is to the conscience, to the inner man, and that when the conscience is not awake and the inner man is buried, the revelation is not really made. And the most consistent interpretation of this, I am anxious to maintain, is that man is created for union with the Living Word and is not in a true living state except in union with Him. But if this is the state not of one man but of all, and if each man, as he enters his true state, becomes more of a man and less of a mere individual, does it not seem strange that there should be no instrument through which the mind of the Living Word is expressed to the race, one that overreaches the feelings and judgments of each particular mind while giving them clearness, purity and strength? Does the expectation of such an instrument, a conviction that it is necessary and will be given, not grow just as we take in this idea and see how it contradicts the notion that each man is a law to himself? Supposing there were such an instrument, what kind must it be? You say the same facts, words and records convey a different meaning to the enlightened spiritual man and the fleshly ignorant man. Be it so; what prevents us from believing that the truth meant to be conveyed should be conveyed in facts, records and words? Why can it not be a spiritual communication because it is embodied in ordinary human discourse? Can you imagine it embodied in any other form? I willingly grant you may not be able to conjecture what facts and events would be adequate to make known to man the law of his being, his relation to God, and God’s character. But suppose you were told that a peculiar nation had been selected as the organ of a divine communication to the nations, and that all its circumstances had been contrived to fit it for that function. Would you say it was à priori improbable that this would be the method the Lord of man adopted? Would you not feel a singular fitness in it, and some difficulty in conjecturing how any other method could equally accord with the principle we have acknowledged?

Where, then, does the mystic’s reluctance to receive the reformed doctrine come from? I believe from the same defect we have seen in him already. He perceives the conditions under which man exists and his relation to a divine guide and teacher, but does not trace that relation up to its ground in an Originating Will. Stopping short of that, he cannot, I think, heartily believe in a Revelation. He thinks of the eye that receives the Light and cannot steadily reflect that there was a Light before the eye, which called into being the eye that should behold it. I do not say he does not implicitly acknowledge this truth. But its explicit acknowledgment is what I believe gave the Reformation all its moral strength and grandeur, and above all gave the Protestant doctrine about the Bible all its meaning.

2. The Quaker complains that this doctrine subjects the spiritual man to the government of words and letters. A much more popular objection is that it frees particular men, however ill-taught and undisciplined, from all authority but their own. “Interpretations of Scripture have been compiled by a series of wise, learned, holy men, some taught directly by the apostles. So there are evidently amazing difficulties about its meaning. Yet the most ignorant mechanic is to be treated as if he could take cognizance of it and fully understand it.”

However the Reformers’ doctrine of Scripture may have become identified with private judgment, it did not present itself to them in that form but in the most dissimilar form imaginable. They expressly proclaimed the Bible to be the book that puts down and humbles private judgments, asserts its claim to be heard above and against them all, and is able to make that claim good. They believed its words came with power: when it spoke, man felt that power and either submitted or consciously rebelled. I appeal to anyone who has looked into their writings whether this is not their habitual tone. Was the notion absurd or extravagant? It might have been justified, and was to the men of the revival of letters, by obvious analogies. Was it the fact that men, women and children in the Greek isles and on the shores of Asia heard the Homeric songs from wandering rhapsodists and received them into their memories and hearts? That in the most cultivated period of Athens the songs were still heard, with perhaps less genuine admiration, but with delight and a confession of their strange power? That afterwards they became subjects of philosophical speculation for Aristotle, but at the same moment stirred Alexander to invade the East without reference to his master’s criticisms? Did English or German schoolboys wait to study Aristotle or Eustathius before they felt them, and in the best sense understood them, in their first dress or their own tongue? What sane man will say there was arrogance of private interpretation in all this, or that it set up a right to criticise rather than abandoned all such right in submission to an irresistible influence? Were the Ionian women and children, the Macedonian prince and the English schoolboys disparaging the labours of Aristotle or Eustathius? Were they not giving them the best justification?

All the Reformers said, then, in claiming the Bible for peasants, was this. If it pleased God to make Himself known to His creatures, and this book contains the records of that revelation, it is no stranger to expect His power to go forth and carry the book’s meaning home to those it was meant for, and the words of a human book to make themselves intelligible. Nor must the peasants of Christendom wait for a commentary before opening their ears and hearts to the one book, any more than the peasants of Greece had to wait for a commentary before receiving the other. This was their notion, though it did not even need this reasoning to confirm it, since they had evidence before their eyes that the Bible did speak to poor men and make itself heard, the more as it was more directly and livingly set before them. I say livingly, for we must not impute to the Reformers the opinion that the power of the book would be felt by the mere reader. They attached an importance and sacredness to the preacher’s office that we are apt, not without reason if our own day’s circumstances govern our belief, to consider extravagant.

3. There is another side to this objection. The doubt recurs, as in the other cases: is this power promised to individuals or to a certain body? Are individuals as such to expect that the word of God will reveal itself to their hearts and consciences? The former answer must be repeated. The Reformers certainly believed the word was to each man, not to a mere mass of men, and that the Bible had its peculiar lesson for everyone as well as its general lesson for the world. But to ascertain how the two lessons bear on each other, and under what conditions a man may hope to profit by either, we must know whether he is in his true state living in a certain body or standing aloof and asserting his independence. This is, as I have said, a very important question, perhaps the question for us today to decide. Some help may come from the comparison I first used in defence of the Reformers. The Homeric poems were sung to Greek women and children, and were received and loved because they had Greek sympathies. We receive and love them because, as members of a nation, we can enter into those sympathies. Whether savages with no sense of society could have listened with equal rapture, or any rapture at all, is worth considering. But this is only a hint for reflection; the subject needs fuller consideration.

IV. It will be more convenient to pass over for now the objections to the Reformers’ doctrine about National distinctions and the power of Sovereigns. Until we have considered many topics not yet before us, the force of these objections cannot be appreciated, so it would be unfair to try to refute them. The omission matters little, since we shall find that the systems the Reformation produced have been only indirectly affected by this tenet.

One remark I must make, however, to make clear the contrast I have tried to draw between the Quaker tendency and that of the Reformers. I said the assertion of an Absolute Will was the main peculiarity of the Reformers, and the assertion of a relation between the Divine Word and His creatures that of the Quakers. This might seem inconsistent with my other statement, which will be readily admitted, that Luther delighted to realise our Lord’s connection with all human circumstances and relations while Fox turned away from such contemplations altogether. A minute’s thought removes the contradiction. The Quaker perceived the relation between the heart and spirit of man and its Divine Teacher. To connect ordinary human relations with this seemed to him impossible, almost profanation. The Reformer, standing on the ground of the Divine Will and seeing the Bible as the revelation of that Will, had no such delicacy. The common earth was God’s creation. Kings, fathers and husbands were appointed by Him and spoken of in His word, and the whole economy of His kingdom had been carried on through them. The Papists had treated the world as the devil’s, with their “touch not, taste not, handle not,” but there was no safety in such abstinence; safety lay in serving God with a clean heart and thanking Him for His gifts. Such was the Reformation feeling, in which we must see signs of a high truth, which might lead to deliverance from sensuality or materialism or be perverted into them. This was at all events the immediate effect of its proclamation. The Teutonic nations, where family life had always flourished, where the King could assert a position distinct from that of the premier baron, and where there was a tendency to business and enterprise, became Protestant. The Latin nations, with a lower standard of domestic and national feeling, but more of the feeling and sympathies that dispose to general social intercourse, and also, I think, more tendency to pure contemplation, continued to call themselves Catholic.

Section III.—Protestant Systems.

While I have maintained that the Protestant principles are inseparably connected, and that all are implicitly contained in the first, I have also hinted that they appeared in quite different aspects and relations to the different Reformers. Justification was the central thought in Luther’s mind; Election in Calvin’s; the Authority of Scripture in Zwingle’s; the Authority of Sovereigns in all the political patrons of Protestantism, and in some of its theological champions, especially in England. These differences showed that persons who bore the common name of Protestants had different, even opposite, habits of mind. They had a right to the name, not only because all opposed Romanism but because all accepted the positive doctrines Romanism denied. And the differences foretold the growth of immediate divisions.

I. The character of the German Reformation is mainly, but not wholly, expressed in Martin Luther. Most students feel that to understand it fully we must connect Philip Melanchthon with him. Some have wondered that Luther, whose language against the Church’s teachers, in his own time and in the past, is probably more vehement than any other Protestant’s, should so sympathize with the man least inclined to separate from the old Church, and should turn away with dislike from those working to consolidate a Protestant System. It is certainly curious, and I think it cannot be explained merely by the influence a calm, logical man has over an ardent, energetic one. The truth seems to be this: Luther believed at first, and to the end of his life, that the Creed and the Sacraments were the great witnesses for justification, if it was not proper to call them acts of justification. They were so partly because they were acts of trust in a person, and partly because the sacraments at least were, he believed, not merely human acts but acts of God, recognising and adopting those who received them. But everything in the new effort to create a Protestant system was drawing men away from this creed and these sacraments. Systematic articles and confessions were beginning to be formed; justification was again being taught in the scholastic manner as one dogma among many; its very meaning was escaping. Melanchthon was probably scarcely aware of this danger, for he was an Aristotelian schoolman, half inclined to accept the scholastic theories Luther abhorred. But his dislike of separation led him to the same result. To leave the old German Church, the Church of his fathers, was terrible to him. He would have said: “We have protested against the abuses of Romanism; perhaps our work is done.” Asked what then becomes of justification, he would have said: “Has it not been asserted in a sense in the Church at all times? The doctors maintain a justification.”

For a moment such words may have moved Luther, whether they came from himself or from his friend, and they will have gained strength from some Anabaptist atrocity or some Zuinglian discourse on the Eucharist. But then an infamous act of the Roman court will have come to light, or an imperial decree will have made reconciliation impossible, and such thoughts will have been thrown off as suggestions of a fiend. His life’s business was to assert justification: not a justification, but the one real justification. A man who asserted this could have no peace with Rome; he must break every bond, and even the name Catholic must be cast away. There must be an Evangelical Church, witnessing for justification by faith though all Christendom witnessed against it, and Germany was to be its seat. But it was not to be built on a mere notion; the Sacraments were to be its great constituents. Baptism was to declare to its members their spiritual citizenship. They were not to regard the Eucharist merely as a feast at which they express their own faith and love. The consecrated elements were not to be spoken of as made something by the receiver; they are something in themselves, consubstantiated with the Divine Presence. This is the Lutheran system, and the Evangelical Church of Germany professes to be its great guardian.

II. It is evident from this that although the leading Protestant doctrine was meant to be embodied in Lutheranism, we must look to Calvinism for the purely Protestant system. We need not suppose that Calvin, from the moment he began to witness against Romanism, planned a separation from the old Church; that would be contrary to all we know of ecclesiastical history and of the people who acted in it. But the idea of an Ecclesia made up of individuals taken out of the world by Divine Election was always present to his mind, and gradually subordinated every other idea. Since all the appearances and conditions of the so-called Church outraged this idea in his view, it had to embody itself elsewhere. A self-willed act to construct a new body of believers might not be justifiable. But the circumstances of the time seemed to show God’s will and purpose, and his position at Geneva might let him carry it out by planting the seed of a divine society.

At first he would have wished this society, except in acknowledging a Pope, to correspond to the one he did not deny God had once established, though it had become so corrupt. Some links with that old stock might be good on some grounds, though dangerous on others. But the main point was that it should witness to the idea of a distinct election, and so the question practically decided itself. The Church was essentially a collection of individuals. Now an instinct, helped by his learning, taught Calvin that Episcopacy involves another idea: it witnesses to something besides mere individual association. So Episcopacy was at any rate not necessary; might it not be, on the whole, a perplexing and unintelligible institution?

On some other points Calvin could use language not very different from that of the Fathers and the Catholic Church. He gave high importance to Baptism and a mysterious worth to the Eucharist. Where then was his difference from them, and even from Luther? The Fathers actually regarded the Incarnation as the foundation of the Church, and Luther wished to; Calvin sought that foundation in individual election. All other differences are included in this one. The idea of election involved the idea of a particular redemption; the selection of particular men, viewed merely as a Divine decree, logically implied the reprobation of the rest. So the Calvinistic system is formed, a system essentially distinct from the Calvinistic principle but necessarily involved in the constitution of the Calvinian Church. Men came from other lands to Geneva, the nucleus of this system and cradle of this Church, for teaching and illumination. From there came John Knox, who planted the most vigorous shoot of the Genevan stock. From there came the Englishmen who had been refugees under the Marian persecution, to lay the foundation of our Puritanism and the nonconformist sects that have issued from it. However much these bodies differ, the Calvinistic doctrine is the animating principle of them all; when it is forgotten or merged in another, their existence has no meaning.

III. Luther and Calvin greatly revered the old Creeds, and had some reverence for the Fathers. Those in whom reverence for Scripture took the place of every other feeling gradually acquired the habit of disparaging both, though this was not their main characteristic. Looking at the written Word as the declaration of God’s will and His great gift to man, they grew impatient of the value the other Reformers put on the Sacraments, especially the Eucharist. Had not this been the great snare of the Romanists? Had not belief in sacramental grace made them substitute something else for the great facts the Bible records? It was well, as commanded, to keep a memorial feast of those facts, or of the most transcendent of them, as the sign and bond of church fellowship in all ages; but the idea that this feast had the virtue the German and even the Genevan doctor inclined to attribute to it opened the way to all superstition. These were the elements of a peculiar system, but they had not strength to be the groundwork of a society. The Zuinglians gave way for a time to the Calvinists, and their maxims were embodied nowhere; but for that very reason they were destined to exercise a more powerful influence over the whole Protestant mass.

Another influence of the same kind began to show itself within the century of the Reformation — of the same kind in more senses than one, though apparently most unlike the Zuinglian, since that seemed to contain the very essence of Protestantism and this to contradict its most remarkable peculiarity. I mean the doctrines of Arminius and Grotius. On one side they bore the distinct stamp of the Reformation. They were set up against all mystical notions; they were offered as the plain, popular, practical view of men’s duties and responsibilities; they were drawn from texts of Scripture; and their chief promoters probably felt them to be much more unlike the sacramental views of the older Church than the Calvinistic views were. But on the other hand, acknowledgment of the absolute will of God was believed to be, as we have seen, the recognition on which all the Protestant doctrines, not one only, were grounded. Because Calvinism had put this acknowledgment forward more prominently than Lutheranism, it had become almost identical with Protestantism and was believed to be the witness against the self-willed inventions and self-righteous doctrines of the Romanists. It was not strange, then, that vehement Protestants in England and elsewhere identified Arminianism with Popery and thought the same decisive measures were needed to root out one as the other. They succeeded in preventing Arminianism from establishing itself as a rival Church; they wholly failed to prevent it from leavening the minds of those who adopted the Genevan model and subscribed the Genevan confessions.

IV. Our last duty in this section would be to consider how far any of these systems became connected with the government of the nations where they were established, or whether any other has arisen to assert the relation between Protestantism and national life. But the last of these questions is closely tied to the history of the English Church, and the first will be better considered under our next head.

Section IV.—The Practical Working of the Protestant Systems.

I. Luther’s character, like that of most true Teutons, mixed hearty joviality with deep sadness. It has often been remarked that the sadness, which went with his conflicts and his calling, weighed on him painfully in his later years, when one might have hoped for serenity if not sunshine. Romanists, and many who are not Romanists, say this allows only one inference: he felt bitter disappointment in the result of his labours, and if pride had let him, he would have confessed that he had entered on them rashly and sinfully. That sounds plausible and convincing to those who suppose a man begins such a work as Luther’s calculating some effect that will bring himself satisfaction, profit or honour, or even benefit the world. But no one to whom mankind owes real gratitude was ever governed by such calculations. A mighty Power which he must obey drives him on. At every step he is reluctant, and often says to himself, “I will speak no more words in His name.” He is ashamed and confounded that someone like him should pull down and destroy, but the fire in his heart must come out whatever it consumes. Such a man will have much sorrow in himself and will be little understood. All he can say in his defence is, “I know this was to be done, and that I was to do it.” Men will say a knave could use the same words. He will admit it and answer, “Whether I be a knave or no, I do not stand before your tribunal. My judgment is with the Lord, and my work is with my God.” Romanists may say Luther accomplished nothing he wished or his time required when they can explain, without reference to him, the extraordinary change in the morality and energy of their own hierarchy in the next generation. Protestants may sneer at Luther when they have made sure that every step out of the errors they deplore in their own systems would not be taken most effectively by understanding and entering into his spirit. They must also make sure that every attempt to set aside his principles would not entrench those errors and add others from which they were once free.

Still, the fact must not be concealed: Luther did feel that Protestantism in every form, even the one he founded, would not be an adequate or faithful witness to the truth he lived to proclaim. He did not merely foresee the loss of the freshness and fervour of new converts. He felt, though he could not find a reason that satisfied him, that something in the very idea of the Evangelical Church would force it into great practical contradictions. Experience has justified his fears, and, faithfully used, may help us find their ground.

As soon as a body was set up expressly to assert justification by faith, its confessions and formularies setting out that doctrine came naturally to be held in the highest esteem. They were the casket holding the jewel, and the jewel could not be kept without the casket. It was no use saying the Creed contains the doctrine, or, as Luther would say, that whoever can declare “I believe in God the Father…” is justified, because Romanists accept the Creed too, and justification had to be defended against Romanists. It was still less satisfactory to say the Bible contains it: Romanists accept the Bible, and the Bible, interpreted one way or left uninterpreted another, might seem to deny it. So a particular interpretation of the Creed and the Bible had to be guarded and upheld. The formularies were carefully worded to include that interpretation and exclude every other, and to them we must adhere.

Nobody could see how to escape this argument; it seemed conclusive. Yet very soon some men arose who said they had been deceived. You tell us justification is our bond of union, they said, but it is not. Justification is a living thing. The justification Luther and St. Paul speak of is the deliverance of a man’s conscience from a burden and a bondage. The justification you speak of is a notion or theory about something you call by that name, contained in a document you call a confession. This will not do. The Evangelical Church is no Church, and does not deserve the name, unless it consists of men who are really justified. And the Bible, a real book speaking of real justification, must be the Church’s book, not these formularies; otherwise Protestantism is not Protestantism.

This proclamation went out and was felt to be true. The living preacher was followed and the dry doctrinalist deserted. But what is the living preacher to do with his followers? They are to form the true Church. How is it to be established that they do? We must see that they really feel what they profess and experience this justification, not merely use the word. So another set of tests had to be introduced, and another set of books written to decide which tests were sound and which false.

Then comes another reaction. What! it is said, do you call this Lutheranism? Luther abhorred feelings and experiences; all Romanist imposture lurks in them, and he set up justification as a witness against them. This proclamation too goes out, is seen to be true, and is heeded. The preachers of feeling and experience are pronounced unsound. What can be done but return to the good old way? The confessions regain their esteem. Believing them must mean believing justification; there is no other rule to be had. The record of this series of reactions is the longest and most important chapter in the history of Lutheranism.

But there is another chapter. I said that the ideas of Imputation, Satisfaction and Representation were expressed to Luther in living acts of faith and devotion: in the Psalms, the Creed and the Sacraments. Apart from these he did not wish to contemplate them, though controversy might force him to. But his disciples made it their business to set out all these ideas, inseparable as they were from justification, in very precise and accurate expressions. The nature and mode of imputation had to be described in propositions. It had to be made clear and definite to everyone how divine Justice was satisfied, and what amount and measure of sacrifice was needed to deliver man from the penalty of sin.

Had these statements taken the purely scholastic form that theological propositions had before the Reformation, people would have seen that they were not sufficient for men’s needs, and that something else was required, since the Gospel was meant for the poor. But the Reformation, as we have seen, had a peculiarly popular character; Protestantism addressed itself to common men. Even the books written for preachers had to share this character, since the preachers were to hand the dogmas on to the people. So the definitions and propositions became strangely mixed with popular illustrations, and the language of the schools and the language of the world were blended into a bewildering mosaic. Precedents and customs from the law courts, maxims of trade, and the vulgarest proverbs of worldly men were pressed into the service of the sanctuary to explain and defend the acts of Him before whose righteous judgment all these customs, maxims and proverbs must finally be brought. And in human dealings, deep and true principles sometimes, through misunderstanding or misuse, come to act as fictions and be regarded as such. As fictions they are praised as clever by men who do not know that whatever use they have comes from the original truth in them. These semi-scholastic, semi-popular theologians carried this habit of thought into their own study, and taught their disciples to think it nothing horrible that fictions should be attributed to the God of Truth.

I do not say this temper was more characteristic of the Lutheran or Evangelical Church than of other Protestants. But it particularly affected the doctrines that Church had undertaken to defend, and the opposition to it was perhaps more marked there. That opposition arose from the feeling that these dogmatic teachings had nothing to do with practical morality, and even harmed it. The Reformers had resisted the Popish notion that family duties, national duties and the business of common life were less holy than the services of the cloister, and had openly or implicitly discouraged the idea that men who renounce ordinary social life may attain a peculiar saintship. Now their language was turned against the doctrines they bequeathed. Christianity, it was said, must have as its main object a pure, simple, practical scheme of Ethics. It could not be meant to introduce a theory more difficult than any heathen philosopher’s, since he had not pretended to provide a gospel for the poor and ignorant. Least of all could it contain notions about God’s ways, designs and character that flatly contradicted our ordinary notions of justice and benevolence. As this way of thinking spread, a set of maxims, partly about outward conduct and partly about the discipline of temper, affections and dispositions, took shape and was received as the essential part of Christianity. These became the main topics of the preacher’s discourse. The schoolman’s business was to show how the Bible might be interpreted as meaning no more, or not much more (doctrines being freely interpreted), than these maxims. Some satisfied themselves more easily than others that they had managed it. Those who were critically honest felt great difficulties: much of the Bible had to be given up so that their opponents’ notions would get no support from it. Luther himself had set the precedent, and it was possible to believe that carrying it out was carrying out the principle of the Reformation. Hence the beginning of the form of Rationalism that marked the last age.

These modern Protestants demanded a simple, practical, intelligible system that the wayfarer who ran could read. But experiment showed that the wayfarer who ran did not read the scheme of Christianity presented to him. The poor said it had nothing to do with them. This startling practical difficulty led to results affecting Protestantism in every direction, not just in its Lutheran form. What I want the reader to notice here is how little the body that took justification by faith as its motto and principle has been able, at any stage of its history, to assert that doctrine. Whether interpreted by earnest believers or stiff dogmatists, orthodox doctors or mere moralists, the system has constantly been labouring to strangle the principle to which it owes its existence.

I cannot touch now on the later history of Lutheranism. It belongs to the record of a great struggle whose beginning our time has witnessed and whose completion it may witness: whether the doctrines the Reformers proclaimed are to be overcome by Romanism, merged in Pantheism, or find a surer basis than either.

II. Some of my remarks on Luther apply to the Calvinistic bodies, but they have features of their own that deserve separate consideration. We have seen that the idea of an Absolute Will choosing individual men out of a fallen world is not merely recognised by these bodies but is the ground of their existence. All ages have shown the strength and the deep, irresistible truth of that belief, and the history of Calvinism not least. The same divine might (I dare not call it anything else) that went forth with the Islamite armies was given, I believe, not seldom to the Covenanters of Scotland and the Puritans of England. The Muslims proclaimed that God’s will was still the supreme law and the spring of all human energy, at a time when Christians had lost the faith that they were God’s chosen servants and had sunk into a slavish unbelief in a spiritual Presence and Kingdom. The Covenanters and Puritans likewise dared to trust a spiritual arm and mock the human and material weapons set against it. God forbid that we should lose the lesson of their victories, or deny that they were victories given to faith, whatever inconsistencies were mingled with it.

But in the Mahometan conquerors, mingled with the very truth that gave them their power and, to their understandings, inseparable from it, there lay a dark and desperate fatalism. The difference in its effects was to prove how unlike it was to that truth, and to prove that the system of Mahomet, which owed its life to this principle, was a lie and carried a curse, not a blessing, for mankind. The history of the Calvinistic bodies should show whether a similar fatalism is hidden in their creed. It should also show whether they owe their escape from the gloom and helplessness that followed the early vigour of the soldiers of the Crescent to the sound parts of their creed, or to the influence of a surrounding atmosphere that they did not create and would gladly have exhausted.

1. The Calvinists on the Continent have shown little of the energy I attribute to their principle since they were first established. A Dutchman or a Genevese might ask me with a sad smile what period in the Reformed Church’s history illustrates it even slightly. But the conflicts of the Synod of Dort soon brought out the denying side of the doctrine and gave it clear predominance. The Arminian doctors set up, or seemed to set up, belief in a will in man against the idea of Election. The Calvinists began to set up the Absoluteness of the Divine Will against the idea of a will in man. Dogmas and determinations came out that were perfectly adequate for contradiction and utterly inadequate for assertion. In the next age the Calvinist found the notion that he had no free will firmly grafted into his creed and rooted in his mind. All he had lost was the clear conviction that there was a Divine will and that he had any connexion with it.

Then began some of the same reactions as among the Lutherans. Men felt they needed more than logical formulas about Election, and declared that the sense and experience of a Divine election was the condition of it. This led to tests for finding who met that condition, and those tests were in turn denounced as setting aside the very idea of absolute and unconditional sovereignty.

The able Dutch and German Commentators who arose in the early part of the last century combined the spiritual and dogmatic elements, but they used the same method already noticed, of illustrating God’s scheme by human precedents. The clear, acute, reasoning temper that Calvinism especially fosters detected the inconsistencies. The disciples of the Genevan Reformer said they were meant to be witnesses for simplicity, and that simplicity in forms ought to be matched by simplicity of doctrine. The Ethical system became universal, and Voltaire wrote to D’Alembert that few preachers in Geneva believed a word of the doctrine Calvin spent his life propagating.

2. I have said as strongly as I can that I believe a vital and powerful element lay in the Scotch Kirk and in English Puritanism, and that it showed in the formation of the one and in the other’s conflicts with our Royalty and Episcopacy. I am not anxious to qualify this by dwelling on the cruelties and meannesses of Scotch Presbyterianism in the sixteenth century, its alternate cringing to the State and insolent domination over it, or on the intolerance and persecution of the English champions of religious freedom in the seventeenth. These may be put down to the temper of the age and to evil lessons learnt from our Church. It is more important to see how these bodies testified to their founding principle after the motives to violence had gone. Their different positions make this a fair experiment. After the Revolution the Scotch Presbyterian body was raised to an establishment; the English sects gained a recognised position but, as they boast, were not hampered by any state alliance. The English Calvinistical sects were also several distinct experiments in expressing their common principle. The Presbyterians held that Scripture lays down one general platform of polity for all the faithful. The Independent asserted each congregation’s right to be its own lawgiver. The Anabaptist maintained that each individual should be conscious of his own adoption into God’s covenant before receiving its sign. In this last sect’s doctrine we plainly see the idea all were labouring to embody. Each of the others confessed it was not a Church but the shell of a Church and its effort to become one. The Anabaptists believed that if they had not actually solved the problem of making the professing body identical with the elect body, they had come as near as possible to solving it.

In the period after the Revolution we have abundant testimony from their own authorities that faith and doctrine gradually decayed in both England and Scotland. In Scotland the older Covenanters ascribed it to the agreement with the government; the English dissenters can account for it only by the general temper of the times. What renewing principle either had to overcome state influence or the world’s infidelity does not appear. Both attribute the restoration of their old doctrine, and something like their former zeal, to action from outside, from a corrupt body against which they were each protesting. Presbyterians and dissenters tell us that the preaching of Whitfield in Scotland, and of Wesley and Whitfield in England, woke a spirit long dormant among them. But only one of these Oxford divines was a Calvinist, and his preaching was not of a kind likely to recreate a Calvinistical system. Once the quickening was given, however, these bodies began to need a doctrine. Just then an American divine appeared who joined remarkable strength of thought to an earnest spirit and what he believed was a profound veneration for the name and creed of Calvin. The Edwards version of that creed, or some modification of it, became from then on the recognised system among English and Scotch Calvinists. Now so far as this system took its complexion from its author’s piety, it is unquestionably an assertion of a Divine Will. The strength of Edwards’ mind seems to have come from acknowledging a distinct Being dwelling in his own Absoluteness and Awfulness. But what gives his system the logical consistency his disciples admire is his way of dispensing with a Human Will. Man is a piece of mere mechanism acted on by a set of motives. He is not a stone, for he has affections and sympathies open to outward influences, but the notion that he can be determined from within is utterly repudiated; the very object of the scheme is to set it aside. Yet anyone who looks at the power the earlier Calvinists put into action, or even attends steadily to their deeper utterances, must see that they not only recognised these inward determinations but that belief in them was the life-giving principle of their minds. Whether or not they could explain the connexion philosophically, the idea of the Divine Will was inseparably bound up with the energy of their own human will; they realised the one in the other. This logical development of the Calvinistical idea therefore stands in the most direct practical contradiction to that idea as it existed in Calvin’s own mind and in all who sympathised with him. I say practical, for this is no quarrel about words. In Scotland especially the working of the new system has been very remarkable. Find any man who has drunk deeply of the spirit of Knox and the old Covenanters, and ask him what doctrine he hears from Scotch preachers generally (I do not mean the Robertson school, but the school most opposed to it), and whether it is essentially what he supposes was delivered two centuries ago. In one form of words or another he will let you know he feels the most violent contrast, and that modern Calvinism is a compound to which, if John Knox has contributed one part, Thomas Hobbes has contributed three. The result is that a young man going from his father’s house to a Scotch university passes by the most natural steps into the philosophical system with which the religious one has been leavened.2 He adopts it as the most consistent interpretation of the phænomena of the world, and the idea of anything spiritual is lost in his mind. Afterwards he may perhaps take up one of the philosophical theories by which his countrymen have tried to modify or subvert pure Materialism and Utilitarianism. But he takes it up as a theory merely; it has nothing to do with his life, where the maxim of the other system reigns the more undisturbed because it appears less formally and obtrusively. Then, if he has a good hardy Scotch understanding, which may dally with abstractions but sympathises greatly with the palpable and the actual, he soon tires of this child’s play and goes out into the world. There he shows by his successful management that he has not lost his forefathers’ sense of individual importance and position, though he turns it to a different and, as he thinks, much more profitable account. I know well what noble minds there are in Scotland, moved by a different influence, who think with bitter pain of the materialism that has crept over their land. But they have no dreams that the old faith can be restored. They speak with great reverence of the first age of their Kirk; they denounce Prelacy and the English Church vehemently; they dwell affectionately on the patriarchal life and discipline that existed, among the middle classes of rural Scotland, a short time ago and was at least connected with Presbyterianism. But they acknowledge that the system is worn out and no longer has power to produce energetic action, deep thought or a simple form of society. They say it flourishes only while it has something to fight with, and that the symptoms of its decrepitude come from evils and weaknesses that were hidden in it at its best. They say that even at its best it could not satisfy the wants of which they are conscious. What these wants are, and in what forms they have expressed themselves, belongs to a larger subject we must presently enter.

The present political crisis in the Kirk will be better treated when we come to its connexion with England. The circumstances of the Nonconformists here are still more bound up with those of our own Church, and it is almost impossible to notice them as illustrations of Calvinism’s history without looking at their political side. As theologians they have struck out no path of their own; what philosophy they have comes from Scotland or America. So it is precisely as bodies with a certain outward organisation that they suggest any important topic for reflection. They themselves seem aware of this, and feel more and more that they exist to oppose and destroy certain institutions established around them. Looking at the sects separately, we find they are confessedly not spiritual bodies, only bodies professing to include a number of spiritual individuals. New congregations arise out of old ones, protesting that these have become earthly and corrupt and that the only hope of a pure Church is in fresh division and secession. Members of the old societies denounce these pursuits of an ideal perfection and maintain that experience has always refuted them. Their own members give accounts of proceedings in electing and deposing ministers and forming congregations that are, to say the least, what men call secular. The sects engage in angry controversies with each other; the Pædobaptists, for instance, vehemently denounce the Anabaptists for the fearful heresy of holding that immersion was the earliest mode of initiation into the Christian Church. The leaders complain of the great deadness of their congregations and try to produce revivals by methods that seem to us thoroughly mechanical and material. These are the indications the dissenting bodies give when looked at separately or in relation to each other. Is it wonderful that they would rather fix our attention on the great united force they can bring to bear at public meetings, in vestry rooms and in newspapers against what they call the secular, anti-spiritual Church of England? If she is secular, or just so far as she is, these weapons may prevail against her, for the secular may be destroyed by the secular. If she is spiritual, they will be as powerless against her as secular armour has always proved against a spiritual principle, whether it came forth in Puritanism or any other shape. But that is not the question now before us. The question is whether the evidence from the Reformed Church on the Continent, the Presbyterian Kirk in Scotland and the English Nonconformist sects tends to confirm or refute two notions: that the Calvinistical principle is a sufficient foundation for a universal Church, and that it can be safely preserved in a Calvinistic system.

III. The Zuinglian doctrine was not able to work out a system or church of its own. And in speaking of the Lutherans and Calvinists on the Continent I have already noticed how faith in the Bible, the strongest element in that doctrine, fared under those who put it forward as their exclusive profession. So I may close here my remarks on pure Protestantism. Our next duty is to trace the characteristics of the system of which Zuinglianism has often been called the parent, and into which, as we have seen, all the Protestant systems showed a tendency to merge in the last century.
