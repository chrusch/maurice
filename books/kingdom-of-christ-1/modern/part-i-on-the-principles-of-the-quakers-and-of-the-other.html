Part I. On the Principles of the Quakers and of the Other Religious Bodies Which Have Arisen Since the Reformation, and on the Systems to Which They Have Given Birth

An Inward Light dwelling in every man, George Fox’s central doctrine, is defended as neither mystical nor unscriptural, yet the Quaker Society is shown failing its own test of a spiritual, universal Church.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Chapter I. Quakerism

Section I.—On the Positive Doctrines of the Quakers.

The Indwelling Word—The Spiritual Kingdom—Spiritual Influences.

Mr. Gurney, in his work on the peculiarities of the Society of Friends, says that the doctrine “which lies at the root of all their particular views and practices, is that of the perceptible influence and guidance of the Spirit of truth.” Earlier he maintains that “a measure of the influence of the Spirit is bestowed upon all men whereby they are enlightened, and may be saved.” Clearly he does not think this second principle nearly as important or as characteristic of the Quakers as the first. I can see why. A mere theory about the condition of the world cannot matter to a man as much as a principle that touches his own conduct and responsibility. But I doubt that the old Quakers would have stated the second doctrine in Mr. Gurney’s terms. I think they would have given it a more practical form, and in doing so would have shown the relative place and value of these two parts of their creed quite differently.

Anyone who reads Fox’s Journal will find that he held to perceptible impressions and influences in the most literal and practical way. His whole conduct was governed by the conviction that he was commanded to do certain acts and say certain words. Wherever he went, whomever he denounced, whatever tone he took, he believed he was obeying a divine prompting. The belief may seem strange today, and some of its results would seem strange even to Quakers. Yet no one who knows the years between 1645 and 1660 will think Fox and his followers were unusual in this. Hundreds, perhaps thousands, in Cromwell’s army lived and acted under the same belief, and as consistently as any Quaker. Fox often clashed with such men. Again and again he speaks of the Ranters who troubled him, on this very ground that they all believed themselves under perceptible spiritual influences. In one remarkable passage he reports that his convert Justice Hotham told him he had been raised up to utter a principle that defeated these Ranters, and that without it they would have overrun and destroyed the whole land.

I. This principle, then, and not the doctrine of perceptible influences, must have been the central one of Primitive Quakerism. An earnest Quaker would even have let the truth and value of his spiritual impressions be tested by their agreement or disagreement with it. James Naylor, whose strange doings at Bristol are in our ordinary histories, confessed he had been deceived by a false spirit or the fleshly workings of his own mind. Yet he professed his faith in Fox’s principle to the last, and saw his errors as the result of departing from it. What was the principle? William Penn, in his preface to Fox’s Journal, puts it this way: “They were directed to the light of Jesus Christ within them as the seed and leaven of the kingdom of God; near all, because in all, and God’s talent to all. A faithful and true witness and just monitor in every bosom, the gift and grace of God to life and salvation, that appears to all, though few regard it.” He calls this “their fundamental principle, the corner-stone of their fabric,” their “main distinguishing point,” and says the light of Christ within, given for man’s salvation, “is the root of the goodly tree of doctrines, that grew and branched out of it.”

Every page of Fox’s Diary shows that this doctrine was the ground of his teaching. A conviction that the Spirit led him might make him break out in this or that steeple-house, or attack this or that Independent, Baptist, Presbyterian or “Common-Prayer man.” But when he spoke, his words were: “Brother, there is a light within thee: resist it and thou art miserable; follow it and thou art happy.” He says repeatedly that these words had a real moral effect on his hearers, and that everything else he said was valuable only as it arose from them or illustrated them. He believed he spoke to something already in those he addressed, and that, being there, it answered his appeal.

Fox did not learn this from the teachers or popular books of his day. The way he describes his early life is quite unlike Puritan biographies. “At eleven years of age,” he says, “he knew pureness and righteousness”; as a child he was taught how to walk so as to be kept pure. Apprenticed to a shoemaker who dealt in wool and cattle, with a great deal passing through his hands, he never wronged anyone, “for the Lord’s power was over him to preserve him,” and people generally loved him for his honesty and innocence. His later inward conflicts had nothing to do with the religious or political controversies tearing England apart. Of Prelacy or Covenant, King or Parliament, he knew nothing. The question that filled his soul was: What am I, and what have I to do in this strange confused world? Every man must meet that question afresh, though thousands have been vexed by it before. Those Fox consulted gave him little help, so he withdrew from people and studied his Bible. Even that did not seem to tell him the secret. But he learnt one thing: something in him shrank from the inquiry and wanted to forget it, and something else in him would have no rest until it found the answer. Was this not already a great discovery, showing him in part what kind of being he was? He had desires that drew him down to what he could see, taste and handle, and desires that aspired to something his senses and appetites had nothing to do with. There was a second discovery in this. Real earthly objects attracted him, and his nature inclined to them, yet when he obeyed that nature he seemed to lose what was most real in himself. Must there not be a counter-attraction, as real as any of those things, raising him out of them and urging him toward a real, substantial good? Must that power not be in truth greater than all that resisted it, however things might seem? Could he not obey it, and by obeying find life and peace? He felt that he could, and was meant to. The light was stronger than the darkness, and he was privileged to dwell in it.

But was this light given only to George Fox the shoemaker? How could that be? It witnessed to him that whenever he was setting himself up, he was resisting it; when he followed his selfish inclinations, he knew he was fleeing this great teacher; and when he wished to be led by it, he knew he was a man. So it must be given to him because he was a man, and must be “a light which lighteneth every man who cometh into the world.” A terrible majority might strive against it, but their striving proclaimed the truth, since the kind of misery men experienced showed the happiness intended for them.

Once he reached this conviction, the Bible seemed a new book to him. From first to last it witnessed to the invisible good men are to seek and against the visible idolatries that draw them from it. The lives of the patriarchs, Moses and the prophets were lives of men following the light, the teacher of their hearts, the Lord of righteousness, and resisting the appetites that would make them slaves and worshippers of outward things. The records of the sins of the Jews and of the heathen were records of revolts from this guide, by men who chose darkness rather than light, the outward and apparent good rather than the inward and real. The darkness grew grosser in each person who gave himself up to it, and the light brighter in each who steadily followed it. So it was in each new period: greater blindness and sensuality on one side, greater and more immediate illumination on the other. Jews and Gentiles grew more estranged from the One who was revealing Himself to both, while holy prophets held more wonderful converse than their fathers with the Word of God, rose further above outward emblems and institutions, and obeyed his inward suggestions more fully. This, or nearly this, is how the Old Testament seems to have appeared to Fox. So the opening of St. John’s Gospel seemed to him to fit naturally with all the records it refers to. And St. Paul’s declarations in the first and second chapters of Romans were no puzzle needing critical ingenuity. Paul says the Gentiles knew God but did not glorify Him or care to keep Him in their knowledge. He says Gentiles as well as Jews who seek glory, honour and immortality will obtain eternal life, and Jews as well as Gentiles who are contentious and obey unrighteousness will have tribulation and wrath. To Fox this was the plain announcement of a truth with which the rest of Scripture agreed.

II. But how was men’s condition affected by the appearance of our Lord in human flesh? This question probably did not occur to Fox at first, but by degrees he and the other Quakers found an answer. Men who had given up their spiritual privileges and surrendered to the flesh were not forsaken by their heavenly Teacher, but could not be treated as spiritual. They were trained by outward emblems and images, the elements of the world. The Jews were given a direct hint of the nature and purpose of their discipline; the Gentiles, through a thicker film of sense and with fewer helps, might still discover their invisible guide if they would. But these were preparations for a clearer day. Christ, the Living Word and Universal Light, appeared to men and showed in his own person what he was carrying on in all hearts: subduing the flesh, keeping himself separate from the world, submitting to death. This manifestation began a new dispensation. Sensible emblems were no longer to block man’s view of his Lord, national distinctions were to be abolished, and men might be treated as belonging to a higher state than the one they lost in Adam, and might reach a perfection that did not exist in Adam.

The Quakers found Scriptural testimony to this on every side. Stripped of the fantastical dress it sometimes wore, it was not, few readers will think, forced or unnatural. The Prophets announced a dispensation marked above all by spirituality and universality. The Sermon on the Mount plainly annulled earlier rules and maxims and put a spiritual principle in their place. Our Lord constantly declared that he came to establish a kingdom, and that it was to be within us. The Evangelists announced that his parables uncovered mysteries hidden from the foundation of the world, and he himself said he would show his disciples more plainly of the Father. Galatians says a spiritual covenant had succeeded the formal Jewish one. Ephesians says an economy hidden for ages and generations was now made known to the Apostles by the Spirit. Philippians and Hebrews urge pressing on to perfection, evidently on the ground of the new position of those addressed. These are only samples of what every page of the New Testament seemed to the Quakers to show: that our Lord came to bring in a universal Light, to establish a perfectly spiritual Kingdom, and to encourage men to seek a perfectly spiritual Life.

III. The very idea of this constitution implies that men are brought under a directly divine government or influence. Those who yield to the light and become members of the spiritual kingdom recognise this influence in all they do. They will not move without it, and will move anywhere at its bidding. Sacrificing all personal inclinations, energies and will, in short self-annihilation in its highest form, is their duty and privilege. So they become fit to utter the divine voice and ready to do the divine will.

For this doctrine the Quakers would plead John the Baptist’s announcement of the baptism of the Holy Spirit and fire as the great promise of the new covenant. They would point to the Apostles’ ignorance until they received the gift from on high, and to the silence and waiting enjoined on them until it came. They would cite the whole course of the Apostolic history, which shows the first ministers of Christ believing they acted under immediate inspiration and could not act without it. And they would add the principle, so often asserted and everywhere implied, that the kingdom was to be everlasting and that those who first saw it established were to be patterns for all who followed.

Section II.—Objections to the Quaker Theology considered.

I am far from saying that the early Quakers acknowledged no theological principles except these three. In a sense they admitted most of the doctrines that other men put into creeds or articles. But these three principles determined that sense. They were the ones the Quakers had truly realised in their minds. The rest hung loosely about them, and at times was heartily recognised and at times almost rejected, depending on whether it seemed to fit the primary truths or contradict them. So these three doctrines make up the positive theology of the Quakers, and their system has been deduced from them. Before I ask what that system is, and how far it is legitimately connected with these principles, I will say briefly why I cannot join some prominent opponents of Quakerism in denouncing them. I believe they are either truths or hints of truths that are vital and important.

I. Three objections are usually brought against Fox’s doctrine of the Inward Light, or the Indwelling Word. First, it is said to be mystical. Second, it is said to be unscriptural. Third, it is said to rest on no fact or authority except that of an ignorant mechanic and his credulous disciples.

1. I will not dodge the first charge by saying that “mystical” can mean anything or nothing, or that it has been used against the best-established principles of physics as well as of morals, or that if mystical means mysterious then all science is mystical. I will give the word a sense that may be legitimate and is at least common. Mysticism is often the name for the tendency to treat the feelings, states of consciousness and temperaments of individual men as sacred, when that sacredness belongs only to truths of universal character that can be brought to a universal test. Religious men in all ages have been prone to this, and I know no records with more frequent instances of it than those of the early Quakers. But on this definition, and I know no other that clearly condemns mysticism, the question is whether the doctrine we are considering is essentially unmystical, and whether we might almost call it the antagonist of mysticism. It disclaims exclusive ownership more vehemently than almost any doctrine, and it submits more directly to a universal test. Fox did not say, “This light is mine.” He said, “It is yours as much as mine; it is with you, and in the healthiest, truest, soberest states of your mind you know that it is.” So the principle stood in marked contrast to the peculiar experiences and interpretations on which he often laid so much stress. It showed its difference from them by its effects. It also gained some sanction from being forced on the conviction of men whose characteristic weaknesses would have led them to an entirely different conclusion.

2. The notion that the doctrine is unscriptural has drawn support partly from the opinion that Fox and his followers habitually disparaged the Scriptures, and partly from Fox’s own admission that he knew the doctrine before he saw it in the Bible, though he later learned to support it from the Bible. I will consider later how far the general charge is true. It does not affect this case, because they appeal not to a few isolated texts but to the whole tenor and context of the inspired volume. Nor do I see anything in Fox’s account of how he came to this principle that differs from what writers most opposed to him commonly say: that once they were spiritually awakened, the Bible, which had been a dead letter, seemed full of meaning, and the only wonder was that they had not seen it before. I think this language is simple, reasonable, true to the experience of most earnest men, and no insult to the Bible. It is not incompatible with the belief that studying the Bible may have been one of the chief means of calling forth the capacity that makes its words understandable. And nothing about the path another man followed should stop us from asking whether his view of a book sheds light on it and makes its contents more coherent. I have given a few reasons that lead others, and compel me, to believe that denying Fox’s doctrine makes the scheme, spirit and letter of Scripture all perplexing. If more proof were needed, I would find it in the violent and tortuous expedients critics have used to escape, as they say, the absurdities of mystical interpretation. For example, I hear a grave, learned and (so far as hostility to Socinianism earns the name) orthodox interpreter suggest that the Λόγος, “the Word,” in the first verse of John’s Gospel means only “the one spoken of,” the person promised. He supports this with the question of John’s disciples, “Are you the one who is to come?”, and treats the two phrases as equivalent. When respectable scholars adopt such a view as a convenient refuge from mysticism, I am forced to think I would not keep my respect for the letter of Scripture purer, or my sense of what is reasonable in human language clearer, by refusing to believe that the Word of God before He came in the flesh was the light that lightened all men. That principle is as much confirmed to me by profane history as by sacred.

3. I speak of this evidence because I think the third objection is just as untenable as the other two. It has been said, and I think justly, that if Fox’s claim about the light the heathens possessed were well founded, their records of acts and thoughts would show it clearly. The indications would not be a few fine sayings scattered among heaps of evil and offensive matter. They could not come from a rhetorician like Seneca, who lived after Christ and might have borrowed Christian notions. They would have to be something other than the mere notions of immortality and an indestructible part of our nature, which have floated in the minds of civilised and savage men, and which had argumentative consistency but no practical influence among the Roman Stoics and Academicians. They could not be drawn from the denials of polytheism into which some Greek philosophers were led, which ended only in atheism or, at best, pantheism. Even a man who steadily recognised a standard of right would not suffice if his thoughts and those of all heathens before and after him moved in opposite directions. I fully admit the justice of these maxims, and I take them as my guide in stating why I think Fox, though he knew nothing of the men of the old world, judged rightly about them.

The best recent works on Greek philosophy, Ritter’s for example, divide its history into the periods before and after Socrates. Anyone who has compared this arrangement with older methods, and seen the light it throws on the growth and sequence of speculations once thought independent, will admit it is the true and natural one. Yet at first sight there seem to be insuperable objections. Socrates left no books. It is often complained that we cannot tell which of his disciples reported him most correctly, what his opinions were, or even whether he had any. All the interest of his doctrine has been said to lie in his life. How then can he be the centre of his countrymen’s theories and systems?

The answer seems to me to be this. However the accounts of him differ, they all testify, the comic poet’s satire as clearly as the narratives of Xenophon and Plato, that he saw in human life a struggle between two principles. One pulled downward and belonged to the earth. The other claimed fellowship with something pure and divine. Those who take him for a mere ironist or sceptic cannot deny that, whatever his words do not mean, they mean this. Those most inclined to reduce his thought, and everyone’s, to a system must admit that this doctrine lay at the base of his system, and that it finds fuller expression in a man’s acts, or in his talk with people of different tempers and pursuits, than in any formal treatise. So it is not strange that one observer should be struck chiefly by his efforts, in disciplining his own mind and his scholars’, to overcome sensual inclinations and lift the spirit to higher and purer apprehensions, using among other means his country’s traditions and faith. Another was shocked at the tendency such efforts to escape the worship of outward things must have had to weaken respect for the country’s gods. A third, a deeper and more earnest thinker, saw in the conviction that governed his master’s life, personal and distinct as it appeared, the hint of a method by which men might be led out of vagueness, superstition and unbelief into pursuit of permanent truth. By that method the imperfect hints, crude generalisations and seemingly contradictory discoveries of earlier thinkers might be interpreted, quickened and reconciled. This last idea may easily have come to anyone who felt, and saw other young men of the day feel, that Socrates interpreted the feelings working confusedly within them and the aims they pursued blindly. It proves itself by experiment: with Socrates as our guide, we can understand what the Greeks were pursuing in different directions for generations before and after him. But if so, then the worth and peculiarity of his life lay in steadily pursuing, amid many inconsistencies, a pure and invisible good, and in trying to overcome the obstacles in himself and the world that kept him from grasping it. That does not set him apart from the thinkers of the old world or make him a prodigy. It lets us see more clearly that the same struggle was going on consciously in every better and truer man, and unconsciously in all. Such, I believe, is the witness the records of Greek philosophy bear for Fox’s doctrine.

It may be said, however, that this testimony is incomplete. Fox always spoke of a personal teacher of men, while Socrates goes no further than acknowledging a search or appetite in man for a supreme good. That good need not be personal, or if personal, may not have originated these desires or taken any interest in them.

I believe no one who attends carefully to the language of Socrates or his greater pupil will suppose he doubted that the longings and movements of his spirit had a divine source and were subject to a divine impulse. He was deeply convinced that an attending dæmon guided him. He constantly referred to traditional stories, and he firmly believed in divine interventions and judgments. These show that he was not merely seeking to grasp “the Being” but often, if not habitually, acknowledging that a Being had first taken notice of him. Still, I admit the remark has some force. So far as Socrates was simply a philosopher, in the sublimest sense of the word, he acted as a seeker after what is true and good, not as a receiver of an influence from it. His high merit was that he acknowledged the need of something besides philosophy in order to realise what philosophy means. That is a very peculiar merit, which sets him and Plato immeasurably apart from their followers and from most of their predecessors. Yet it may be just the merit that makes him so important as the interpreter of other schools. Be that as it may, one fact deserves notice. As soon as a Jew could study and understand the Greek philosophers, the defect complained of was supplied. Philo recognised all the wiser heathen’s cries for light, wisdom and truth as genuine signs of what the prophets had prepared him to expect in all men, and as produced by the teaching and inspiration of the Divine Word. So much has been written lately on the purpose and character of his writings that I need not multiply proofs. A few are given in a note, as specimens of a tone of thinking that could not have been so habitual in one man if many had not shared it.

Some modern critics hold that the study of Philo is the proper introduction to the study of the Fathers. I will not discuss the truth or limits of that claim. Its ground is the discovery of a close resemblance between Philo’s language about the Divine Word and that of one class of Fathers, those who lived in Alexandria before the Arian controversy. The resemblance is generally acknowledged, and inconvenient inferences from it are avoided by calling these Fathers, and often the Fathers in general, Platonizing doctors who mingled the pure truths of Christianity with Gentile Philosophy. Such phrases are hard to meet because they are only phrases. The Fathers who came into direct contact with the philosophers told them, on what they took to be the authority of Scripture, that the Divine Word had spoken to the conscience and reason of men in all ages, and by various acts of discipline had urged them to turn from their idols and seek Him. They said that whenever a man showed any kindly feeling or earnest zeal for truth, this Invisible Guide had inspired it. They showed no mercy to pagan fables or philosophers’ conceits. They simply declared that all error was forsaking God’s guidance and all sincere, good thoughts were obeying it.

Did they, in using this language, give more honour to mere human wit and judgment than those who credit human wit and judgment alone with everything generous and true in the acts and feelings of heathens? I leave that to my readers’ understanding. Is it wise or decorous to attack men whom I may call pious and venerable, not by showing where they contradicted Scripture, but by pinning on them the nickname of Platonizing? I leave that to the consciences of those who use such language. One thing at least is evident: Fox, the seventeenth-century shoemaker, was not the first to understand the opening verses of John’s Gospel in a literal rather than a metaphorical sense. Before leaving this subject I must remark that the charge brought against these Fathers shows where the main objection to the doctrine comes from. We are told, sometimes very solemnly, to beware of corrupting the simplicity of the Gospel with philosophy and vain deceit. The caution may apply less to Fox and the Quakers than to others, for he hated Greek and philosophy cordially and his followers have generally kept faith with him in this. Still, it is an important caution. Those to whom it is offered should receive it gratefully, and they cannot show its value better than by returning it. I believe anyone who investigates the origin of his own opinions will find that neither reverence for Scripture nor love of simplicity, but precisely a slavish addiction to a certain system of philosophy that established itself in this country about the time of the Revolution, is the cause, conscious or unconscious, of his dislike for a principle recognised by the humblest and most ignorant men as well as by the most profound. Earlier thinkers had looked on it as one of the most plausible, natural and degrading forms of error, but it was adopted as a new and surprising truth that there is no knowledge except what comes through the senses. Since then, communion between the Divine Word and the heart, conscience and reason of men has naturally been rejected. The subject will often recur in our inquiries.

II. I need say very little about the other two main articles of the Quaker faith. The principle of both is contained in the one we have examined, and nearly all Christians admit them to a certain extent and under some conditions. The proposition that Christ came to establish a spiritual kingdom, not of this world, differing from the Jewish kingdom by being less carnal and more spiritual, is constantly proclaimed by those English dissenters who are most inclined to denounce Fox’s primary tenet as unscriptural and false. They only think he pushed this truth too far: the kingdom is spiritual, but not quite so spiritual as he fancied. The same goes for the gift of the Spirit and the subordination of man’s powers and utterances to His government. They believe what Fox said was true up to a point, but that going beyond it is dangerous. I shall examine the case for these restrictions later. For now I will say only that I do not think the Quakers have carried their principles to excess. I believe all their errors have come from their narrow, imperfect and earthly notions of what a spiritual kingdom is, and from the low estimate they have formed of the transcendent gift God gave His creatures when His Holy Spirit came down to dwell among them. My meaning will be clearer when I have spoken of the negative articles of Quaker Theology.

Section III.—The Quaker System.

1. It is not hard to imagine how the principle of an inward light must have struck a man educated as Fox was, provided he was perfectly earnest and sincere. I have called it his doctrine, and it was his doctrine, for he taught it wherever he went. Had I called it a dogma, I might have described very exactly what it has become to modern Quakers, but neither word would have seemed right to him. He had discovered a law to which he himself and every other man was subject. Would anyone tell him this was a mere notion, like the ones about justification, sanctification, final perseverance and so on that he had heard from the pulpits of his day? The language of the preachers and the books might be about something that concerned him and all men, but he had found the thing itself. He had a fact to proclaim, not a theory or a system. So from the first he denounced dogmas and formulas as corrupting and misleading. The young mechanic told preachers trained in all the distinctions the Westminster Assembly had worked out with such labour that they knew nothing of what they were talking about. Those who had silenced their brethren for lack of spiritual knowledge were now rebuked for the same sin, and sometimes silenced by the voice of one man, not the vote of a trying Committee. But if formulas were evil, could forms be better? Here were men professing outward acts and ceremonies, and claiming an intimate connexion between these and the Christian life. It seemed strange, almost incredible blindness. Did the Christian life not consist in following an inward Guide and invisible Teacher, and in avoiding what is visible and sensible? What could outward things have to do with that? The argument was irresistible, and bearing witness against such idolatries became a main part of Fox’s vocation.

2. Someone who studied the Old Testament diligently might sometimes have thought that forms had been, in the olden time, the very testimonies for this light, the means by which the Jews were warned against sensual worship. The Jews turned them into excuses for a natural idolatry. Yet, being God’s appointed protests against idolatry and His means of delivering men from it, they were appealed to from age to age by the prophets who told the people their sins. Those prophets were far more diligent in observing the forms than the sensualists and hypocrites they denounced for neglecting their meaning. Such a thought may have glanced into Fox’s mind, along with the reflection that a method good once might be good still. But he could silence such suggestions, or dismiss them as coming from an evil source, by the second doctrine I mentioned. That might have been true until Christ appeared; but Christ came to establish a Spiritual and Universal Dispensation. Because it is spiritual, outward institutions are abolished: circumcision, the passover, a priesthood, outward sacrificial worship, particularly sacred seasons. But are not Baptism, the Eucharist, a Ministry appointed by imposition of hands and divided into three permanent orders, Liturgies, and the observance of Fasts and Festivals equally visible and outward? On what plea have you put one set of ceremonies in place of another, when you claim to belong to a spiritual kingdom?

The dispensation is also universal, so national distinctions are no more; they belong to the economy of the world. War is the fruit of them, and under a spiritual and universal dispensation war is a sin. All nations, the Jewish as much as the rest, have called on God to witness their ordinary transactions; Oaths are forbidden under the new dispensation. Nations have generally provided for ministers of religion and counted them parts of the commonwealth; such arrangements are altogether inconsistent with a spiritual and universal dispensation.

3. The Quakers turned away in disgust from all confessions, so they were unlikely to distinguish the dogmatic articles of later ages from the creeds of the Church’s infancy. Even the simplest creed was objectionable to them, because it directs our thoughts to the outward acts and events of our Lord’s life on earth rather than to His presence in the heart. The Scriptures were a harder question. They record actual events and seem outward in character. Yet the Bible was the only book Fox and several of his brethren knew, and in it he had found the strongest confirmation of all he believed. So the language of the Quakers became more tinged with Scripture than that of any sect, while they described Scripture in terms no other sect would have dared to use. Reading it was said to be a luxury rather than a necessity to the believer, and what mattered most was that he draw his knowledge from the inward teacher, not the outward book. Learned doctors had often warned, before and since the Reformation, of the danger of trusting the letter, and of the impossibility of finding its meaning without help of another kind. But among the Quakers these warnings took on a new and much stronger meaning, drawn from the doctrine of Spiritual Influences. Only the man called by the inward voice was a true teacher, and he taught rightly only at the moment he obeyed that voice. How, they argued, can he also be subject to the dominion of a book? He may read it, and passages may come to his mind, but he will apply them properly only when he feels himself in the position of those who wrote it, speaking by the same inspiration. The book may be the best of books, but it is valuable as an instrument, not strictly as an authority. That seems to have been their practical conclusion, though the wording often varied.

It seemed to follow still more obviously from this belief in immediate spiritual influence that preparatory studies for the ministry were unlawful and faithless. Studies as such might not be positively forbidden, but since the teachers were in some sense the standards of thinking and feeling, a sense of the inexpediency, if not sinfulness, of any high mental cultivation inevitably spread among the disciples generally.

A body holding the positive doctrines and having the negative characteristics I have described gradually formed itself and took the name of The Society of Friends. Its members believed it was called into existence to exhibit the features of the kingdom Christ came to establish. Without wishing to be uncharitable, or denying that good men might not belong to it, they practically regarded it as the Church of God on earth, the witness against the world. They were therefore to keep entirely free from the habits of the world, its changing fashions, its amusements and, as far as possible, its phraseology. The so-called Christian body had been defiled by these. Even the devices by which it seemed to assert its existence were earthly and sensual, bearing no testimony to the distinction between light and darkness, to the spirituality and universality of the kingdom, or to the presence of the Spirit.

Section IV.—On the Practical Working of the Quaker System.

We now have to ask whether this body has fulfilled the office which its founders believed God had appointed it to. What evidence is admissible, and how much will it prove? Quakers agree with us that permanency marks a divine Church. It was never meant to last only a generation; it exists to testify against a changing, capricious world. So neither they nor we may plead the ordinary law of decay in human bodies as an excuse for the Church failing in its functions. Both of us must suppose that He whose handiwork the Church is foresaw this tendency and has somehow counteracted it. The Quakers’ peculiarity is that they do not count permanent institutions and symbols among the remedies provided against the danger. Man may misinterpret such things from time to time, but they go on testifying in spite of his misinterpretations and against them. On the Quaker view, a spiritual body rests on the faith, purity and vitality of its individual members. That being so, I think their confessions of degeneracy from an older standard must be called very startling. They imply little less than that the kingdom God set up in the world has been overcome and crushed by the world’s kingdom opposed to it. Yet these confessions are most numerous in the writings of all the divisions of Quakers today, not of one alone. They take different forms according to the views of those who make them, but in one form or another they appear everywhere. Even so, such evidence, however much it may rouse anxious inquiry among Quakers, could not by itself prove to them or to us that the experiment has failed. The signs of failure should be very palpable. They should not rest on the feelings or observations of particular persons, whether impartial or prejudiced in favour of the system, and they should be clearly connected with the form and order of the Society. Otherwise they ought not to be produced, at least not to disturb anyone who still holds to it.

1. One such indication, I think, will occur to every thoughtful person. The Quakers said that all who do not walk in the divine light, and do not recognise the presence and guidance of an invisible teacher, are of the world. The pure and holy company, the Church, the Society of Friends, must consist of those whom the Spirit leads to perceive their connection with the invisible Guide and to follow Him wherever He leads. No body of men answered to this description, so one had to be formed, and all who did not join it had in practice to be treated as belonging to the world. So far all seems easy. One might think it a little exclusive, with a few people treated as aliens who might be citizens of God’s household, but that could not be helped. A palpable distinction between true men and false was needed. If the distinction was not perfect, it was good as far as it went, and faithful men must expect the Spirit of God to bring everyone, in due time, to see that this was the Society to join. But a difficulty soon arose for which the founders seem to have made no provision. Children are born to the members. What are they, Friends or citizens of the world? The consistent answer would have been: “They are of the world; they are not consciously following the light; until they do, it is a mere dream and contradiction to count them in the Society.” But feeling, and, I believe, conscience, gave a different answer: these must belong to the Society, or it is a sin to have given them life. The only resource was to separate these children, outwardly at least, from the surrounding world by every possible means. Parents would then feel they had done their best, and think as little as possible of the falsehood at the root of the whole proceeding. But a falsehood can be hidden only for a time, and this one is now becoming palpable. The younger Quakers look around and ask what it means that they are kept from the world. If the world means those who do not walk in the light, there is a world inside the Society as well as outside it. Would not their fathers have been right to exclude the idea of blood relationship from the Society altogether? For there is evidently no connecting link between the law by which human society is propagated and the law which governs this body. The heavenly kingdom has nothing to do with earthly relationships. Unless the body could be continually recruited by conversions from the world, it seems it could never escape the penalty of constantly violating the very distinction its presence was meant to testify to.

2. But the Quaker Society was also to witness to a Universal Kingdom. In this faith Penn went out and preached to the Indians. He was satisfied that they had a sense of right and wrong and that the Word was speaking to them as to other men. I believe the results of his interesting mission show how true the conviction was that encouraged him. But what else do they prove? Did the settlement of Pennsylvania become the nucleus of a great missionary society? Did it draw to itself the native Indians and the English settlers? It grew into a colony of prosperous traders, holding a very creditable position in the States, marked off from its neighbours by badges of dress and manners, increasing at the ordinary rate of population, and more indifferent to missionary enterprise than the other sects. I speak of America because the system cannot be said not to have been fairly tried there. Whether you look at Quakerism in the country where persecution made it flourish, or in the one where it had the greatest opportunity to expand, I ask what witness it has borne for universality, and what signs it gives that it is the universal kingdom to be set up on earth.

Perhaps it will be said that the philanthropy of the Quakers testifies to that fellowship with the whole human race which their principles of a universal light and kingdom were likely to foster. I do not at all deny this philanthropy or wish to detract from its merits. I have no motive to, for I heartily hold the doctrine which is rightly supposed to be the only ground of sympathy with man as man. I have no doubt that this principle, or its tradition, has produced whatever is sound and good in the Quakers’ feeling for their white and black brethren. But our question is how far the Quaker system is a witness for that principle, and I think the way Quaker benevolence has shown itself, especially in recent years, is a striking and conclusive answer. The moment they began to do anything besides bearing individual testimonies, the moment they tried general, social, organic acts on behalf of their fellow creatures, they found it necessary to join with members of other societies. They joined societies for distributing the Bible, for emancipating the negroes, for promoting universal peace. Fox and Penn would certainly have done no such thing. They would have said: “Our Society, raised up and constituted by God Himself to witness for what is spiritual and universal against what is earthly and national, is the Bible society, the emancipation society, the peace society; we know of no other, and there can be no other.” Uniting with the world to promote spiritual objects would have seemed to them monstrous. Yet their followers have adopted this as the only method they know for carrying out Quaker principles.

3. Of all the benevolent projects of our day, none has interested the Quakers more than the progress of education. They have been almost the founders of the British and Foreign School Society, and its greatest supporters. This is not the occasion to discuss any point on which I differ from them. I mention their exemplary zeal only to note how much it clashes with the Quaker system, so far as that system asserts the doctrine of spiritual influences. The Quaker minister speaks only when an immediate, perceptible impression determines him that he ought to. To prepare for his work, to receive a regular appointment to it, or to be paid for it, is incompatible with the spiritual nature of the function. But the Quaker teacher, or the teacher whom a Quaker supports in a school, must have a formal appointment, prepare regular lessons, and receive a regular salary. It follows either that the spiritual minister is not appointed to educate, or that education is not spiritual. If education is as important as the Society of Friends think, and I think so too, what testimony is borne here to the spiritual economy, or to the spiritual influences that go forth so that men may administer it? Education, which is to have so mighty an influence on society, is to be conducted on principles exactly the reverse of those proclaimed to be the only spiritual ones.

“But the Quakers,” it is said, “have borne a more consistent testimony than others against the habits and maxims of the world.” I do not now ask what precise meaning to give the word world; I take it in the sense it has among religious people generally and the Society of Friends especially. The world in that sense, though built on one common evil principle, takes many shapes, and a body raised up to protest against it at a particular time or place ought mainly to bear witness against the form most characteristic of that time or place. A society that testified against gladiatorial shows in the nineteenth century, or against cannibalism in Europe, might be praised for prudence but could hardly claim strong evidence of a divine vocation. The Quakers’ position has been almost entirely in Great Britain and the United States, between the Civil Wars and the reign of Queen Victoria. I ask any plain person what has been the characteristic sin of these two countries in that time. No doubt a large body of people in each have made amusements the end of life; but no one comparing England and America with France or Italy would say the pursuit of pleasure has been the especial sin of us and our Transatlantic children, least of all of that part of our populations with which the Quakers come into contact, and whose evils they therefore ought most to have denounced. Again, some people have pursued literature and mental cultivation as the end of life, and overlooked higher and more universal ends for its sake. But this has not been our chief infirmity; other European nations have been far more tempted by it. One deep, radical disease has been infecting our two countries, entering deeper into their constitution over the last two centuries until it has nearly reached the vitals of both. Will not everyone say it is money-getting? How, then, has the Society of Friends borne witness against this sin by its habits and constitution? It says, indeed, that no part of the body’s wealth is to be set aside to support its ministers, whose subsistence is to be wholly precarious. This may be taken as proof that money has nothing to do with what is spiritual. But I do not see how this testimony is to act on the world when the world sees that Friends believe all amusements, and many branches of mental cultivation, to be necessarily evil and incapable of being sanctified to a good purpose, yet believe that acquiring wealth is safe and lawful and is, by the very nature of the community, the business of everyone who enters it. A Society whose members, ministers included, are to all appearance mainly occupied in trade, and which until lately feared being occupied in anything else, is supposed to witness against a world whose most characteristic and irreligious distinction is the worship of mammon.

But has the existence of the Society of Friends had no influence at all in leading men to believe that the heart and spirit are meant to converse with holy and invisible things? I hope it has. I cannot believe any system is permitted to exist which is working no good. There may be minds outside the Society (many inside, of course) to whom Quakerism has suggested thoughts nothing else would have. Yet the positive witness it has borne for spirituality seems to me of the most equivocal kind. I am afraid that if most Quakers were asked what the peculiar spirituality of their body consists in, they would answer: “In our not baptizing, not keeping an outward feast, not offering prepared prayers, not having an outwardly ordained ministry.” And that answer expresses very much the feeling which the Society gives to indifferent people watching from a distance. A man of the world who finds the Church’s ordinances troublesome or meaningless may note now and then that the Quakers do without them and are still a very religious and thriving people. At another time he will be just as struck that, lacking these marks, they have others that seem to him no less outward and visible. They have no fixed forms of prayer but have a fixed form of dress; they have rejected sacraments but keep a peculiar kind of language. A man who is anxiously inquiring what spiritual Christianity means must be puzzled when he is told that those are marks of a formal earthly body and these of one essentially spiritual and divine.

It may be supposed that these are mere accidents of the Quaker profession, showing how strong the tendency to formalism is in the human mind, but removable by those who understand the Society’s true objects. That is a great mistake. The younger Quakers are probably impatient of these restrictions, but not because they have insight into any essential principles. On the contrary, indifference to the outward badges generally goes with indifference to the ideas Quakerism rests on, or with attachment to them only so far as they oppose something else. The older and more earnest members maintain, I believe on just and philosophical grounds, that these peculiarities, unimportant as they seem, cannot safely be abandoned, and that the very existence of Quakerism is bound up with keeping them. They assert, equally truly I think, that every relaxation of the first Quakers’ rules about amusements or literary pursuits makes the body’s existence less intelligible. It even tends to an immoral result, by presenting every restraint on self-indulgence as a hard and needless burden, to be avoided as far as prudence and others’ opinions allow.

I do not venture to predict how fast decay may proceed in a body showing these symptoms. At present Quakerism is threatened from outside on two sides, the Evangelical and the Unitarian. In England the younger Quakers generally want to be more like those who profess what are called the doctrines of the Reformation; in America they have been strongly drawn the opposite way. These feelings may not lead to great secessions beyond those they have already caused. But one or other influence will henceforth predominate, and Quakerism will have less and less a basis of its own. All its grand pretensions are at an end; its greatest defenders now speak of it not as the Church or Kingdom of God but as the best of the sects that make up the religious world. Such language can never satisfy those who keep any of the old Quaker spirit. They must believe there is a spiritual kingdom somewhere, and if they cannot find it among the Friends, they will look for it in the opposing systems I have mentioned. Let us ask what prospect they have of being rewarded for their search.
