Chapter IV. On the Tendency of the Religious, Philosophical, and Political Movements Which Have Taken Place in Protestant Bodies, Since the Middle of the Last Century

Methodism’s personal faith, later religious societies, and German and Scottish searches for a theology converge on Edward Irving’s failed attempt to found a new Church on the Incarnation, suggesting one already exists.

Section I.—Religious Movements.

Methodism—Religious Societies—Search for a Theology.

In one aspect the history of Methodism belongs to the history of the English Church, so it is not part of my present subject. But any considerate reader will admit that France has been the centre of the political movements of the last hundred years and Germany of the philosophical, and that England has been the centre of the religious. I need not claim that the first impulse came from England. Wesley may have taken much of his teaching from Zinzendorf, and other efforts to awaken a more earnest religious feeling may come earlier in time than those our countrymen saw. Still, the form these efforts took here was so much more determinate, and their influence so much wider, that if we want to study their character generally, our own soil is the proper place.

It is often said that Methodism aimed at, and achieved, a revival of the great principles of the Reformation. In one sense that is certainly true, but unless the sense is carefully defined we may fall into great mistakes. The Unitarianism that made up so large a part of eighteenth-century religious sentiment was, as we have seen, essentially impersonal, even in its best form. Those who felt most deeply that great and wide principles must be asserted about the unity and character of God found no way to connect them with the individual conscience. Unitarianism arose in opposition to Calvinism, as an escape from it, yet seemingly as a consistent deduction from some of its maxims. The more it became a system of denials, the more it was proclaimed as deliverance from that theology’s narrowness. Above all, Calvinism had held that a set of individual believers constituted the Church and were to witness against the world. The Unitarians said there was no warrant for such a protest. An enlightened age was far ahead of those who pretended to separate from it, and the aim of such a world should be to extinguish the idea of an Ecclesia in whatever shape.

In any strong revival of religious feeling, these notions were bound to be attacked first. That is, men’s personal interest in religion would be asserted, along with the distinction between those who felt it and those who were indifferent. Such convictions mark any strong awakening of conscience; they may be said to be the awakening. But the vague phrase “personal interest in religion” cannot long describe the feelings of men who have begun to use it in earnest. One who knows he is a person needs a personal object, and an abstraction cannot satisfy him. So the doctrine that a man is justified by faith and lives by faith in Christ became a principal element in Methodist teaching, as it had been in early Lutheran teaching. The doctrine that individual believers form a peculiar Ecclesia grew out of that. And the Bible was again put forward as the poor man’s book, which he could receive in its simplicity though the learned tried to explain it away.

It is remarkable, though, that the most decided proclamation of these Protestant dogmas came not from Methodism itself but from a reaction among those who had been brought under its influence. The very violent conflicts of the Calvinists under Toplady and Sir Richard Hill against Wesley and Fletcher are abundant evidence. Whitfield, the most powerful Methodist preacher, did join the opponents of his master and early coadjutor. But he was probably led to it by seeing that his preaching stirred feelings rather than giving men a firm resting-place, and he sought a counterweight in Calvinist doctrine. At any rate, though far greater influence was attributed to him in his lifetime than to Wesley, he left a comparatively insignificant body of disciples. So I conceive that the revival of these Protestant doctrines, though perhaps an inseparable accident, was not the essential distinction of Methodism. There was something in it different from the feelings that worked in the Reformers, and even opposite to them, though not incompatible with them.

Everyone must admit that Luther and Calvin directed men much more to the invisible object they are to contemplate, or to the original source of faith in the Will of God. The Methodists directed them much more to the operations of a Divine Spirit on their own minds. The distinction is so obvious that it needs no proof, though it deserves careful notice. It was brought out in those controversies by persons who admitted they had once held the Methodist peculiarity and who showed they could not shake it off even while working to speak another language. The great characteristic of Unitarianism, and of the age steeped in it, was the denial that men could be subjects of spiritual influence. The distinguishing property of the teaching that disturbed and partly subverted the liberal system was the assertion that such influence is real and continually manifested.

There were other peculiarities connected with this. Methodism was not, like Quakerism, the proclamation of a law or power in each man’s own mind. It was expressly addressed to large masses of men. The power was believed to descend on them especially when they met together. Every individual was indeed said to be taken apart and brought into debate with his own conscience, yet it was not denied that the feeling of a united influence greatly heightened each person’s consciousness of it. All that was most fanatical in the Methodists was connected with this belief. It also gave the most plausible, often the most just, ground for saying that the effects of their preaching came wholly from contagion and sympathy and would vanish when the moving cause ceased. Still, we do not reach the meaning of a fact merely by calling it “contagion and sympathy.” Fanaticism, and even conscious quackery, can produce nothing unless they have some true principle to work with. The principle in Methodism may be one that has often been dawning on us in our earlier inquiries, though we have never found a satisfactory development of it. We have often had to ask whether the distinct individual acts Protestantism dwells on so exclusively may not, must not, depend on some relation in which men stand to their fellows. Can we start from individuals and form a society out of them, or is society implied in their existence? And if each man has a spiritual existence and is under a spiritual government, must there not be somewhere a spiritual body of which he is a member? The facts of Methodism may offer few helps toward solving this problem, but they force it on our attention.

Other points of great interest are closely connected with these two. The proclamation of a spiritual power came from men bred in a university reputed to keep more of the old Catholic temper than any other, and whose nickname showed they had been more scrupulous than most in devotion to forms and ordinances. Those who know only the practices the Wesleys later tolerated would hardly believe how much importance they attached, at the outset, to the episcopal ordination of ministers. Yet an impatience of order, even a conviction that it ought to be broken through, can be seen in them from the first. They saw an immense body of souls that had no national position and that the nation took no account of. The upper classes cared little for religious ministrations but could have them if they wished. The middle class, if not especially well affected to the National Church, had provided for themselves in organised and tolerated sects. But the class below, the mob, the canaille as their despisers then called them, the masses as their flatterers call them now, were as little regarded by the churchman who inherited the family living as by the dissenting minister appointed by the tradesmen of the market town. To these the Methodists, like the friars of old, addressed themselves. Like the friars, they awakened thoughts and hopes to which their educated countrymen had long seemed strangers, and in providing for the people’s wants they invaded the privileges of the parochial clergy (both sides would have called them the secular). I do not know how Bishop Lavington argued that the enthusiasm of Methodists and Papists had many points in common. But anyone who considers these facts, or a hundred others connected with the peculiar superstitions each gave currency to, will not doubt that he found plausible arguments. At all events, Methodism had important peculiarities that it did not derive from Protestantism, and with which a pure, exclusive Protestantism can scarcely co-exist.

2. The practical belief in a spiritual operation on men’s minds and hearts constitutes Methodism so far as it is a creed. But once the creed prevailed, a system developed which, as Mr. Southey remarked, is a more complete specimen of organisation than any produced in Europe since Loyola. The more it is examined, the less it seems to have to do with any spiritual principle, and the more clearly it is an invention of human policy. The Wesleyans themselves will scarcely deny this. Though they assert divine inspiration in individuals more strongly than most, they have claimed less than almost anyone that their scheme had a divine origin, and attribute it to the wisdom and sagacity of their founder and his successors. In one respect only does the system resemble what called it into being: the Wesleyans’ spiritual feelings led them to overlook national distinctions, and their system is essentially extra-national. It is an effort to set up a powerful government in the heart of a nation that nowhere touches the nation’s own government. It differs from the old dissenting sects in this: the limits of each of them are fixed by the profession of some peculiar tenet distinguishing it from the others and from the rest of Christendom. The Wesleyans profess no tenet that the National Church does not recognise or tolerate. They exist simply to assert their independence of it, and the importance of such an organisation for converting mankind.

In this respect Wesleyanism is an indication and specimen of religious tendencies that prevail far beyond its own circle. The religious feeling of the last century has given birth to religious societies in which it is hard, at first, to find any likeness to their parent. The first drew men into themselves; the latter throw them altogether out of themselves. The first rested on the acknowledgment of a directly spiritual influence as the only source of moral change in individuals or the world; the latter are built on the most earthly principles and seem to attribute all power to them. The contrast is felt both by the good men of the last age who survive and by the younger men raised under their teaching. The first confess, with some timidity, as if afraid of disparaging the fruits of a tree they believe divinely planted, that the restless bustle of modern religious life is not what they wished to produce. The latter complain more openly, sometimes angrily, that under spiritual words and pretexts a great machinery has grown up among us: complicated, noisy, ineffective, and obeying no law except certain vulgar maxims that suit only trade, if even trade does not demand simpler and nobler principles. Yet the very people who complain confess they do not know how to do without it. There must be some method of combined voluntary action, grounded not on our relations as members of a state but on some higher and more universal relation. Here again we see signs of a Catholic feeling arising from the heart of Protestantism.

3. The religious feelings of our age, in both forms, seem almost incompatible with a positive theology. Men studied the movements of their own minds until these and their endless changes so absorbed them that the idea of the absolute and the permanent was almost lost. They fled from this inward contemplation to an external mechanism which they believed would promote God’s glory. How hard it is, then, not to think meditation on his being and nature an idle occupation for schoolmen, and not to feel wholly alienated from such studies! So most of what has been called theology in Great Britain lately has been an attempt either to systematise individual experiences or to find a general theory of the world’s condition and prospects. Still, the craving for something deeper and larger can never die out. The literature of past ages, which shows that men have entered a more awful sanctuary, was not wholly closed. Gradually, from weariness of merely experimental divinity and mere views about the world, or from a conviction that these cannot stand alone, men have come to ask whether what was once called Theology is a reality or only a dream.

In Germany there are few outward religious excitements, students are students in the strictest sense, and habits of meditation are carefully cultivated. There, those who had felt the evangelical influence, and had been freed by it from the materialism of the last age, soon saw that unless it led to a search for theological principles it would melt away or produce only a succession of fever-fits. So they set about turning the religious feelings in this direction without losing or weakening them. They had seen the mischief done by the dry dogmatism of the Lutheran and Calvinist bodies, and had seen that the human spirit, when strongly stirred, grows impatient of it and escapes by making spiritual motives and consciousness everything. Did this not prove that the affections, where these consciousnesses reside, are the proper and appointed organs of religious belief? May not all religion, so far as it acts from outside, be simply an orderly cultivation of the affections, drawing them out and enabling them to perceive the objects and character that answer their wants and can give them a living, permanent form? The circumstances of the age readily suggested that this is the all-comprehending, all-satisfying idea of Christianity. It was in fact the scientific methodism of the Evangelical feelings. It cleared them of the charge of being incoherent and fanatical and promised an escape from the danger of becoming so. It seemed to justify much in Scripture that philosophical men of the last age had given up, and to make it no longer unsafe to abandon much that religious men had thought indispensable. On this hypothesis it was no strange contradiction that the all-perfect Being should manifest himself in one of their own nature. That was the only form in which he could be an object to their affections, and in which the affections testified they needed him. Symbols that bring this image closer to men’s hearts carry an evidence of truth no abuse can set aside, and they guard us against mere intellectual dogmatism. The history of the divine manifestation might perhaps be open to criticism. But criticism could do no harm by dealing severely with the shell, for the kernel within was something the affections could not part with and would defend against every effort to rob them of it. The criticism of the last age, because it did not admit this principle, was generally false. It threw away as superfluous what the affections felt to be necessary, and perhaps let stand things they protested against. These teachers say that the affections reject, as foreign to themselves, whatever the understanding on other grounds insists must go. “On this principle we are to deal with the Jewish records. They explain how the religious feelings of one nation were awakened, but we must not give them an exclusive dignity or make Christianity depend on them. The affections are in all men, so every history and every theology will contain hints of men’s efforts to find what they needed for this part of their being, and every scheme of philosophy (though philosophy has a distinct province of its own) will have tried to methodise these hints. Christianity must be seen as the announcement to men of what they had been seeking.”

Some of these views may be very offensive to the evangelical school in this country. Yet they have been adopted, by men of deep thought and learning and, I believe, of simple, earnest piety, in the most thoughtful country of Europe. They hold it to be the only theology that someone starting from evangelical premises, and admitting no others as sound, can consistently accept. Nor do I think the history of the doctrine’s progress in the country that gave it birth will show them wrong. But if it is asked whether those who embraced this scheme could in practice stay in it, however well elaborated and fenced by a clear logical understanding and sustaining a devout and honest heart, I imagine the answer is No. They have had to advance or to retreat. Its principle is that the manifestation of Christ is the one great fact of Bible history, to which all others are subordinate, and which we may go on recognising even if we must reject many of the records thought to foretell it, and some parts of the story that contains it. The believer clings to this fact as his deliverance from large Pantheistic notions of the Godhead, and as the warrant and protection of the personal religion he takes to be Protestantism’s special mark. But in following out the plan of distinguishing what men must accept as congenial to their inward feelings and wants from what belongs only to outward form and history, the recognition of a personal object has evaporated altogether. Our Lord’s life only embodies certain great ideas and principles that have been at work in men’s hearts at all times. They probably showed themselves remarkably in him, and may have seemed to his affectionate, credulous, or interested disciples to show themselves in him as they never could in anyone else. But we can contemplate them apart from the accidental form they took in that or any age, as principles belonging to our general humanity. That is one result of the method, as directly opposed to the feelings and intentions of its originator as can well be imagined, and yet seeming a natural deduction from it. On the other hand, many who by their habits of mind and the manner of their initiation into Christianity were most inclined to this theology, and who probably adopted it, have felt more and more that the doctrine of a personal manifestation of God cannot rest merely on individual experience or the felt need of it, however deeply they acknowledge that experience in themselves and wish to produce it in others. It must be sustained by a still more awful truth, and that truth must in some sense have been given to men so that they might enter into it. I apprehend that this faith is growing stronger every day in many serious German minds and must in due time bear important fruits. What those fruits may be I shall not inquire here. At least this fact is established. There is a Catholic tendency in the views on spiritual influence and on outward organisation that seem at first most exclusively Protestant, and equally in the theology traceable most directly to a Protestant origin. Even the system that builds theology on something purely internal still makes the idea of a divine manifestation or incarnation its central idea and connects it with outward symbols. And two opposite schemes that grew up beside it and seem to have developed from it bear clear witness that Protestant Germany cannot be content with a purely Protestant system. It must have Catholicism either as Pantheism or as definite Christianity.

I said that Unitarianism in its last-century form was crushed, though not extinguished, by the Evangelical movement. But it could take another form, and has done so among the descendants of the English Puritan colonists of North America. The coating of dry materialism that seemed inseparable from it has been cast off. Modern Unitarians charge the orthodox systems with disregarding man’s spiritual nature and powers. The idea of a divine humanity in one person is rejected only because it interferes with acknowledging it in every person. These teachers do not pretend that the idea of the Divine Being is anything but mysterious. They make no attempt to refute the old doctrine of Christendom by showing its absurd inconsistency with notions that apply to sensible things. Instead they accuse it of being formal and systematic, drawing accurate distinctions where everything ought to be left vague and indeterminate. Sabellianism has in fact superseded Socinianism.

The modern school’s views of the world, or the age, also differ from their predecessors’. Everything is growing and progressive, and the present age sees further than earlier ones and can afford to reject much they believed. But the age is only to be seen in a few illuminated persons who set themselves against the ordinary feelings and habits of their contemporaries. So the idea of an Ecclesia has come to light in another and remarkable form. The Ecclesia, a very narrow and exclusive one of men of genius and intellect, is assumed to be the true world, of which what is called the world is only a counterfeit. That is modern Unitarianism. The different sects in America feel they must strive earnestly against it, and say, I believe most truly, that it makes as little appeal to the conscience and has as little to do with men’s life and heart as the system it replaced. The wise among them affirm (experience will show whether they are mistaken) that between this Unitarianism and Pantheism there is only an imaginary boundary, which will soon be crossed. Neither assertion checks the system’s progress much. The Americans crave something Catholic, not sectarian, and this system appears to have that merit. It is commonly thought that either Unitarianism or Romanism will overspread America, or that the two will divide it. If there is no Catholicism that is not identical with one of these, I cannot doubt they are right.

Some allusion has been made to discontent among earnest and pious Scotchmen with the Necessitarian system, which has taken in that country the name and reputation of the old Calvinism. There, as in Germany, though for somewhat different reasons, men cannot be content with mere individual feelings or schemes of action; they require a theology. The great solitary principle of Scotch theology is that the Will of God is the original to which everything real in the universe or in man must be referred. As we have seen, it may take two opposite forms. It may assert that there is a sovereign over the world who disposes of all things and persons as he pleases. Or it may assert that there is one from whom all good comes, evil being the contrary of his nature and the resistance to his purpose. As the former became more and more characteristic of the new Calvinism, the latter began to be proclaimed as the only principle consistent with Scripture by a small minority of Scotch divines, whose zeal, love, and often knowledge made up for their weakness in numbers. After a short but violent struggle they were excluded from the Kirk, the doctrine of the Universal Love of God being declared incompatible with its formularies and its existence. But a principle once proclaimed can never be suppressed by any body of men, and that is not the only reason this apparently insignificant demonstration matters to those taking account of the religious tendencies of the age. We have seen how the idea of the incarnation, God’s manifestation in the person of man, presented itself to the Evangelical Germans as the groundwork of religion if there is a religion, and how the question whether the incarnation is a fact or a dream has become the turning point of all theological controversy there. So the Church body created to witness to justification by faith has found it must have a deeper ground, if it is to rest at all. We have also seen that among the American Unitarians, a body that imagined it existed to protest against the possibility of God taking man’s nature, language has become popular which, however vague and unlike the doctrine of a divine humiliation, involves all the difficulty and mystery of that doctrine. Yet neither change seems to me so remarkable as this: Scotchmen, trained from infancy to see the fall of man as the only foundation of divinity, and the incarnation as intelligible only as a deliverance from its effects, have been led to acknowledge the incarnation as the central truth and to show all other truths in its light. This is true without exception of those who protest against the Calvinism of modern Scotland, who all speak of the divine manifestation in Christ as that which constitutes Christianity. It was remarkably true of the Scotch divine best known in this country, for good and for evil, whose thoughts have left traces on many minds that will last long after the body bearing his name is forgotten. The late Mr. Irving was bred in the straitest school of Presbyterianism and kept, I believe to the last, a vehement admiration for Knox and his principles. Yet he came to hold that the doctrine of the Incarnation had been strangely kept out of sight in all Protestant systems, and that it is the centre of all divinity. The deeper mystery of the Trinity is at once its foundation and the truth to whose full knowledge and fruition it is to lead us. In trying to reassert this doctrine he was betrayed, as is well known, into strange and perilous language, which was vehemently attacked and often much misrepresented. A man will inevitably use such language, I believe, if he has not quite given up the notion that the Fall is the law of the universe and is trying to reconcile that Calvinist theory with the Catholic faith. But Irving was not content with merely proclaiming a principle. He felt it must be the foundation of one Catholic Church: if a Church exists, this must be the truth it rests on, and if this is a truth, there must be a Church. No one had taken more pains to proclaim the coming of a future and perfect dispensation. But he found that the mere prospect of a Church did not satisfy the language of Scripture, nor the faith of one who really believed in a divine humanity. That humanity was not a prospect but a reality, attested by facts, so the society built on it must be a fact too. Such a Church, he contended, does not exist; it has been, but has ceased or is about to cease. It must therefore be restored, and only a divine intervention can restore it. There must be a fact embodying the union of God with man, which is the incarnation. There must be an organised body built on that fact. And there must be a manifestation of spiritual power to attest its existence and enable its members to perform their functions. The religious public of England might safely indulge their humour, if such occasions are ever safe for jesting, over the evidence the disciples of this system offered to show they had been constituted the Church of God in the world. But I maintain that this public cannot set at naught the principles that made men desire such evidence and accept almost anything as the answer to their wishes. The three characteristics we have noticed in our modern religious movements are the recognition of a spiritual influence, the demand for an outward organisation, and the craving for a positive theology. Until now they have existed in the greatest contradiction to each other, each in turn putting itself forward as the great necessity and each in turn crushing the others. Here was an attempt to combine them all, in the relation I think we all feel to be the right one. If there is a falsehood in a conception that seems to unite so many elements, all indispensable yet unsociable, and every fact in the history of Irvingism convinces me that there is a great and terrible falsehood, one would think it must lie in the assumption that there is not already a Catholic Church, grounded on a theological truth, possessing a divine organisation, and endowed with the living Spirit, and that it must therefore be founded in the nineteenth century.

Section II.—Philosophical Movements, 1.

Feelings respecting man—Poetry and criticism—Pure metaphysics—Eclecticism.

1. When Wesley and his fellow-labourers were proclaiming Spiritual Power and Influence with such energy, the common belief among philosophical men was that man is a mere creature of flesh and blood. Only in the enlightened coteries of France was this stated in full: there, thinking, hoping and loving were traced to the processes of digestion, or rather to the mass of matter in which those processes go on. Elsewhere people more or less confidently held it as an article of faith that the body contains a thing called a soul, known mainly by negative definitions, which survives death. Even so, the Encyclopædists did not state as a proposition what was really the habit of feeling of their age. The problem the men of that time thought they were set was this: given matter, find out whether there is anything besides it, or whether all things can be reduced to its forms. I find the experiments of those who were willing to argue from these premises profoundly interesting, and their results very satisfying. Among them are Hutcheson’s discovery of a Moral Sense and Butler’s of a Conscience. I have a right to call them discoveries: the facts had once been taken for granted, but had become a terra incognita, one that on the charts then accepted could not exist. Like many great facts in the physical world, they had more weight with the next generation than with the one they were addressed to. Meanwhile it was tacitly accepted that the material is the substantial part of man, and that the spiritual, if it exists at all, is only his accident. This doctrine, though refuted by the conduct and much of the language of the evangelical movement, was a considerable element in their own opinions. They taught that the soul is all in all, that everything must give way to its salvation, that all men have souls, and that most men neglect them. Yet if asked whether they really regarded men generally as spiritual beings, they would not have known what the question meant. “Spiritual beings! No: an unconverted man, one not brought under divine grace, is simply carnal; there is nothing spiritual about him.” They knew this language was in some sense true and denoted something real, and the habits of their time kept them from seeing the contradiction hidden in it. “What is philosophy to us?” they would have said, “we only want to declare the Gospel.” But philosophy was a great deal to them. The materialistic philosophy held their minds unconsciously, and the most extravagant notions and acts charged against them came, I think, from mixing it with the spiritual maxims they drew from Christianity. It seemed so strange to men of the eighteenth century that human beings should show any spiritual feelings or energies that these were almost necessarily seen as startling, and as the effect not merely of divine influence but of a magical one.

The Methodists, however, led other men into a belief they did not hold themselves. They were unconscious, unacknowledged, yet very powerful instruments of a great change in philosophers’ views. It is hard to say when such a change has actually happened, that is, when a principle long met with opposition and ridicule can be treated as the recognised, popular creed. The accepted test is this. While a doctrine is held only in a country like Germany, of recluse students, it does not belong to the age; once it is the common talk and profession of the lecturers and coteries of France, it has established its claim, whatever resistance it still meets from inherited opinions and habits. This test applies especially to the doctrine that man is a spiritual being. The materialism the last age adopted implicitly did not fully develop in that age; the successors of the Encyclopædists carried it further and proclaimed it more confidently. Yet for the last ten or twelve years it has been losing ground every hour in the country that seemed to belong to it, until there is scarcely a subject, even physiology and medicine, where it has not been vehemently assaulted and almost dislodged. So we may fairly assume that a philosophical revolution has occurred, as signal as the one in the middle of the seventeenth century, though of the opposite kind. The change we noticed in the phrases of the Unitarians, and in their whole conception of their system, is an index of it and may help us understand its character and effects. It is not a dry, tame admission that man has an immortal part that may outlast his animal frame. It is a distinct affirmation that the powers and properties within him, which the senses cannot take account of and which cannot be reduced to mechanical conditions, are what constitute him a man; and that the most important part of his history is the history of these powers, of the restraints put on them, and of what they have achieved. A dynamical philosophy has gradually replaced a mechanical one where philosophy is valued in its own right, and, despite the influence of trade, commercial and political, is trying to replace it in England too. By calling it a philosophy I mean something that spreads through ordinary popular literature and has made a language of its own, which will soon, if it has not already, be familiar in clubs and drawing-rooms.

This language will often touch on points religious men have thought belonged only to them. The facts of inner life and consciousness, the religious changes of different periods, the personal conflicts of Christians will be spoken of with deep interest, as far more important than wars, state intrigues and the fall of empires. Men of old will be admired for counting themselves subjects of divine inspiration and acting faithfully on that belief. Ages when faith gave way to infidelity will be denounced as barren and worthless. It will be said that today, as much as ever, men ought to exercise faith and see their talents as marks of a spiritual vocation. Such expressions, mixed as they usually are with others religious men cannot sympathise with, are commonly said to be fraud and insincerity, language wilfully twisted from its ordinary meaning. There may often be such insincerity, and the more fashionable the tone, the more of it. But the charge is dangerous: it is often untrue, and may be thrown back on ourselves. As I see it, the case is this. A considerable number of people in Germany, France and England believe they have found an explanation for most or all of the facts that readers of the Bible attribute to the power and Spirit of God. They believe the last age had no such explanation and that its attempts to interpret or deny these facts were ridiculous. Treat human beings merely as material, mechanical creatures, they say, and you will always be puzzled by what pious people say they have felt and done; treat them as spiritual beings and the difficulty ceases. You can account for everything. Divine interventions, spiritual illuminations and miracles were not mere inventions of priestcraft, though priestcraft has much to do with the continued belief in them; all trace back to man’s spiritual nature, and by observing what theory prevailed on such matters in each age you form a notion of its character and its relation to the next. This language fully satisfies some persons, who can keep these subjects at a distance and speculate about them calmly. Others use phrases very like these, even the same, yet in them they show an entirely different state of mind. These men do not think it their duty to explain facts away or philosophise about them; they cannot look at anything apart from themselves; when they talk of sufferings and conflicts they are not expounding a metaphysical scheme but speaking of what they have known and can therefore sympathise with in others. They cannot adopt the old religious language, because it seems to set aside facts they feel certain of, and to deny that a man is anything in himself, or has an eye with which to receive light. Yet they cannot wholly reject it either. They feel that the philosophical language is no substitute; that the words about gifts and inspirations meant more than that a man has all powers within him; that giving up one’s powers and denying self marks all really honest men; and that humility, not exaltation, acknowledging that one receives rather than boasting that one possesses, should be the test of spiritual excellence. So they hover between the two languages, using either as the weakness or falsehood of the other weighs on them, often committing gross inconsistencies and uttering absurd extravagances, yet proving their honesty more by these than by much that seems coherent and reasonable to their admirers. I believe they put religious men, and especially divines, under an obligation to ask what this perplexity means, and whether their own ways of speaking or thinking, the vagueness of their minds or the faithlessness of their hearts, may have given occasion for it.

Section II.—Philosophical Movements, 2.

2. I attributed much of the good and the evil of the last age to its exclusive study of Natural Philosophy. This pursuit seems tied to certain habits of mind: self-forgetfulness, a sense of something permanent and absolute apart from us, a tendency to overlook the importance of man, and a tendency to treat God as merely another word for nature. Wise men have therefore alternately praised it as the one pure and safe region for the soul and fled from it as dreary and infectious.

In our day eminent individuals and societies have worked hard to assert that this study is superior to all others. They contrast the certainty and steady progress of its conclusions with the insecurity of moral principles, which are either commonplaces obvious to savage and sage alike or else uncertain. They point to its mighty practical results as proof that it is the useful, and so the precious, kind of knowledge. Yet none of this has kindled zeal for it in the young men who best represent the age or are likely to stamp it with their own character. They still give themselves to inquiries said to have no present advantage and no promise of fruit hereafter. The Utilitarian does not accept the natural philosopher’s appeal to usefulness, and says there are more useful studies than that of the stars or of strata. Religious men are begged in vain to believe that the great evidences of God’s existence and character lie in the outward universe, since, as we have seen, they tend to reflect almost wholly on their own feelings. Above all, nature itself has largely been taken away from the natural philosopher. Men have discovered sympathies between the beholder and what he beholds. Trying to express these sympathies, or to investigate the conditions and laws under which they exist, has become the favourite occupation of the more thoughtful and abstracted men of this time, and threatens to become their only one. A few hints about this important revolution are needed here. I cannot do the subject justice, but no one studying the influences at work on himself and his fellows can overlook the tendencies of modern poetry and criticism.

From about the middle of the last century we can trace the beginning of a poetry that referred much more directly and substantively to the outward universe than earlier poetry had. The doings of men, and the songs that celebrated them, had become artificial and conventional. People whom home life had taught to dislike the hollowness of general society, or whose early cultivation made them want something more living and lasting than the fashions of one generation, took refuge in nature. Their simple, sincere utterances were followed by violent fits of rapture over nature’s grander images, with vague and abortive attempts to describe them. Both were signs that a new state of feeling was coming rather than expressions of it. Then European society was shaken by an earthquake. Conventions loosened or dissolved, the links between past and present snapped, and passions smothered or frozen by etiquette broke out. Men of different classes remembered that they had a common humanity and common rights, and the question was earnestly debated: what law are we to obey if old observances and decorums no longer command us? The love of nature became strangely bound up with these movements of the human heart and will, and several forms of poetry showed the connexion. One gave chivalrous legends of other days, letting us feel a bond with them though the institutions they left us might be perishing; nature’s scenes appeared as a gallery of pictures that had lasted for generations, which the heroes and the readers alike had gazed on. A second voiced the laments of those prematurely disgusted with society, or unable to reconcile its demands with their wish for independence; the storms of nature echoed their discontents, and its peaceful scenes were welcomed as the only medicine. A third was the calm utterance of a calm mind seeking the bonds of fellowship between itself and men of every order and degree; nature was plainly a common possession in which lord and peasant could share, and no prescriptions or formalities could shut anyone out. A fourth was more comprehensive, if not deeper: a profound thinker’s effort to find a principle of life and action, expressed in some such words as these: The perfection of a man is to be in harmony with nature.

So we arrive at the result toward which the other experiments were tending. It is of the highest practical importance, and likely to influence the period after one of remarkable poetic genius more than that period itself. But many people find it hard to see how works of imagination became involved with views about human life and action. They suppose that the only moral effect of such works is to create and strengthen good or bad feelings, not to clarify or establish principles. So I must try to trace the steps by which poetry acquired this new character.

In the last age men of letters were commonly divided into two classes: those who followed the vagrant impulses of genius, and those who submitted to rules and forms. The first received a patronising, qualified admiration, but were beacons rather than examples; the second, having less originality, could more safely be followed. Men who were just recovering the sense of strange powers within them naturally reversed this verdict. They asserted the prerogatives of genius, boasted of its chartered libertinism, and denounced forms as inconsistent with it. But that language could not last. When men compared writings that have stood for ages with those that merely flatter their nature and freedom, they saw that the secret of power does not lie in carelessness or vagrancy, that wantonness is allied to weakness, and that genius is marked by owning principles, not despising them. The last age was not too careful in asserting laws to which all art and poetry must conform; it only mistook their character, supposing them to be rules about the outsides of expression and construction rather than forms belonging to thought and mind itself. If there are such principles and forms, then the intuition of them, together with the capacity to work by them, is the very quality of genius, and the way to learn what the artificer knew is to study works of genius in a spirit of submission, not dictation. There may be a purely creative intuition that need not be conscious of the laws it follows, and a critical intuition that discovers them after they have been exhibited in practice and need not be able to embody what it recognises. But the critic is a judge of the poet only so far as he can perceive when the poet has departed from the principles that give his work coherence and harmony. These doctrines seem to carry their own witness of truth, confirmed by the success of those who have used them in criticising great authors and by the feebleness and confusion of their predecessors. They have inevitably led to further questions. What are these laws and forms, and where are they to be sought? Are they laws of nature or laws of the mind? Does the man of genius author them, or merely perceive them and adapt himself to them? It has proved impossible to affirm either answer or to adopt either form of language as sufficient and exclusive. Those who try soon contradict themselves; some unguarded phrase asserts in one sentence what was denied in the last. It seems to follow that the law of the imagination is a law of fellowship or intercommunion with nature. You cannot describe it in any terms that do not imply this, and you cannot go deeper than to say that it creates only so far as it sees, and sees only so far as it can create. Sight and sound are likewise the product of both: neither belongs solely to the eye or the thing beheld, to the ear or the thing heard.

Now suppose we grant, as I think we may, that the clear grasp of this position, and of its many consequences for poetry and the arts, was reserved for our time. As the last age’s characteristic task was to discover the laws of the physical world as it is in itself, so ours has been to investigate the relations in which men stand to that world. Then we need not wonder if this study, like the other, seems to those who have made progress in it all-satisfying, as if it fixed the very ends and conditions of man’s being. Anyone who has thought about the history of astronomy, logic or political economy would not be surprised that a study in some age should claim to be the universal, all-including one. But here I feel there is a stronger reason. I cannot but think that those who have detected this law of the imagination, this law of sympathy and communion between themselves and what is distinct from them, are gripped by a conviction they cannot resist or part with: that some such law of communion is the law of their whole life, that life is an unintelligible blank without it, and that here must be the key to its deepest mysteries. I cannot doubt, either, that they feel robbed and cheated of this truth, and that it is time to assert or recover it. So when I hear people say that harmony with nature or the universe is the great attainment of the wisest and greatest man; when I hear them draw the corollary that the artist or poet is the elect man, the demigod of the world; when I hear it maintained that all religious systems have been attempts to embody a theory of man’s relations with the universe, that forms suited to express those relations in one age become unsuitable in another, and that the gifted man knows when old forms have worn out and new ones must be invented — I believe greater danger lurks in such language than in any phrases ever current. It seems likely to subvert all acknowledgment of fixed, unchangeable truth, and to perpetuate and sanctify the tyranny of modes and fashions against which it pretends to protest. Yet I cannot treat it merely as the quackery of talkers, or the fanaticism of men who have seen one fact and want to explain all facts by it, still less as deliberate wickedness that undermines mankind’s faith under a show of compliment. Such quacks, fanatics and wicked schemers may be plentiful, but they did not invent these expressions; they adopted them as they would have adopted the cant of the last age, which they can now afford to despise. The sincere minds who gave currency to this way of thinking, or to whom it conveys real meaning, show their difference from the rest by their inconsistency. At one moment they proclaim harmony with nature the great object of all men’s strivings; the next they deeply admire those who believed they were sent into the world to fight the inclinations and appetites that tied them to nature, who thought this the characteristic glory of men, and urged others to be men by doing likewise. One while they worship the artist because he submitted to nature, another because he humbled nature to himself and created it afresh. First they speak as if the universe existed so that poets and artists might live and reign, and as if all without their faculty, or the faculty of worshipping them, should be hunted out of it; then they declare that what distinguishes poets and artists is a greater sense of common humanity, and that there ought to be a spiritual commonwealth in which the meanest labourer and serf has a share. Now they interpret all religious systems as imperfect attempts to explain man’s relations with the universe and to embody them in forms; presently they extol some great Reformer or Iconoclast who utterly rejected the parts of the popular system that were links between man and nature, as spurious later outgrowths from a time when men had lost their sense of connexion with a Being above them and so bowed down to images of things below. And they praise him because he reasserted the worth and meaning of those old forms that witnessed to their higher relation.

These are strange inconsistencies, but honourable ones. They show that those who fall into them earnestly and affectionately want to hold what is true, even when it crosses views they regard, or think they regard, as the climax of all past discoveries and revelations. So I cannot help believing there is something in these views that ought to be upheld, and can be upheld, not amid contradictions that make practical application impossible, but together with principles that fix their meaning and prove their reality. We cannot say to these men, Cast aside your faith in bonds between man and the universe; those bonds exist, they have been felt and realised, and the more so the better. Nor can we say, You have an individual soul more precious to you than all these bonds; they will go all lengths with us on that. They were generally bred in a school of pure, exclusive Protestantism, and they believe in this individual soul and all but worship it. Still they feel that there are human bonds, bonds not only for the individual soul but for humanity, that these must be acknowledged as much as the needs of the individual soul, and that the soul itself bears witness to them. But their Protestant teachers told them there are no such bonds between humanity and God; he is connected only with the individual, and all forms signifying a more general relation are unmeaning and obsolete. They have learnt this lesson and believe it as heartily as such a lesson can be believed. Only they also believe that if it is true, humanity must seek happiness in fellowship with something other than God, or rather must make its god or gods out of objects that can have living intercourse with it. The thought may be strangely agonizing: that humanity has after all had nothing firm to rest on or commune with; that the only objects it could admire and love kept changing their aspects and took their beauty from the beholder’s mind; that the idols therefore changed with every period; and that the incalculably few who could discern meaning in what they dealt with have been the real gods, because the god-makers of the universe. It is painful in practice to deny any constant Being who has held the fragments of humanity together, even while asserting that they are bound together, and to treat the idolatry that seems the cause of all its divisions as the only explanation of its unity. Yet even this must be borne, because facts seem to enforce these conclusions, though conscience and reason may revolt. Is it not the fact, these men say, that all Protestant systems, the last and most perfect attempts at a religious system, are crumbling? They swallowed up all earlier forms of faith, and now prove weak and good for nothing. Men have found wants in themselves such systems cannot satisfy. It is idle to pretend these narrow platforms can be a ground for mankind; they are not wide enough for a few individuals to stand on together without quarrelling and kicking. Their very merit lay in their exclusiveness, both of what they admitted and of their objects. A few with particular sympathies on certain points of religion are drawn together in them. But the study of nature, of art, of man, they confess, belongs to another sphere; they may tolerate or prohibit it, but their religion and fellowship have nothing to do with it. If they do try to find a connexion, it is by making these studies dishonest, by forcing them to say what they do not say, and changing their aim from investigating truth to confirming pre-established maxims. This is language we may hear everywhere. I beseech divines, and the men who influence the religious feelings of this age, to ponder it earnestly and to consider what it indicates.

3. Before either of the tendencies I have described had clearly shown itself, the question of the grounds of knowledge, which Locke was thought to have settled, had been put to a new and very rigorous examination. For a long time, here and in Scotland, a ridiculous notion prevailed about the eminent German thinker who carried out this examination. People seriously believed he was swayed by a vagrant, mystical imagination. Even philosophical writers were not ashamed to hint that the wild freaks of some of Germany’s least cultivated or most immature poets gave the British public a fair idea of German metaphysics. It is now well understood that those who encouraged this pious fraud were put off from studying him not by looseness but by the severity of his logic. They missed the vague, popular ways of thinking and speaking that they and their readers were used to. He began his inquiry with no theological bias that would make him hostile to Locke merely because Locke’s system had led to infidel results. He strongly disliked the system an earlier German philosopher had set up against the worship of experience. His conscience told him to reject any customary notion that hindered the pursuit of truth, and his understanding was suited to calm, patient analysis. Those who best understand the tests by which physical facts and laws are established will probably respect most the critical course that led him to give experience a large and important province. To save that province from the ruin its extravagant pretensions threatened, he then showed what region lies beyond it and by what faculty that region can be known. But even those least able to judge these merits must see that his doctrine has endured a storm of ridicule and abuse. It was resisted by the most accomplished intellects in Europe and, more importantly, by all the habits of thought rooted in ordinary men’s minds. It had as many appearances against it as the Copernican doctrine or any other startling one. Yet it has not only held its ground; in one form or another it has forced its way into the speculations of thoughtful men, and it is moulding the language and opinions even of those who least understand it or least wish to admit its truth.

The importance of this fact for theology shows in one circumstance. English writers constantly use the word rationalism as though it named a single set of opinions or one mode of thought. But the rationalism of the last century and the rationalism of this one are widely different: the one grew up under Locke’s patronage, the other comes from Kant’s influence. The former may take the mild, feeble shape our critics gave it last century, or the destructive character that frightens us in some of the now obsolete German neologians. Either way it comes from a wish to be rid of facts that clash with the ordinary notions and experience of mankind, and to make revelation the announcement of moral notions and axioms which men, by their constitution, must receive from outside. The latter underrates facts because they belong only to the region of experience. It holds that naked principles, which alone matter most and alone concern the reason, are not imparted to the reason but contained in it. So the results of the two systems may often coincide, but the habits of thought that produce them are so opposed that anyone who mistakes one for the other is likely to misunderstand the workings of his neighbour’s mind, if not his own.

It may be objected: “Surely whatever the difference, it favours the older opinion. That assumed a revelation was real and necessary, only confining its use within narrow limits; this dispenses with revelation, perhaps denies its possibility altogether.” I am not anxious to disprove this, and I may feel its force as strongly as those who use it as a denunciation or a warning. But before I can value it much, I must be sure it does not disturb the faith of those it condemns or counsels in a truth. I must be sure this truth is not one God would have people of this age especially receive and hold fast. And I must be sure it is not one that, thoroughly understood and heartily embraced, may do much to recover the principles and assert the facts it seems to set at nought.

I would ask anyone to reflect calmly on the circumstances of the country where this philosophical revolution took place. There Luther had asserted that certain truths are so necessary to the life and being of man that simply proclaiming them, “the foolishness of preaching”, would bring conviction to countless hearts. They were proclaimed in the words of a book, because that book gave the simplest, most genuine, most vital exhibition of them. That was the first act of the Reformation. In the second, the curtain rises on Luther’s disciples poring over this book, often in vain, hoping to extract some meaning from it, and then fighting one another to defend the fragment of meaning each had found. Then another generation is busy at something different: tearing page after page out of the book because it speaks of unintelligible matters that reasonable men need not care about, while still defending the parts consistent with the general verdict of nature and experience. Then a man arises who asks what this general verdict is. He takes apart all the demonstrations by which men had fancied they could make out the importance of morality, the immortality of the soul, and the being of a God. “All these,” he says, “are good for nothing; they establish no conclusion, because the conclusion is assumed in the premises.” But he adds that this is no reason to doubt these truths. If they are fundamental truths, they must be the premises of every demonstration, not its results. The greatest witness for these elements of human faith is that everything seems to prove them, because in fact nothing can be proved without them. This argument from outside is then clinched by a corresponding one from within. “You say it cannot be, because no faculty takes cognizance of such primary truths. I say there is and must be such a faculty. Otherwise mathematical science, on which the demonstration of physical facts rests, would be just as impossible as moral science. And this faculty is not merely one of the faculties of humanity; it is precisely the human faculty. It belongs not to an individual as such but to each man as a man, a member of a race. It deals with what is universal as well as what is necessary.”

Such language, as far as it is understood and believed, must displace a whole host of notions and conclusions previously held sacred, not because they were old but because they were new, having seemed the last and most perfect effort of the human intellect to repeal the decrees of former times. So the doctrine took on something of a destructive character. It was not really destructive even of the positive maxims of the school of experience. It clearly ratified Locke’s doctrine that all notions and conceptions result from sensible experience, and that sense impressions precede all generalisations (such as “the whole is greater than its part”) in time. It merely affirmed principles, whenever they are discovered to the mind, that are presumed in the existence of the mind itself. Without them the mind could form no notions, conceptions or generalisations, and could not even receive impressions. Still, the persons who adopted the system felt that they had found something that set aside the favourite theories of their immediate predecessors. And had it not set aside, even more completely, the theories of the thinkers before them, whom those predecessors had overcome? Had it not proved to be involved in man’s very constitution what had been supposed merely delivered to him, and delivered as if essentially at odds with his constitution, so that he could receive it only by miracle? The Encyclopædists talked nonsense when they tried to say what Reason was and claimed that what they called Reason was the judge of what is true. But they were right that reason is the judge of what is truth, and that truth and reason cannot exist apart from each other.

I would ask for patient consideration of the earlier difficulties of the men who adopted this language, of the temper of mind they inherited even when they thought they had thrown it off, and of the temptations to pride that come with a sense of new discovery. This should come before such phrases are assumed to carry all the mischief I readily admit may lurk in them. Above all I ask my reader to reflect on a fact I have already had to present in several other forms. The Protestant system encourages every demand of the human mind for satisfaction, yet provides nothing to satisfy the demand for truths or principles that belong to us not as individuals but as members of a race. Protestants say each man must realise every truth for himself, and that when enough individuals have become conscious of the same truth they should meet and have fellowship in professing it. They have never effectively taught that there are truths belonging to men as men, which do not depend for their reality on our consciousness of them but are the grounds on which that consciousness rests. The doctrines of Protestantism, I believe, necessarily imply this, but they do not distinctly affirm it; they refer formally to men as individuals. The systems of Protestantism not only fail to affirm it but in practice deny it.

So I think this metaphysical revolution points the same way as the other changes in men’s feelings I have noted: toward a demand for something Catholic, and for it not as an accident or addition to the faith we hold as individuals but as its very groundwork. Suppose I am asked how a doctrine that seems to set aside all revelation and tradition, and to claim for human reason a more direct independence than any other has ever claimed, may yet be leading, by God’s gracious guidance, to the assertion and confirmation of the principles Christians refer to revelation or tradition. Those principles are said, and I think rightly, to humble man’s reason. My answer is the kind of conflicts that Kant’s doctrine has stirred up among philosophical men.

Almost from the time its meaning began to be earnestly debated, three great difficulties, or at least blanks, were found in it. First, some complained that it was hard and dry, a doctrine with which a man who had a heart could have no sympathy. Its highest affirmations were: God is, immortality is, freedom is. These great primary truths of the reason lay there with no power to address or connect themselves with any form or feeling. They were the Pillars of Hercules of the intellect, or the premises of a demonstration, and nothing more. This objection probably led to the division between the objects of philosophy and religion that I noticed in the last section, with religion assigned whatever concerns our feelings and sympathies. Others, who found this distinction artificial and unworkable, laboured to build a philosophy with the warmth and cheerfulness of a religion.

Next came a feeling that in a Protestant country was bound to force itself on many minds. The reason speaks of all these great and eternal verities, but what have I to do with them? What link is there between my personal consciousness and these grand, universal affirmations? Tell us this, they said, or your scheme, however well fenced with demonstration, cannot satisfy a man.

Lastly, there seemed to be a fatal contradiction, if not in the principle itself, then between the principle and the inferences instantly drawn from it. There is an organ in man that speaks of what is absolute and eternal. You believe this organ, call it reason or what you will, is distinct from the one that merely forms notions and affirms propositions. But how is it distinct? If it only affirms “There is something absolute; there is something eternal,” these are propositions, and to suppose so destroys your own doctrine. If that is not the reason’s witness concerning the absolute, what must it be? It must affirm the existence of the absolute not as the intellect affirms a proposition but as the eye affirms an object. As an object, it must be distinct from the one who beholds it, anterior to it, that without which it could not be. Suppose the Universe is the great, eternal, absolute thing we feel it must be. Then this Universe spoke to us first; we did not form it or even discover it, but it revealed itself to us. But it is the eye or the imagination that demands an external universe; the Reason must demand something different. Does it not, on your own showing, demand something homogeneous with itself, an absolute Reason? And if there is such an absolute Reason, a real Being to whom the reason in man looks up, is it more consistent to believe that the reason found him out or that he revealed himself to the reason?

By this last statement, the doctrine that there are principles prior to experience, which the reason of man knows, makes revelation unnecessary only when it contradicts itself. And there is more. If this view is right, the revelation the reason demands cannot be merely of moral principles or axioms; it must be the revelation of a living Being. It cannot be one in which events are mere accidents, separable from some idea that tried to embody itself in them. Facts may be only the drapery of doctrines, but they seem the only possible way of manifesting the Being, the essential Reason. By hypothesis this Being transcends space and time. This one faculty in man can behold what is not under those conditions, while all the others are subject to them. So it would be neither strange nor contradictory if the facts embodying the revelation presented him at once to all our faculties and let the highest one use its own prerogative of looking through them. In this way one might find a hope of reconciling the law of the affections with the law of the reason, without separating them into two departments as if a scholastic boundary could keep them apart. One might also dream of a way in which each individual’s consciousness would be called forth, through his sense of relationship to the Being who was revealing himself and condescending to his needs. But whether or not this is so, it seems clear that this new rationalism cannot be satisfied with itself. It will become irrational if it cannot find something to unite with it. Followed out fairly, it implies the following. Something must originally have been given to the reason. This gift must be some truth that is transcendent and divine. It must proceed from, and refer to, a living Being, and it must concern all men as men. The best test that it concerns them and is truly necessary to humanity’s constitution is that men received and believed it on the bare announcement of it. And in every later stage of human history it must have been doubted, contradicted and ridiculed, yet kept its ground. It must have proved itself, in the most advanced civilisation as in the simplest, to be what men need as the sign and bond of their fellowship.

Section II.—Philosophical Movements, 4.

4. One more feature of the philosophical face of our age must be noted, or our picture of it will be incomplete. The effort to bring all systems of thought into harmony, or to build a system to which each contributes certain elements, has appeared in many periods. But it was never so strong as it is now, not even just after Christianity was first proclaimed. Nor can we measure its strength simply by counting how many conspicuous men, from the nation that best represents the pressure of the time, have felt and indulged it. Everyone can probably find it working in himself. Plan after plan of union may have failed. Some awkward performer may have insisted, with cruel stubbornness, on playing his solo regardless of the order imposed on the rest. Or when we got rid of the discords, the music may have turned so flat that no one cared to listen. Yet through all discouragement, despite the just ridicule of others and the sadder scorn that the weary peacemaker (Irenicus) turns on himself, the wish continues. A secret spring of hope sustains it: an unconquerable conviction that the dream was a true one, however plainly practice seems to contradict it.

This temper, as we have seen, appears even among religious men, where it might least be expected, and who would abhor any system merely stitched together from earlier speculations and heresies. Nor is it strictly true that the philosophers of our day set this as their aim. The name Eclecticism, which they readily accept, suggests a dried collection of flowers from every soil, arranged to the collector’s taste. If they spoke for themselves, they would describe it very differently. They would say that their will has nothing to do with it, and that Eclecticism is a necessity of the age. One partial theory has displaced another in earlier periods; none can now be adopted as adequate and self-sufficient; all are seeking a larger, more universal scheme in which to merge. The difference from the last century is made more striking by one point of likeness. The disciples of Cousin revere the age they were born into as the disciples of Voltaire revered theirs. But the earlier school believed it was created to destroy every earlier form of opinion; the later believes it is meant to sanction all of them and to find the principle that crowns them. The first effect of this difference is an opposite estimate of history. The French school of the last century slighted it as a record of absurdities to be ridiculed and avoided. The French school of this century treats it as the key to all knowledge and studies past acts with reverence as well as diligence. Such a habit must bring its reward, and these philosophers have thrown valuable light on the meaning and succession of events, especially in the history of modern Europe.

They have thrown it on events, but, I suspect, very little on the feelings or character of men. Even their greatest admirers commonly complain that they cannot understand a living human being except as a link in a chain of operations. They do not wish to set aside free agency, and their theory would rather lead them to honour it. But they can look at it only in relation to a theory, which is nearly the same as dispensing with it. This is a most unfortunate peculiarity in men who hope to harmonise the different thoughts and feelings of our time. Set aside religious opinions and habits, as too vagrant and fanatical for an eclectic experiment. Set aside political questions and the struggles of individuals and classes for recognition in the state. Even so, the philosophical dispositions of our day are awkward, refractory material. The strongest, I observed, was to acknowledge a spirit in man and to view all his other concerns in reference to it. Then there were men full of living sympathy with nature and longing for forms to express it. No theory, however it allows for these two inclinations, can really satisfy them. So the Eclectic theory must confine itself to what I called pure metaphysics: the adjustment of the doctrine of reason, and of truths prior to experience, with earlier speculations. Even this limited application meets difficulties. First, the rationalist will say the work is already done: the critical philosophy distinguished the provinces of reason and experience, and to eclecticise on that is to gild refined gold or paint the lily. The second objection is more fatal. Reason, as I have already remarked, speaks of an actual Being, an absolute Reason. Any attempt to make it merely utter a proposition about that Being destroys its nature as the rationalist explains it. So no scheme of Ontology, however complete, can replace or include the truth he proclaims. It may be sound and valuable, but it will be a theory concerning something, and he is trying to bear witness to that something itself.

Still, no argument will persuade people today that reconciliation between warring opinions and feelings is impossible, or that it will not eventually come. Individuals and governments will keep suggesting and trying compromises that suppress truth. Those succeed only while men feel and care about nothing, and are laughed away the moment an energetic man or thought arises. Strange as it sounds, they are on the whole more hopeless now than in any earlier day. Yet they will be tried, with a desperate conviction that one must at last prosper, and with infinite rage and astonishment that this one fares no better than its ten thousand predecessors. There is certainly great need for the philosophical feelings I have described as belonging to this time to find a meeting point, since though equally strong and seemingly from one source, they often clash. The assertor of man’s spiritual powers exalts the hero who fights circumstances and triumphs over them. The artistic philosopher delights in the man who adapts himself to circumstances. Whatever fellowship they have rests on the one’s pleasure in a curious, puzzling specimen of human nature, and the other’s awe before a man who has reached a state he knows he can never reach and with which he half admits he has no sympathy. Both again dislike the metaphysician, one because he seems to bind human energy and freedom by fixed absolute laws, the other because he sets a dry truth against all forms and images. If all these tendencies are sound and true, as I have maintained, there ought to be a method of harmonising them that keeps each in its strength, that not merely accounts for them but embodies them and lets them bear real fruit. But would it not be at least possible, if reason affirms a truth that must always have been, if communion with nature is implied in our constitution and so in that of men who lived a thousand years ago, and if humanity is essentially spiritual, that the reconciling method already exists? The work of our age may then be not to create it afresh, but to discover its meaning and realize its necessity.

Section III.—Political Movements, 1.

American Revolution—French Revolution—Individual Rights—Individual Will—Schemes of Universal Society—Education—Power of the State.

I have spoken of the theological temper of the United States of North America. It illustrates one stage in the history of Protestantism, and it shows a desire for something Protestantism does not supply. But the political change that came when these states revolted from the mother country is, from the same point of view, even more important.

Among the leading characteristics of the Reformation I noted an eagerness to assert the rights of national sovereigns, and with them the separate position of each nation. I said this was closely bound up with the sense of personal distinctness in each man, which is the mainspring of Protestantism. Once the Protestant systems had developed, these inseparable twins grew impatient of each other’s company. The reformed monarchs found that each man’s right to act and think for himself cut awkwardly across their authority and did nothing for the unity of their nations. They saw that strong religious feeling made everything else subordinate to itself, so unless it could be made to serve their government’s aims, it would thwart them. They saw two expedients: to force religious feeling into agreement, or to weaken it as far as possible. The first was tried and failed; the second worked better for a time. The religious bodies responded in kind. They grew more jealous of the sovereign’s interference and resisted it in times of excitement. But those times made their terms of communion stricter, so they became less identical with the nation. When peace returned it was easy to believe that they had nothing to do with national affairs, that their business was wholly religious and the monarch’s wholly secular. Reformers adopted this opinion only very slowly. The Lutherans thought, and still think, State tyranny more tolerable than abandoning the Reformation principles. The Calvinists in their palmy days resolved that if the State could not be religious with a sovereign, it should be religious without one. The Scotch Covenant declared the State essentially theocratic. The whole effort of our civil wars was to establish the same principle, and in one strange interlude the Scotch tried to create a Presbyterian theocracy in the person of Charles the Second. Only when these schemes were disappointed did the modern doctrine, in its various forms, begin to prevail. Meanwhile an experiment was to be made: if religious men could not influence the old societies of Europe, might they not frame societies for themselves in another world?

The legislation and government of the Puritan colonies bore every mark of their origin. They were, if the term may be forgiven, sect-commonwealths, held together by their religious peculiarities more than by a common language, a common origin, or subjection to a distant sovereign. Before that last tie was snapped, the colonies had become important trading communities. The settlers’ religious feeling had lost much of its strength but had left industrious habits, clear understanding in common matters, indifference to physical or intellectual refinements, and a useful pertinacity. The heroes of the revolution were made of these elements. They resembled the Puritans in these qualities, but their notions of government and society had no spiritual principle and referred wholly to this world. The Declaration of Independence shows the change. In it the old Protestant feeling, that each man is a distinct being with distinct rights, is curiously mixed with a vague notion of general fellowship then gaining currency in Europe, which was more a reaction against Protestantism than its natural result. The ultimate consequence was the union of the independent states. Future history will decide whether that union came about by natural fusion or only by the decrees and contrivances of legislators.

These events certainly signalled that a strife of principles was at hand, though its scene was not to be the land of Franklin and Washington. It was in an old-world country, where the Protestant doctrine had been stifled two centuries earlier and society had been everything and human beings almost nothing, that the most vehement declaration of individual rights would be made. There the death-struggle began between the impulses that lead each person to maintain his rights and those that lead him to seek communion with his fellows.

It has been truly and profoundly observed that the French Revolution could not have come about through the philosophers’ scepticism, the sins of civil and ecclesiastical rulers, or the people’s starvation, nor through all these together. An element of faith was needed to mix with and contradict the scepticism, to create moral indignation against the sin, and to turn hunger from a dead anguish into a living passion.

The Parisian philosophy of the eighteenth century was little more than cleverer and bolder men expressing a feeling that pervaded all society. The teachers only made their disciples conscious of the unbelief already possessing them. Their wit was irresistible because it exposed contradictions in the very persons addressed. Where such contradictions are painful and the conscience still says, “This which you are not you are meant to be,” this wit is most torturing. The mind may feel an awful delight in it, as in a just penance, but no grave admonition is half so bitter. Where the conscience is not awake, or only awake enough to see that hypocrisy is dishonourable, the wit is received very differently. The idle upper-class lounger, convinced that everything will go on as it always has, that words do no harm, and that his position entitles him to see further than a clown or shopkeeper, listens gladly. The doctrine suits his practice and lets him dismiss any lingering sense of responsibility as absurd. The active, aspiring middle-class man, who thinks himself unfairly depressed, sees that society’s habits are false, and knows it is supported by reverence and awe tied to invisible principles, eagerly welcomes the discovery that no such principles exist. Then a system that is outwardly hollow, and that hinders his ambition, may gradually fall apart. So this philosophy took hold of both the Paris soirées and the enterprising provincial lawyer, for opposite reasons. But nothing in it could appeal to poor men who were actually suffering. Many philosophers were economists and could discourse on why bread was dear and how industry might pay better. But hungry men hate abstractions, and most of all those that refer to their hunger without relieving it. In many districts, moreover, the doctrines the wise men mocked were tied to the friendly faces of curés who had suffered with their flocks, and to actual bread at the convent doors. Even if the poor did not believe those doctrines as realities, the association remained.

Such relics of religious association and sympathy must have been far thinner among the mechanics of the capital. The habits of the classes above them will have descended on them, and the citizen’s quicker wit will have the more quickly detected the falsehood and hypocrisy, so much more glaring, of his instructors. Still, what in the teaching of a man like Helvetius could have given even this class the least pleasure? Sir James Mackintosh calls the Helvetian philosophy the philosophy of the pot-house. But the frequenter of a pot-house would scarcely have cared to hear that a man, apart from society and the help of legislators, soirées, and tailors, is good for nothing. It would not have helped to add that these had managed their affairs badly through priestly imposture, that philosophy must reform them, and that many now proscribed might then enter the charmed circle of civilisation. The poor man must have felt that whatever good might come to him hereafter, for now he was outside the horizon of the philosopher’s telescope.

How different it was when a voice from Switzerland proclaimed that each man has in himself, apart from all social institutions and civilisation, rights and power. He may claim those rights and put forth that power. He must, if he would break the bonds that legislators, tailors, and soirées have fastened on him and form a society in accordance with nature and truth. This appeal went straight to the hearts of men who had nothing of their own except their limbs and countenance and whatever, known or unknown, moved those limbs and gave life to those faces. It appealed to the sense of strength, of wrong, and of suffering, which is extinct in no one. It called into action what the philosophical systematisers mostly denied or would have preferred not to exist. It joined with the notions about the frauds and tyrannies of priests and lawyers which the unsentimental school had spread. It turned to its own use the materialist notions of the age that governments originate in compacts and conventions. Finally, it forced the sages to admit that the government of reason must begin in outbreaks of popular fury, and to join the people in founding society on a declaration of individual rights.

The allies soon became enemies. The philosophers could do nothing with their theories, and the poorer men tried other weapons. The onlookers, terrified, began to ask whether the notions they had adopted as the highest discoveries of the enlightened intellect had been falsely deduced. Could law and government really have been born as the eighteenth-century teachers supposed? Must they not have had a higher source? Was it not necessary to believe that some mysterious power upheld them? These thoughts, stirring especially in the Protestant nations, prepared men to listen to Burke. He told them, as one who knew, that law rests on deep invisible principles, not on philosophical maxims; that it is to be feared and reverenced as above us, not treated as our creature and servant; and that if its existence and awful derivation are trifled with or denied, it will prove its power and take its revenge. This teaching, so unlike what the last age was used to, was received by many wondering nobles and ecclesiastics as a new truth given to defend their houses and lands. Others welcomed it more warmly and thankfully, as the application to new circumstances of a doctrine familiar to all great thinkers and delivered with peculiar power by the noblest English writers. Many now confess how much Burke, an adventurer, an Irishman, a philosopher, did to restore the tone of English feeling among men of action and of meditation, in the upper and middle classes. His influence was not confined to this country. The deep historical researches of Niebuhr and the jurisprudential wisdom of Savigny, if not called forth by his writings, took their direction largely from him. They would have found no readers able to appreciate them if our statesman and orator had not first prepared the soil.

The French Revolution has thus led many thoughtful persons, and many who are not, to the conviction that the doctrine behind the declaration of rights is essentially false. A man who chooses independence, chooses to be an individual, chooses the state of nature, can have no claims on his neighbour. To build a fellowship on independence is a monstrous contradiction, which shows itself the moment it is tried. And law, as the appointed corrector and judge of man, must derive from and rest on sanctions men regard as superhuman. But not a few, without directly opposing these doctrines, and perhaps agreeing with them as answers to Rousseau, draw from the same facts inferences most unlike them, almost incompatible with them. ‘Whatever is said of the majesty and transcendent character of law,’ they say, ‘men plainly did set themselves above law during the Revolution and showed they could defy it. The popular will proved that all the terrors of law, affirmed and made more fearful by religion, could not bind it. When at last it gave way, it was not to law but to the will of one man, who showed that there was that in him which all the units of the nation together could not resist. Afterwards the political machine seemed to run back into its old ruts, and tradition and custom seemed to resume their sway. But the same truth was established again: all such influences prove ineffectual. A will, a despotical will, is wanted somewhere, and to this alone will men really bow. Whether or not individual men have a right, there is—(I borrow the favourite phrase of a writer who has shown this most clearly, and who turned the history of the Revolution from an abstraction into a living reality)—“a might,” and this might will make itself felt, either in a whole nation or in some single person who compels the nation to acknowledge that he is meant to govern it.’

Put this way, one might fancy Hobbes speaking again in the nineteenth century. But whatever the resemblance in words, the feeling behind them is the opposite of the hard materialist’s: it is reverence for spiritual force. The triumphant despot is not obeyed because men find it more convenient not to fight, or find one ruler less perplexing than many. They do him homage because he has the highest title and the most perfect ordination, because he was truly created to be their king. So this is another, and I think a more advanced and reasonable, form of reverence for will as superior to the forms of government and society, the reverence that led many to regard rule and subjection as hateful inventions of priests and monarchs. The writer of “Prometheus Unbound” and the “Revolt of Islam” preached man’s freedom from all outward forms and restraints. Those who say subjection is a necessity of man’s being, that he longs to be governed, are equally sure that he can submit only to the dominion of a man, never to the authority of an outward rule. Both differ from the sentimental teachers of the last age, who told men to follow their natures and give their good feelings and impulses fair play. Both hold that a man must not yield to inclination but must win a victory over his nature, or else he can be neither free himself nor lord over his fellows.

Meanwhile these notions, which in this form might be dismissed as idle dreams, are forcing themselves in another form on all practical politicians. Not only in quiet chambers but by fierce mobs the doctrine is proclaimed that Will is superior to Law, that it ought to be, and that it has the power to unmake and remake what pretends to hold it down. Anyone who attends to the phrases now current will, I think, find them far more tremendous than those heard at the start of the French Revolution. “We have a right” acknowledges some antecedent principle. In our day this language, when we hear it, translates at once into “We will.” That is the ground of the right, and it aspires to be the ground of all things.

‘And why has it not yet attained its aspiration, and what can hinder it?’ asks the terrified statesman. When that question is raised, he finds politics has become an awful science. A man who confines himself to official rules and precedents, and admits nothing transcendent as his concern, can scarcely pursue it successfully. However he may laugh at metaphysics and scorn theology, he must discuss a subject touching the deepest principles of both: by what means the existence of law can be reconciled with the existence of the human will. The debate between the disciple of Rousseau and the disciple of Burke brought out the old controversy: is the nature of man good and to be trusted, as Rousseau affirms, or evil, as the Reformers said, to be kept down and triumphed over by every good man? After the French Revolution, politicians decided this in favour of the ancient opinion. But now another old scholastic controversy is brought to the same practical issue and the same adjudication: is Man, as the successors of the Reformers have affirmed, to be identified with the nature attached to him? Or, when he sinks under that nature’s dominion, is he abandoning his proper state and subjecting himself to that over which he was meant to rule? If it is decided in favour of the first notion, that of modern Calvinism, the politician must invent what charms he can to lull that will to sleep, the will that has often been bound with fetters and chains and has torn the chains apart and broken the fetters, that no one has tamed, and that dwells continually in the mountains and the tombs, crying out and cutting itself with stones. If instead he admits that there is a will or spirit in man, safe and free only when it finds another will to govern it, and that in its vain effort at independence it becomes the slave of its own natural inclinations, then there is no contradiction in supposing two things. Law is meant to overawe those inclinations; and there is a spiritual government in which the man himself has a claim of citizenship, and in which he may find his rightful king.

Section III.—Political Movements, 2.

2. This hint leads to another aspect of the French Revolution, its properly political one. It began with a declaration of individual rights, but it claimed to build a society on that declaration, and the society was to be universal. The proceedings were thoroughly French from first to last. French patriotism surged up against the invasion of the Allies, and a wish for French predominance can be traced in the nation’s counsels from the start of the war to Napoleon’s abdication. But I am speaking of the real principle that governed the minds of those who acted, not the nominal principles of parchment documents and pompous phrases. That real principle alone saved those documents and phrases from being wholly meaningless. It was the substitution of a universal polity for national polities. Every monstrous absurdity of its speakers, writers, and actors showed this feeling. All its achievements, whether as a republic or as concentrated in one man, tended the same way. Even the constitutions proposed one after another for France had no more to do with France than with Kamschatka. They were built on universal principles and meant for mankind.

These illustrations showed how valuable particular governments are when they are framed on general maxims. They roused the thoughtful men of Europe to study national history, and the inner life by which nations keep their identity for generations through every external change. These investigations drew light and strength from the national feelings that French propagandism and the tyranny of the universal empire called forth. Men could no longer take the histories of Voltaire, Hume, and Robertson as representing the mind of past generations. Children came to feel a bond with their fathers: they were heirs of the same soil, and had been left names, memorials, and institutions more lasting than its oaks to keep.

But this patriotic temper hardly outlasted the war in Germany and the Peninsula. The historical inquiries it had inspired were not neglected, and were valued more as their wisdom and learning became known. Yet they influenced the popular mind only by supplying armour against some of its most prevalent tendencies. Despite the solemn warnings of those who made these inquiries, schemes of universal society have been most rife among us. They pass over differences of race and language as insignificant, ridicule custom and reverence for ancestors, and annul the old constitutions that embody them. It may be well to notice two or three of them.

Philosophy in Europe has been changing, perceptibly only in recent years but since at least the beginning of this century. So it may seem surprising that Mr. Bentham, a philosopher of the last generation, formed in the school of Locke and Hobbes and aiming to put their maxims into practice, should have influenced so many people of our day. It would be hard to explain if his influence had lasted and overcome its opponents. But I think it is not impossible to see why it was felt for a time, in his own country and abroad. He came into notice after the great Rousseau experiment had been made and had led to the consequences I have described. No one could deny that Burke and the Constitutionalists had gained much for their argument from it. They seemed right and the defenders of popular government wrong. Still, there was a restless feeling that the trial might have been conducted differently and then have answered its authors’ wishes. Besides, the countries of Europe, and soon the Spanish colonies of America, were left so stripped by revolution that they seemed to need some new constitution. Mr. Bentham met these feelings. He told men that the Rousseau scheme was false in its very conception. Before society exists we have no rights, and apart from combination we are good for nothing. The end of any combination is the greatest happiness of the greatest number of the units who enter it. The combination is held together by its members’ mutual suspicion, and by contrivances that dispose the governing body not to violate the interests of those whose affairs it administers. With these few, comprehensible rules, he applied a very acute and painstaking intellect, first to constructing a scheme of government and legislation to fit his theory, and second to exposing in detail the contradictions and absurdities in the practice of different nations, especially his own. Young men ashamed of being thought sentimental, yet longing for the glorious commonwealth the sentimental school had promised, found it consoling to see a new society built on logic. Their elders could more easily grasp, even if unwilling to admit, the justice of his practical charge. In ordinary acts and talk they admitted no principle but his, yet they maintained institutions that principle could not justify. They were thus applying the doctrine of self-interest where it served their ends and repudiating it where it inconveniently interfered. These facts could not be denied. If Mr. Bentham could have made his system seem to concern only law and government, he would for some time have kept his disciples and kept gaining more. After the shock of the Revolution and the war, men inclined to occupy themselves with the workings of their own minds lost interest in politics and decried them as belonging to a merely outward region. So they could readily let almost any political system gain currency. But they soon found that a compact, organized political scheme must involve the questions they thought alone sacred. Social Utilitarianism could not be separated from individual Utilitarianism, nor could an ultimate end acknowledged in one region of thought be flatly denied in another. This became apparent both to the most real minds, impatient of artificial boundaries, and to the strictly logical. Poets found that their art could be defended only as an amusement, on the same grounds as cards or horse-racing. Religious men, however reluctant to relate such topics, were driven to ask whether the doctrine of Paley and Bentham could be reconciled with the Sermon on the Mount, and if not, which was to be given up. The school was thus unexpectedly assailed by all the new and strange thoughts on literature, metaphysics, and the spiritual universe that had been working themselves out in minds across Europe while Mr. Bentham was busy in his study with the rationale of evidence. Having nothing to answer with, it could only sound a retreat. At whatever risk of inconsistency, theoretical or practical, it tried to defend its philosophy by confining it within narrow limits. Even there it has no safe dwelling. Man’s desire for a universal polity grows stronger daily and is bound ever more to deep feelings and passions, ever less to the mere calculating understanding. Yet the Benthamites appeal only to that understanding, and by it would fashion the whole scheme of human life and of the universe. Their right thing is that the will of the majority be omnipotent. But what calculus have they found for measuring the strength in that word will, or for knowing what becomes of all their theories and axioms of legislation once it has won supremacy?

Far more profound in conception, and I think more interesting in detail, was the system proclaimed in France about twelve years ago under the name of St. Simonianism. The Benthamites discarded all national distinctness, or treated it as accident. But one circumstance of man’s condition could not be wholly accidental or the fruit of bad legislation: men exist in families. It seems that the most fortunate societies have recognized family life most and defined its limits most accurately. The Benthamites took no notice of this and built a society on the hypothesis that mankind is an aggregate of individual atoms. The St. Simonians saw at once that this was a practical delusion. As long as men’s notions are what they are, and the morality that sustains and is sustained by them lasts, men will keep acting, speaking, and voting by families, not by heads. So the aristocratic idea intrudes, and perfect democratic fellowship is impossible. I respect the feeling, whatever its cause, that kept the English Utilitarians from looking boldly into the gulf this thought opens. I am glad they dared not sacrifice their moral impulses to logical consistency, and I can see how they persuaded themselves they were consistent, since it was not for the greatest happiness of the greatest number that family ties should be sacrificed. Yet I must maintain that if a universal society is to be built, on the Utilitarian maxim or on the St. Simonian maxim, “each according to his capacity,” it is an indispensable first step to extinguish domestic feelings, associations, and sympathies, all the laws that uphold them, and all acknowledgment of relationship as a significant fact. The deepest mind ever lodged in a mortal, searching with the noblest and purest aims for the foundation of universal society, could find no escape from this conclusion. Every new project for actually establishing such a society has supplied fresh evidence that these must be its conditions if human hands are to build it.

But the St. Simonians felt that a universal society, even of the kind they imagined, even a universal bank, could not be established by mere human hands. This was another sign of the deeper wisdom at work amid their extravagances and contradictions. As they repeated “each according to his capacity,” they must have asked: where shall we find the judge of capacities? Where dwells the seeing eye that will perceive in each the mighty power in him and assign him his rightful vocation? The question carried them into a mysterious region. This commonwealth must have some supernatural foundation and some supernatural superintendence; on any other hypothesis it was inconceivable. Once convinced of that, they felt it a duty, or what seemed the same thing to them a logical necessity, to invent supernatural machinery and put on the airs of inspired men. This fraud brought every kind of absurdity and falsehood after it, and under their weight the system quickly sank. French philosophers were not yet ready for an Apollonius in the nineteenth century. They had not yet learned to feel as Porphyry felt, that there was a kingdom in the world that could not be opposed without the help of some mythical hero. Perhaps, if the vision of such a kingdom becomes clearer and more threatening, some will be driven to that resource. St. Simonianism will then reappear under another name and form, to see whether it can satisfy the inextinguishable longing of human hearts for a human fellowship.

The socialism of Mr. Owen is wholly unlike St. Simonianism in all its more striking and philosophical features, and for that very reason it may win wider popularity for a time. A benevolent person set up a factory in a district of Scotland on a principle that made it a blessing, not a curse, to its workers and the neighbourhood. One who had shared in the experiment, though he did not originate it, adopted the plausible notion that a similar arrangement might suit all our manufacturing districts. How to deal with the population concentrated there is the most awful problem facing the modern politician, so anyone with even a suggestion, especially one drawn from experience, deserved a patient hearing. Mr. Owen showed that men under a laborious, kindly, self-denying superintendence, with certain contrivances, would be happier than those treated as animals capable of producing a certain quantity of cotton twist. The demonstration was valuable even though the result could have been anticipated. But by a process, alas, most natural, he went on to the conviction that the whole secret lay in his particular machinery. By another step he concluded that such machinery could by itself produce every desirable moral result. Once that Rubicon was crossed, it took only a mind more generalising, daring, and self-conceited than most, held back by little historical knowledge and few intellectual obstacles from experiments in disorganising society, to produce a preacher of the doctrine that men are mere creatures of circumstances, who can be wholly reformed by readjusting them. The corollaries worked themselves out gradually, without intellectual subtlety, as obstructions to the scheme appeared. Whatever principles, practices, or institutions among men are tied to the idea of a will or spirit in us that might be superior to circumstances must be given up. All forms of religious faith inevitably fall under this. But the Owenites have been permitted to enforce a discovery of the highest practical value, though they were not the first to make it: Marriage is in the same category. Its meaning or validity cannot be maintained, logically or practically, when a spiritual principle and spiritual obligations are denied.

Owenism may be described as an attempt, on a larger scale than Benthamism, to apply to all society the maxims already adopted by certain classes within it. Worship of circumstances is the habit of feeling the easy and comfortable part of mankind naturally fall into. Their inward thought is that their houses will last for ever, and that makes them averse to change and sceptical of any invisible government. Now the poor say, “We, too, will acknowledge circumstances to be all in all, we will cast away belief in the invisible, this world shall be the only home we dwell in.” Such language may well appal all who hear it. To one who sympathizes with the poor it is fearful because of what it shows they are ready to abandon. To one who does not, it is fearful because of what it shows they are ready to take from him. Yet the force of these assertions lies in the very point where they are anti-socialist. It is the “we will” that gives them all their meaning, and it is that which gives the dry chips of Mr. Owen’s theory the semblance of life. He protests against the existence of that to which he owes his own pertinacity and all the effects it has ever produced.

So combination in its simpler forms is much more truly the characteristic of our times, and much more truly fearful because it carries more elements of real power, than all the schemes for reconstructing the universe. I mean combination to help the will of the majority and enforce its right to be heard and to rule: trades’ unions and chartist unions. I use language from English experience, but I fear it is quite intelligible to other nations, at least to Frenchmen. This combination is not stripped of religious sympathies, but with piteous fury strives to seize and appropriate them for its own purpose. No doubt every one of these schemes embodies some truth that cannot be lost. The greatest happiness of the greatest number may be the most idle and insignificant of formulas until each of its nouns and adjectives has been translated, yet it must contain a meaning that will somehow be realised. The phrase “each according to his capacity” points to a persuasion, which we cannot afford to lose, that gifts are suited to particular vocations and offices. The idea of co-operation, on which Owen dwells, is of wonderful depth and importance. But each scheme is chiefly remarkable as the shrine of a feeling it cannot satisfy and of a conviction it labours to stifle. The feeling is that a universal society is needful to man. The conviction is that if there is such a society, its characteristic distinction must be the treatment of man as a voluntary or spiritual being.

Section III.—Political Movements, 3.

3. Among the notions Rousseau scattered about the world, some of the most startling concerned education. The author of “Émile” said that Plato’s commonwealth is simply a scheme of education. When the true commonwealth rises out of the ruins of the old society, he said, the question of how education is to be conducted will swallow up all others. Men who started from opposite points to Rousseau, and went in different directions from him and from each other, came to hold this opinion. People began to believe that education would take the place of prisons, penitentiaries and hangmen, and that statesmen above all others were bound to care about it. The French Revolution shook these feelings. As the awfulness and dignity of Law were acknowledged again, the idea of replacing its punishments with gentler influences was scorned. The educational doctrine was treated as part of the sentimental creed about the goodness of human nature. That creed had been proved absurd, since men had been seen to do and wish to do very little without prisons or guillotines, so its inferences had to be abandoned too. These feelings seldom took shape as definite thoughts or arguments, but they were powerful. To them, I think, we may attribute the dislike of popular education shown by supporters of existing institutions, especially in England.

But both sides in the controversy had forgotten one point. Education must henceforth, said the disciple of the new school, be the great agent in the world. Nonsense, replied the English country gentleman; we will keep to the old ways and do as our forefathers did. But what were the old ways? History shows that our forefathers valued education as much as Rousseau did. They believed prisons and penitentiaries were useless without it, and that it had powers never entrusted to them. They had no thought of confining it to any class, since they were always providing for the training of youths whose chief qualification was poverty. This discovery was fatal to the argument against the philosophers, and for a time it settled the practical question in their favour. England’s old educational institutions had been abused through carelessness or selfishness, and on the continent they had been destroyed by revolutionary violence. In both, multitudes were growing up with the new notions of popular power and will, and a new unwillingness to bow to authorities merely because they were established. Jails could not be provided for all, or if they could, it might in time be a question who would be the jailers. So statesmen began to say that we must have fresh schools. The old ones have served well enough to train those with a national position, but they were not meant for the masses who have none. They may train those who want instruction and will make sacrifices for it, but they are no use to those who hardly think of it as a blessing or know what it means.

These thoughts have, I think, greatly changed the minds of all men on the subject. I say all, because the change is as remarkable in those who called education a panacea as in those only lately awakened to its necessity. The former used to urge how much the poor would gain from having intellectual cultivation within reach: new pleasures to command, temptations to avoid. The latter rebutted this with plausible appeals to fact. Did the boys in the schools acquire these tastes? Were they the better for their knowledge? Was the population more refined or more moral? I do not know where such controversies could end, or how much statistics may have been twisted on each side to prove the theorem. But the advocates of education now say: look at these masses of human beings. The question is not what you should do to amuse or benefit them, but what you must do to govern them and keep them from destroying the land and themselves. The ordinary resources of government have failed. Religion has proved inadequate when it serves only as a support to law. You must try some other means. You may laugh at the idea that a silent moral influence like education could be enough, but history and these living masses laugh at the idea that your physical appliances are enough. This is quite different language, and it is not easy to answer. The only answer is the sleepy resolve not to think about it until a revolution forces us to.

This change of language involves other changes that may not be obvious. There may be a hundred differences over what should be taught and who should teach it. But those who defend education on these grounds agree that, whatever the right instruments, the aim is to gain dominion over the will of these masses. The phrasing matters little. Earnest and able defenders of education may be loath to adopt the conviction I noted that many political students had reached by comparing the theories and events of the last fifty years. That conviction speaks of man as a mysterious being whose natural inclinations, if followed, make him a lawbreaker and an enemy of his fellows, but who may be raised above them to a true freedom, above the penalty of laws because he has no wish to break them. Such expressions may seem strange and fantastical to them, and unlike the school they were trained in. Yet they may mean nearly what those who use such odd mystical talk mean. By many words and acts they confess that they regard education as a wondrous power acting in a wondrous way. It is not a charm, and it is not inconsistent with man’s constitution. It is a power that is somehow to reach a faculty or principle, call it what you like, that swords and clubs cannot reach. There is, I think, very general agreement on this, however diverse the elements of the agreement. Take those who say it is hopeless to give religious instruction to all the members of a nation, so that (what they call) secular instruction must come first. They defend this on two grounds. One is that the perils of leaving the people untaught are infinite, with threats of popular outrage everywhere that the legislature cannot put down. The other is that there must be something common to all men, something all can receive, and this cannot be sect-notions and opinions, which clearly do not belong to all. Taken simply as it stands, this argument leads to reflections quite unlike any the ablest treatises on education of the last century would naturally suggest. It sets us thinking what kind of power can address a whole body of human creatures and call out in each of them what gives him the rights of Man and makes him a fellow-worker with his brethren. Sad experience has convinced thoughtful people that the secret does not lie in the mechanical contrivances for gathering children in a school, which were offered a few years ago as the perfect cure for the nation’s evils. These organize and discipline masses into a stupid material consistency, or now and then rouse the powers of one or two clever pupils to a dangerous self-exalting activity. They do not give the whole body a quiet life circulating through each member. The Protestant systems and sects, as I have said, are also found inadequate. They scarcely recognize that in the pupil which can be spoken to and called forth, and they divide instead of harmonising. Still there seems to be such a power somewhere. Who will tell us where?

4. The method used to cut this knot explains the last remarkable peculiarity of our modern political views. All the bodies that profess a voluntary character have proved unequal to conducting education. So it has seemed inevitable that each nation’s government should undertake to form and superintend it. Anyone who has soberly considered how to meet this dire necessity will not be surprised that hundreds see it as the only refuge from despair. We may have to give up many cherished notions of personal and domestic liberty. It may be painful that we in the nineteenth century have no better resource than one we learnt to think unsatisfactory and dangerous in the old Greek republics. Still a wise lover of his species will not be stopped from adopting what he is sure is the only practical relief. But some perplexing reflections intrude before he reaches that certainty. Is it not strange that we assert this marvellous power for the state just when there is the most reluctance to acknowledge even the powers that plainly belong to it? Is not the Will of the multitude asserting its independence of the civil power, and is this not the very exigency we are devising a remedy for? Have not most European governments been hastily stripping off the spiritual properties and functions they claimed in earlier times? Are not those called illiberal that hesitate to do so? Is it not inconsistent that these governments should at the same moment assume what is, by their own confession, an authority over the spirits of men in the strictest sense? I say by their own confession, because we do not escape the difficulty by saying the state shall have charge only of secular education, leaving the rest to more peculiar and individual interests. Is not our aim in education to gain an influence over human hearts that are cherishing fearful dreams of independence and defiance? Is not this the only plea for the state’s interference, that its existence is endangered by ignorance and self-will? And must not the education the state superintends aim above all, by whatever means seem likeliest, and whatever epithet you give them, at accomplishing this?

I fancy the practical statesman will be continually haunted by this contradiction, and perplexed far more in action than in meditation. People cry out for a power that is not merely legal or punitive but acts directly on the human spirit, so that legal and punitive sanctions may appeal to the consciences of men rather than the fears of brutes. To satisfy them, you inspire the state with this power, or at least force it into acts implying it. Yet the state is saying all the time that it can deal with nothing internal and can take notice only of overt acts. People also cry out for a universal body in which men are regarded as human beings and not merely as members of a local society. To satisfy them, you insist that the government of a local society take on a universal human character. All the new schemes for managing the world bear witness that it never has had and never can have such a character, and that its only security lies in its distinctions and limitations. Still the difficulty remains. If there is no spiritual universal society, and every attempt to create one in this nineteenth century has been abortive, the state must take on the appearance and do the duties of one. It must do so at any hazard of inconsistency, despite every danger to individual liberty, and despite the strong and growing feeling that it is not competent.
