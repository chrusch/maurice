Introductory Dialogue with a Quaker

A disillusioned Quaker recounts drifting through evangelical, Unitarian, and Roman Catholic answers to his doubts, and an interlocutor urges him to seek the Kingdom of Christ beneath all the sects rather than abandon his principles.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Quaker. Are you serious in saying you do not want me to leave the Society I was brought up in?

Author. I did not say that. I hope you will not abandon the principles you were brought up in.

Q. You mean the general morality and common Christianity that you and I were both taught to revere?

A. No. I do not believe you will keep those general principles and common doctrines unless you also keep the ones that belong to Quakerism.

Q. What can that mean except that I should stay in the Society?

A. I can answer better if you tell me what now tempts you to leave it.

Q. It is a long story, and I fear you will not hear it out. I was first startled by a contradiction between what our members profess and what they do.

A. You may change many times before you find a body where you will not notice the same thing.

Q. I know that. I hope I have enough sense not to give up a serious conviction because hypocrites pretend to it or a good man holds it weakly. That was not my difficulty. The contradiction seemed to run through all our arrangements, and even, to some degree, through the constitution of our Society. We are witnesses for spirituality, and I feel we are rank formalists.

A. Corruption may have crept into your system, but why judge it by its present appearance? Is reform impossible?

Q. For a long time I thought it possible. I decided to study the early Quakers, where I expected to find the higher, purer spirit that seems to have left us.

A. Were you disappointed?

Q. I thought not. Those writers showed deep earnestness, courage, self-denial, an intense conviction that what they said was true, and a readiness to live and die that others might hear it.

A. So you concluded that your Society had a sound foundation?

Q. If I had been convinced of that, I do not think I would have despaired of its present state. But here was my perplexity. I had read the Scriptures diligently and knew something of my own heart, and what our Quaker ministers said about human depravity, the grace of God, and redemption by Christ seemed to me mostly vague and unsatisfactory. Still, the doctrines on these subjects that are commonly, and I think rightly, called Evangelical are recognised among us, and some ministers bring them out prominently. The older Quakers, whom I was so inclined to admire on other grounds, show these doctrines far less distinctly. They seem to blame other Christians for attaching importance to them, and sometimes use expressions almost incompatible with believing them.

A. Did you think they put notions of their own in place of these doctrines?

Q. I reluctantly came to suspect so. That is why I wonder that you talk of my keeping the principles of Quakerism. They seem to me opposed to what I suppose you consider the leading tenets of the Gospel.

A. How did you come to that persuasion?

Q. By the workings of my own mind, and then it was confirmed by two people who differed in many of their views.

A. Who were they?

Q. The first was an Independent dissenter. He strongly admired the language and conduct of Fox and Penn on many occasions. He said he could not join them in objecting to all payment of ministers, or in disliking baptism, and several such points. But he wished that the energy they showed in denouncing ecclesiastical superstitions and oppressions might pass to English dissenters.

A. That was surely very complimentary.

Q. But when he came to Fox’s theology his tone changed completely. He called Fox a mystic, ignorant of the truths of the Gospel, who proclaimed theories that utterly subverted them. A clergyman of your Church who was present held the same opinion, if anything more earnestly. However much he disliked Fox’s indecorous and violent language about the Established Church, he thought Fox’s doctrine of an Inward Light immeasurably more dangerous. It was, he said, utterly incompatible with the Bible and with the experience of every true Christian.

A. Perhaps you remember some of the arguments your two friends used?

Q. Their arguments did not impress me as much as the clear, settled persuasion they shared, that if Fox were right, the Bible must be wrong. You may smile that I gave weight to assertions when I cannot recall the evidence for them, but I know that assertions coming from deep conviction often move me more than elaborate logical proofs.

A. It is the same with me, so I will not ridicule your admission.

Q. I am glad, because ridicule does not generally do me good. After these two gentlemen had impressed me strongly with their serious objections, they thought it fitting to indulge their humour at the expense of our founders and their mystical notions.

A. Having entered the castle, they were naturally anxious to dismantle it.

Q. They took away more of my defences than they perhaps meant to. While they were with me I felt bound to say something for feelings and thoughts I had held sacred from childhood, even if they were deserting me. When I was alone I found I had defended my Quakerism poorly. But I had also seen cracks and hollows in their system that I had not noticed before. Their wit shook my reverence both for my own faith and for what I had hoped would replace it.

A. A melancholy state of mind, as all who have known it can testify.

Q. In that state I read two Unitarian books, one by an Englishman of the last century and one by an American of the present.

A. The former at least did not treat your mystic feelings more kindly than your evangelical friends had.

Q. No. But he taught me that the Calvinistic doctrine could be ridiculed just as well as they and he ridiculed mine. I could not judge the value of his critical remarks and discoveries. But he said enough to convince me that Scripture has often been strained to support the opinions he attacked, and that the positive texts in their favour were far fewer than I had imagined. His views of God’s character and his feelings toward his fellow-creatures also agreed better with my conscience than what I had heard from the members of any sect but my own.

A. So the impression he left was on the whole pleasing?

Q. By no means. When I put the book down I felt utter coldness and dreariness. The very idea of a spiritual world and a spiritual life seemed missing from it. Materialism was the point all its speculations were heading toward, if it was not their basis.

A. I should think that was not the case with the American.

Q. No, he was of a very different temper. Some of his statements were not unlike things I had met in Fox and Penn. His mind was more comprehensive than theirs, more able to take interest in ordinary affairs and general literature, yet scarcely less morally exalted, and at times I was inclined to say scarcely less spiritual.

A. Could you comfortably accept his religious scheme?

Q. I almost fancied I could. There was something so capacious and engaging in it. But just as I finished the book I fell ill, and before I recovered I heard that one of my oldest and dearest friends had died. Then my interest in both the Englishman and the American vanished. I think I disliked the American most, because his promises were the fairest.

A. But did you not say you had traced a fine vein of humanity and spirituality in him?

Q. I thought so. But however humane or spiritual he was, he was not personal. There was nothing in him from which a soul struggling with life and death could draw the least help. He was plainly meant for sunshine and gala days. Then I remembered what my evangelical friends had said and the doctrines they had set before me. In that moment these seemed all-important, and I bitterly accused myself for thinking them narrow and hard. What had I to do with large views of men’s happiness or God’s character? My own soul was at war, and the friend I wanted was whoever could show it a way of peace. It seemed I had no right to trouble myself with anything else.

A. When you recovered, you probably sought the advice of the Independent dissenter you mentioned?

Q. For some reasons I wanted rather another interview with the clergyman who had spoken so strongly against Fox and mysticism, but just then he left the neighbourhood. I heard that his successor in the parish was an author, so I bought his books before venturing to call on him.

A. Did they encourage you to expect help from him?

Q. I thought I had been shaken enough already by my own discoveries and my various counsellors. Apparently not: these books were to upset me altogether.

A. How? Did you meet a sceptic disguised as a clergyman?

Q. Oh no, he was the most vehement enemy of all scepticism. He said that doctrines had been delivered in the earliest ages of Christianity which we should receive with simple, uninquiring faith. The blessings of salvation, he said, are tied to our submission to a certain system ordained by God. Evangelical teaching had almost entirely forgotten this system, or treated it as merely a matter of external arrangement. That teaching spoke of a period of conversion in which people pass from death to life, whereas the Church and Scripture attribute this wonderful change to baptism.

A. You did not believe these statements. Why did they affect you so powerfully?

Q. Partly, perhaps, because they were uttered with that strong, deep conviction which I admit always acts most strongly on me, especially when, as in this clergyman’s case, it is backed by a laborious and self-denying life. But I think there was another cause. A person who has suffered severely from religious struggles has an inward longing for rest that no one else can know. Bruised, beaten, humbled, he cannot help listening to anyone who tells him he has been all wrong. He has been so tormented by his own miserable experiments and failures that he must be glad to hear he should give them up entirely. So after many struggles with my pride and my modesty, the shame of uttering my feelings and the pain of hiding them, I decided to state my difficulties to the new clergyman.

A. He can scarcely have expected a convert from such a quarter.

Q. He evidently did not want one. I was used to people who spared no pains to bring me over to their opinions, so the change was rather agreeable. But he also clearly did not understand me, and, if I am not mistaken, thought it would be wrong to understand me. I daresay I stammered and spoke incoherently when I tried to tell him my thoughts. One cannot speak as clearly about oneself as about the weather and the crops. And he made so many efforts to turn the talk to those subjects, and fixed such steady, clear, cold eyes on me when I asked for information on anything else, that I was no less embarrassed at the end of our interview than at the beginning.

A. How did he show that he misunderstood you?

Q. You know I was educated to look on all forms and ordinances as sinful. Those thoughts grew with my growth and strengthened with my strength. But lately I have repeatedly felt that these forms might be desirable, and even that something in me needs them. This was the point I especially wished to raise with Mr. ——. I knew that there were things it might not be right for him to teach me yet. But I thought that someone who considered forms most important to our moral life might have removed some of the difficulties clouding my mind about them, or at least interpreted some of the desires and longings I had conceived for them in spite of my prejudices. He seemed to think this wish irreverent and presumptuous. “I can enter into no explanations with you,” he said; “you are not in a state to understand explanations. You have one plain duty: submit to the ordinances of Christ’s Church, confess the sins of your fathers in withdrawing from it and your own in continuing the schism, receive Christian Baptism, and then I shall be happy to instruct you.” “I am much inclined to take this course,” I replied, “but I feel difficulties and scruples I do not know how to overcome.” “Sir,” he said, “the case may be stated very shortly. The Fathers of the Church, the Saints and Martyrs of the first five centuries, the Greek Church, the Romish Church, the English Church, all agree that Baptism is the one door by which we can enter Christ’s fold, and so everlasting life. An ignorant mechanic in the seventeenth century said there was some other door, and you have chosen to disbelieve the whole Catholic Church and follow the mechanic. Is this a safe proceeding? If a speculation in cloth or wool were proposed to you, and a hundred competent judges said it would ruin you, while one person wholly unacquainted with the trade said it might turn out well, would you not think yourself mad to venture on it? Will you put your chances of salvation on a still greater risk?” With these words he wished me good morning.

A. How did this argument affect you?

Q. You may wonder, and I wonder myself, since it seems so consistent and plausible, but I never felt less moved by any words in my life. No, that is wrong: they did affect me. They almost counterbalanced the impression I had earlier formed in favour of your Church and its ordinances.

A. How could that be?

Q. Reverence was what I looked for above all in this clergyman. He had spoken of the sin of irreverence in his book, and my heart felt the truth of his words. He said it was his reverence that kept him from speaking to me about his faith. And yet he could teach me to calculate about eternal life as if it were merchandise! He spoke not to the part of me that was looking above the earth, but to my earthly, selfish nature. He did not speak to the part ready to submit to anything and bear anything to do God’s will and know Him, but to all my proud, contentious, disputatious feelings. He did not address me as a creature capable of reverence, though he accused me of irreverence. He taught me to connect a lower, more grovelling notion of Christianity with it than I had ever held under the most irreverent of my teachers.

A. You felt, then, that you would be more right to remain a Quaker?

Q. No, I did not feel that. My mind was in the most wretched contradiction. But I said to myself: I am urged to forsake the body in which I and my fathers have lived. I am to turn away from those who first taught me to revere the workings of my own spirit and to tremble at the name of God, and to break all the bonds of old affection and sympathy. And why? Because higher objects and interests demand even this sacrifice? Because I must leave father and mother for the sake of Christ? No, because it is the safer, more politic course, because on a balance of probabilities I shall be less likely to suffer selfish loss here or hereafter. Magnify the loss as you will, and heap up the words infinite and eternal: this is a question of principle, not of degree, and when I set principle at nought, I believe those great and terrible words lose all meaning for me.

A. You must not unfairly suppose that the clergyman you spoke with attached no higher, more spiritual idea to those words than what he seems to have conveyed to you. He probably meant an argument aimed at the man himself.

Q. I understood that. I formed the conviction from parts of his book, and still hold it, that he has a very high and spiritual apprehension of things. I knew he would think me unfit to be admitted to his deeper mysteries, and I claimed no such honour. But from the little I have read about the catechumens in the primitive Church, I fancied they were not taught something wholly different in kind from what they learnt after baptism.1 I did not know they were reasoned with on the selfish motives which their later initiation must have aimed to cure. But be that as it may, an argument aimed at the man himself is meant, I suppose, to produce some kind of conviction, or else it is a gratuitous insult. I cannot imagine anyone on whom this kind of argument would produce conviction. It assumes that the person appealed to cares for nothing but profit and loss. Someone who cares for nothing else would merely smile at an attempt to rouse his fears about an unseen, future blessing, since his whole mind is wrapped up in what is passing around him. Your wisdom, therefore, consists in ignoring the part of him that might listen, and addressing the part that has no ears.

A. But if this is an error, it is not peculiar to one school among us. In a commercial country we are all more or less inclined to act, think, and argue on such maxims.

Q. I do not think the clergyman would thank you for saying he took his notions from a commercial age. But I was not bringing a charge against a school. I was only telling you my own story.

A. It is a sad and eventful one. But you have not finished it?

Q. The next incident will surprise you most. I have scarcely the courage to confess that during the last two months I have had frequent and not unpleasant interviews with a Romish priest.

A. The argument from safety is so established and habitual among members of his Church that I think he cannot have been more agreeable to you than the English clergyman.

Q. He has rarely, if ever, used it. Perhaps he guessed from something I said at our first meeting that it would carry little weight with me. Far from denouncing my former thoughts and opinions, he has taken pains to show me how many points of sympathy there are between us.

A. Sympathy between a Romanist and a Quaker!

Q. “I find,” he says, “in the writings of the early Quakers the most earnest longings after a universal Church. Is it wonderful that they felt such longings? What is called the English Church, the only one they knew, is limited to a particular locality, subjected to the yoke of a national sovereign, tied down by national laws and customs. Depend upon it, my friend, your founders were right: that is not Christ’s Church which is not universal.”

A. How did you answer that?

Q. I said our Quaker system was by its nature spiritual, and his Church seemed to me opposed to spirituality. “Opposed to spirituality!” he exclaimed. “Where will you find so many men and women of high spirituality, so many who resemble your own in their love of mystical contemplation, as among us? Has not Thomas à Kempis always been a favourite with your friends? And is not the ‘Imitation of Christ’ the book of Catholic devotion?” “These,” I said, “were individuals, but what of the system?” “Well,” he continued, “look at the system. What is your great complaint against the English system? Is it not that the mouths of men are shut who are urged by the Spirit to speak God’s word? Can you bring that charge against us? Look at our friars, taken from the humblest classes, recognised the moment they show a real inward vocation, adopted at once as our teachers though they have no worldly qualification to recommend them. Do you not fault your English authorities because they forbid one whole sex to act as the handmaids of Christ and His flock? It is true we do not adopt your notion that women may speak in the churches, since we adhere more strictly to the words of Scripture. But the feeling that has led you to raise your voices for their rights and duties meets with every encouragement among us Catholics. We rejoice to see women devoting themselves to the Church’s service, we give them all help and honour while they live, and we account them saints when they die.” He found another point of agreement. “What is the great moving spring and centre of action to which all your writers refer? Surely love. They believe that though faith and hope are great Christian graces, the greatest of all is charity. That is the very principle we contend for. The Protestants wish to substitute faith for love. We say, as your friends also have said, that we will not.”

A. Have these arguments brought your mind to greater quietness and satisfaction?

Q. Quietness and satisfaction! The words sound as if spoken in a dream. No indeed. I am as far from them as any poor mortal ever was. If the thought of joining the Romish Church ever presents itself, it comes as the fearful dream of something I may be driven to, a last hopeless alternative. Just as often it seems I may become a St. Simonian or a Socialist. These systems too have their attractions. They speak to wants in me which I think must be satisfied, and yet perhaps never are to be. Often I wish above all for a potion that would put me to sleep.

A. To sleep, my friend, perchance to dream.

Q. I know it well. This broken fever-sleep is worse than being awake.

A. Need I now answer the question you asked at the start of our conversation, what I meant by wishing you to keep your Quaker principles though you might leave the Quaker Society?

Q. Indeed you must. I am as much in the dark about the possibility of such a distinction as ever.

A. Your first objection to Quakerism arose from the feeling that it was not acting out its original idea.

Q. And my second from the feeling that the idea itself was false.

A. Let us consider. Did you really discover that idea to be false, or some other idea to be true?

Q. I found, or thought I found, certain great doctrines laid down in Scripture, most important to my own being. The early Quakers seemed to take only passing notice of them, if they did not reject them altogether.

A. I do not doubt that you found these doctrines in Scripture, that you found them practically important, or that the early Quakers comparatively neglected them. What I doubt is whether you ascertained that the doctrines the Quakers did notice were unimportant, unscriptural, or inconsistent with the others.

Q. I certainly thought they were.

A. Yes, and to that judgment of yours, formed perhaps on very hasty and insufficient evidence, you attached the same sacredness as to the witness of your heart and conscience, and of Scripture with them, that the doctrines your evangelical friends spoke of were needful to you.

Q. Is that a practical distinction?

A. You yourself recognised it most emphatically. Did you not say that while the dissenting minister and the evangelical clergyman were maintaining their own positions, they made the deepest impression on you? And that when they began to ridicule mystical opinions, they created the most vehement reaction in your mind against what you had been inclined to adopt?

Q. I certainly said so.

A. Well, in that confession I think I can find an explanation of all your later experience. You parted too suddenly with something God meant you to keep, and all the bewilderment and restlessness you have since suffered has been the necessary, appointed punishment of that error.

Q. If so, it is an error I cannot retrieve. The Evangelical, the Unitarian, the English clergyman, the Romanist may have left me nothing to fill the void in my mind, but they have effectively despoiled me of what was there before. They have not convinced me there is a standing-place in any of their systems, but they have made me certain I have none in my own.

A. Alas, it is thus that men, benevolent, honest, holy men, trifle with what is most awful and sacred in their brethren’s minds! But they suffer as much evil as they inflict.

Q. In what way?

A. They turn the truths they hold in their inmost hearts, which God gave them to defend, into negations and contradictions. They are forced to resort to insufficient proofs and false assumptions in support of those truths, because they wilfully rejected the evidence God was supplying in the wants and cravings of their fellow men. They encourage infidelity under the name of faith. They form parties when they mean to proclaim principles that would make parties impossible. They set up theories and systems built on private judgments and individual conceits, while professing to lead us to permanent truths that belong to all and are necessary for all. They create new divisions by their very efforts to promote unity. They invent lines and landmarks of their own, while the great everlasting distinctions God has established escape them altogether.

Q. But why declaim against an evil so deeply rooted in human nature that six thousand years of effort to eradicate it have failed?

A. Why, indeed, if I did not believe that God has provided a complete and effectual witness against this evil, which is, as you say, so rooted in our selfish natures. I would not speak if I did not see that this witness has made itself heard in every age above all the clamours and distractions seeking to drown it. I would not speak if I were not convinced that the world would have been torn in pieces by its individual factions without this bond of peace and fellowship in its midst, or if I were not sure that peace is meant to drive out war, good evil, and light darkness. I would not speak if I could not see more abundant proofs of this glorious fact in our own day than in any before, or if it did not seem to me that all sects and factions, religious, political, or philosophical, bear testimony, sometimes mute, sometimes noisy, occasionally hopeful, oftener reluctant, to the presence of that Church Universal which is at once to justify their truths, explain the causes of their opposition, and destroy their existence.

Q. But the difficulty is that these factions have been in the Church Universal itself, whatever meaning you give that phrase.

A. That is just what I was asserting. There is a sect spirit, which labours to set up individual whims, opinions, and judgments in each of us: in us of the English Church as well as in the Romanist, the foreign Protestant, the Quaker, the Evangelical Dissenter, or the Unitarian. The question is whether we do not all, in our hearts and consciences, feel and know that this sect spirit is a vile, accursed, devilish spirit. If we know this, do not these same hearts and consciences testify that it is not meant to rule the world? And if that testimony is true, are we not bound to inquire what is to rule the world instead?

Q. And you think I may even yet enter on that inquiry with some hope?

A. The early Quakers testified that there was a Kingdom of Christ in the world, and that it would subdue all kingdoms to itself. Are you willing to inquire with me into their grounds for this assertion, to consider whether those grounds are tenable, and whether the Quaker system is or is not the realisation of the Quaker idea? Shall we then inquire into the principles of the religious bodies that want you to reject Quakerism, asking whether these also may be sound and true, whether they have been depraved and degraded by certain negative notions attached to them, and whether the systems invented to express them really do express them? If our conclusions on this last point are unsatisfactory, shall we then consider the Romanist’s assertion that there is a Catholic Church which existed before all these systems and derives from a higher authority than any of them? If he can make that good, we may inquire whether the Romish system is this Church or its disease. We may ask whether that system has exalted the ordinances of the Church which its supporters acknowledge and revere, or degraded them and deprived them of their significance. And we may ask whether this Church Catholic contradicts the ideas held by the Quakers and other Protestant bodies, or is their legitimate and perfect realisation. We cannot finish this investigation without examining the point on which your Romanist friend found so close a resemblance between your views and his: whether a national society and a universal society are by nature contradictory and incompatible, or have been made so only by notions that interfere both with the universality of the spiritual body and with the distinctness of the national body. When we have reached some conclusion on this, we shall be able to speak of our position in England. We can ask whether there is a Catholic Church here or not, and if there is, under what circumstances it exists and what its dangers and evils are. We can ask whether those dangers and evils are reasons for living in separation from it or for uniting ourselves more closely to it.

Q. I am ready to hear what you have to say on these subjects, though I cannot pretend to look for any great discoveries.

A. I rejoice that you do not. If we begin by expecting great results, our pride will be rewarded with disappointment, and we shall add one more scheme to those that looked so fair and proved so abortive. If we desire to walk humbly along the path God has marked out for us, rejecting no light however feeble that He vouchsafes, and trusting Him to guide us to the perfect day, I do not think His promise to wayfaring men will go unfulfilled for us.
