Part II. Of the Catholic Church and the Romish System

Chapter II. Indications of a Spiritual Constitution

Family bonds, then national citizenship, and finally the failed universal empires of Greece and Rome each reveal a spiritual constitution for mankind, one that mythology’s gods as fathers, brothers, and kings confirm at every stage.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

When I spoke of the Quaker system, I noted one practical inconsistency at its root that affects all its workings. A member of the Society of Friends ought to be the conscious disciple of a Divine Teacher. Yet every child born to a Quaker is treated as a Friend until some act of rebellion costs him the title. We found much the same anomaly in the Protestant systems. Consciously justified men ought to make up the Evangelical Church, and persons conscious of a divine Election the Reformed. Yet neither has had the courage to shut their children out of all religious fellowship and treat them as heathens. The Anabaptists came nearest to doing so, but even in them there are clear signs of timidity and inconsistency.

When we looked at the schemes of the world drawn up by philosophers, we saw that they ran into a knot much like the one that perplexed the founders of religious sects, and that they cut it, with more or less ceremony. A universal society was nearly impossible to organise while the distinction of families remained, because such a society needs men to be so many separate units. But family life is a glaring fact that proves they are not units. They are bound together by a law that may be defied and made almost wholly ineffective, but cannot be entirely repealed.

I. This fact that men live in families, which so troubles the inventors of systems, is perhaps the very one most likely to suggest to a plain person that mankind has a moral or spiritual constitution. We must speak of every man as being in two conditions. He is in a world of objects that present themselves to his senses. And he is a son, perhaps a brother. These two states are equally inevitable and perfectly distinct, and no artifice brings them under one law or name. The first is described by what we see, hear, handle or smell. The second is described by what we are: “I am a son,” “I am a brother.” So “circumstances” cannot be used of the one state as strictly as of the other. Everything I deal with I rightly call my circumstances, because they stand round me. But what is needed in an account of myself deserves another name, and we commonly call it a relationship. The difference grows plainer. We speak of a man having a bad digestion or bad hearing, and of his being a bad brother or bad son. Both phrases imply a want of harmony between the man and his condition. But the second says that the want of harmony need not exist: he is voluntarily acting as if he were not in a relation in which he nevertheless is, and must remain. We describe this inconsistency as moral evil, or by whatever equivalent phrase we have invented, for some equivalent we must have.

It might seem to follow that the family state is the natural one for man, and so we call the affections that go with it natural affections. But we also call the savage condition, in which man strives to be independent, the natural state of society. It is doubtful whether that deserves to be called a state of society, since it contradicts all states and all society. Still there is considerable justification for calling it natural. We cannot belong to a family without recognising in others, and feeling in ourselves, inclinations that tend to dissolve its bonds and set up a separate, independent life, which on a large scale we call savage or wild. Discipline keeps these inclinations down, and family affections are called out against them. So it is surely no mistake to give them the name we give to plants that spring up in uncultivated soil, unasked.

Here we have some indications of a spiritual constitution: the marks of a state designed for a voluntary creature, which is his whether he approves or not, and which he has a natural inclination to rebel against. But most people would mean more by the word, and ask how anything can be called spiritual that has no reference to religion. The histories and mythologies of all the peoples we know bear clear witness that men have connected the ideas of fathers, children, husbands, brothers and sisters with the beings they worshipped. That is our first, roughest observation. Searching further, we see that it is not merely a fact about mythology but the fact, without which all others are unintelligible. You may say the gods and goddesses are given all kinds of offices: they rule this town and that river, dispense this blessing or send that curse. Granted; but who are they who exercise these powers? Mythology tells of relations among them, and between them and those they favour or oppose. In later ages, studying the differences between nations’ mythologies, we naturally notice soil, climate, the beauty or dreariness of the country, the rains and floods, as shaping the views the people held of their unseen rulers. It is then an easy step to think these things account for their faith, and that the histories of the gods are accidental poetic embellishments. But consider these points. The worshippers evidently felt what we call accidental to be essential. Merging the gods into natural objects was an artifice of later philosophy. Soil and climate did produce important differences among the objects reverenced in various nations, but their being parents, brothers and sisters was, so far as we know, common to all, missing only among the utterly savage, who disregard human relations. Those who explain mythology from natural phenomena must beg what they call “a law of nature,” saying we are naturally inclined to ask the origin of great and remarkable objects. Yet it contradicts all experience to attribute that disposition to men whose feelings and faculties have not first been awakened somehow. A savage is little struck by any but the most glaring and alarming phenomena, and thinks even less about them. Considering all this, we may adopt the view that the simplest solution is the best: it is not our surroundings of sensible objects that lead us to think of objects we do not sensibly meet, but these perceptions come to us through our family relationships. We become more purely idolaters as those relationships are lost sight of and only the other facts of our condition regarded. A world without family relationships would have no worship; and without worship the feelings and affections of family life would have perished utterly.

II. But is there no meaning in that savage wish for independence? Is it merely the dissolution of the family bonds meant for men, or a sign that men were meant for other bonds too, not necessarily incompatible with these? History seems to decide for the latter. As there is a worse state of society than the patriarchal, there is a better and more advanced one; man’s faculties have never been properly developed except in a national community. Examining such a community from the Pagan world, we find its member had a more distinct feeling of himself, of his own personality, than a mere dweller in a family. This seems puzzling. In Sparta or Rome, or any commonwealth but Athens, society seems to impose the severest restraints on each man’s taste, judgment and will. Yet what gives interest to the better times of these republics is the energetic purpose of their leaders, and our assurance that the same kind of purpose, in lesser degree, existed in every rank of their armies. Likewise what makes their declining ages mournful is the sense that the armies had become mere soldiers, not men. So we have evidence, from where it might least be expected, that personal feeling is bound up with the sense of national union.

Of all men the savage1 has least feeling of dignity and self-respect. He is emphatically a mere workman or tool, the habitual slave of his own chance necessities and inclinations, and therefore usually of other men’s. Whoever understands the words “I am a brother” has taken a mighty step beyond him, even in the very respect the savage prides himself on; he is more of a person, more of a freeman. But he is not yet enough of either. To be more, he must be able to say “I am a citizen.” That is the true onward step; whoever seeks freedom by any other way relapses into an independence that is only another name for slavery. Several facts, so obvious that careless students of history miss them, are closely connected with this. One is that every organised nation at its beginning has a high respect for family relations, which necessarily embody themselves in its constitution. Another is that there is a struggle between these relations and the national polity, though they form so great a part of it, because the legislator feels that each brother, husband and father is a citizen and as such comes directly under his cognizance.

In Sparta the principle of family life, though clearly recognised, was largely sacrificed to the Laws. In Athens the legislator, anxious to leave men to themselves, allowed an independence to grow that proved incompatible with both family relations and national society. In Rome the legislation was so finely interwoven with the family principle that when that grew weak, the commonwealth inevitably fell.

These facts lead us to ask what this legislation means, where its power lies, and how it is both connected with and different from these relationships. First, Law takes each man apart from his fellows. It addresses him with a Thou, makes him feel an eye fixed on his doings and a penalty hanging over him. In this view it is the direct opposite of a relationship, which binds us to each other and makes us feel we cannot exist apart. But second, Law denounces the acts that make union and fellowship impossible, the acts that come from men’s determination to live as if independent of each other, making self-pleasing their end. It declares to each man that he is in a fellowship and must do nothing inconsistent with it. So the desire for individuality, the great foe of family relationship, is the very thing Law threatens and curses even while treating men as distinct persons. A nation, then, like a family, seems to have some characteristics of a spiritual constitution. If “spiritual” is taken as modern philosophers use it, we have abundant proof that where there is no feeling of national union, the exercise of intellectual power is precarious and imperfect. If it is taken as voluntary, we find a constitution evidently meant for creatures with wills: men do not create it for themselves, it may be violated, every man is naturally inclined to violate it, and “bad citizen” expresses moral reprobation as “bad father or mother” does. And are there religious feelings connected with national life, as with family life? The old mythology answers just as decisively. If the Homeric gods were fathers, brothers and husbands, they were also kings; the one character is as prominent and essential as the other. The former may have been the more ancient, which would explain scholars’ notion that traces of an earlier worship appear in the Iliad. By Homer’s time the domestic and kingly characters were joined, and the gods’ offices connected with nature, though gradually mingling with these characters and threatening to become identical with them, remained distinct from them. The princes of Agamemnon’s league felt there must be higher princes than themselves; they could use no authority and take no counsel except in that belief. And though they spoke of these rulers as compelling clouds and winds, they did not regard that as higher or more real than putting wisdom and spirit into Diomed and arming Hector for the fight. So the leaders were always types of the gods in every country that had reached a national polity. Wherever such polities existed, invisible rulers were recognised, a class of men interpreted their judgments, they were invoked as guides in battle, and sacrifices were offered to avert their displeasure or win their protection.

But a time came when men’s minds were awakened to something more comprehensive than either the family or the national constitution. The family belonged to all men, yet it was narrow in separating them from each other. The nation was plainly exclusive: limited to a small locality, it treated everything beyond it that it could not subdue as alien, if not hostile. If this exclusion were to continue, some nation ought to reign, with a right to make its polity universal. Great Asiatic monarchies had swallowed up tribes and kingdoms, but they had set up a rule of mere physical force.

Might not Greece, the land of intellectual force, show that it was meant to rule all? The young hero of Macedon went out in that hope and in a few years accomplished his dream. A few years later his empire was broken in pieces; Greece was not to be lord of the world. Still, in the Egyptian and Syrian dynasties she sent out, she asserted a mental supremacy. But a nation that paid no homage to art or philosophy swallowed up all these dynasties, and with them all that remained of Greece herself. A universal polity was established, and the national life and family life of Rome perished at the very moment she established it.

Was there a religion connected with this universal polity, as with the family and the national? There was. The Emperor was the great God, to whom all peoples, nations and languages were to bow. Under this supreme divinity all others might be tolerated and recognised. No form of religion was to be proscribed unless absolutely incompatible with the worship of a Tiberius or a Vitellius. It has already been suggested that this Roman Empire exactly answers to the idea of a universal world. If there is to be something different, a universal Church, we ought to know what elements it is made of. We ought to know whether it too sets aside family and national life, or justifies their existence, reconciles them to itself, and interprets the problems of ancient history about their mysterious meaning.

Chapter III. The Scriptural View of This Constitution, I

Religious people commonly acknowledge that the Bible is remarkably unsystematic. Sometimes they say so thankfully, even triumphantly, as proof that the Bible is meant to supply daily needs and meet the changing circumstances of the spiritual man. Sometimes it becomes an argument that others must do what the Bible does not do: those nearest the Apostles, or those at the Reformation, or those in a more advanced civilization. Sometimes it is a reason for denying that any book has the character Christians ascribe to this one. On that view the Bible is a collection of documents belonging to one nation, strung together by accident, and given a false wholeness by the superstition of later times.

All these views assume that system and method mean the same thing, so that a book without the first must lack the second. To me they are the greatest contraries. System is what is most opposed to life, freedom and variety, and method is what those things cannot exist without. To explain my meaning I would use an illustration rather than a definition, and the most striking one is the Bible itself. The systematizer is tormented on every page by the stubborn, hopeless material he has to handle. But the person who reads only for his own comfort and profit is, I am convinced, haunted by a sense of harmony. It lies not in the words but in the history, he ought not to overlook it, and without it the meaning of what he most loves is uncertain. This sense of method is also strengthened by the fact that these works were written at different periods, in different styles, by men of utterly different characters, for it makes the volume they form seem all the more marvellous. The most skilful analyst cannot persuade his disciples to give up the word Bible, and he cannot strip it of the feelings attached to it.

I. It may help our purpose to look into this a little. Anyone reading the Old Testament sees that the idea of a covenant between God and a certain people governs it. In plain history, lofty prayers and songs, impassioned denunciations of evil and predictions of coming misery, this idea lies at the root. Take it away and the parts lose their connection, and each book, however simple, becomes an incoherent rhapsody. Someone who only wanted to understand that strange people, which has kept its identity through so many generations, would begin with the account of this covenant. He would see that the call of Abraham, the promise to him and his seed, and the seal given him are the most significant parts. But one thing would strike him above all: the covenant is with a family. It is with a man, but expressly as head of a family, and every promise is bound up with the hope of descendants. Abraham’s patriarchal character cannot be treated as incidental to his being the chosen witness and servant of the Most High; the two are inseparable. His relation to God was interpreted to him through his human relations, and he could fulfil those relations because he acknowledged the higher one. Further reflection on the lesser parts of the narrative, which gain new meaning once this fact is seen as the centre, shows the following. The tribes among whom Abraham lived were sensual, and their worship was sensual. This involved neglect of family bonds. So the witness for an invisible, righteous God, against gods of nature and mere power, was by the same necessity the witness for the sacredness of those bonds. The world imagined a Being who exercised power over men, seen in the clouds and heard in the winds, and it trembled, until utter corruption brought utter atheism. Abraham, the beginner of the Church on earth, believed instead in a God related to men and made known through their human relations. But this truth could not be shown in one faithful individual; it had to be shown through a family. So the rest of Genesis gives the history of the patriarchs after Abraham. What if these, or some of them, were not faithful? What if they did not maintain family relationship, or remember the higher principle in it? What if the world should find its way into the Church? The historian does not wait to be asked; his narrative answers. Most of Jacob’s sons were not faithful. They did not maintain family life or remember the Being who had revealed himself through it. Perhaps, then, Joseph, the true believer, separated himself from his godless brothers and founded a new, distinct fellowship? Had he done so, he would have acted on the principle of Ishmael or Esau, and founded a society built on choice, not on relationship. The historian says he did otherwise. He was separated from his brothers, but by their act, not his, and he remained a witness to God’s covenant, not with himself, but with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob; not with an individual, but with a family. According to the Jewish Scriptures, then, the Abrahamic family was cut off by its covenant from the other families of the earth precisely so that it might witness for the true order of the world. Against that order all sensible idolatry and all independent choice or self-will is rebellion. In it alone men can be free, because to remain in it they must give up the inclinations that enslave them. And through it alone, as we might have guessed from the Gentile records, the idea of God can be imparted. The promise of the covenant was therefore that in the seed of Abraham all the families of the earth should be blessed.

Chapter III. The Scriptural View of This Constitution, II

II. Sentimentalists talk about the patriarchal condition of the world, its essential purity and the misery of leaving it, but the Scriptures give such dreams no support. It was part of the promise that Jacob’s children would enter another state: they were to possess the Canaanite nations and become a nation. The history, true to all experience, describes the passage between the two conditions as grievous, for the children of Abraham sank into moral debasement and slavery. Yet their redemption is tied to a more awful revelation than any they had received, or, as far as we can see, could have received, in their earlier state. It leads to wonderful new discoveries about the relations between men and God. The God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob declares that He remembers His covenant and has seen His people’s affliction. But to the guide and deliverer He appointed, He declares Himself by another name: the name on which the Jewish covenant stands and which is the foundation of all law, I AM THAT I AM. The judgments on natural worship, and on a tyranny that scorned all invisible and righteous government, were accomplished, and the people learned that an unseen Power had delivered them. Then the awful code was proclaimed amid thunder and lightning. It spoke straight to each man’s individual conscience, and it also reminded him, directly and solemnly, that he was related to God and to his brethren. I will not explain here how the tribal institutions, those of family relationship, were built into the Jewish constitution so that they gave this law its meaning without taking away its awful personal character. Anyone who studies the Pentateuch with the slightest attention must see it. It is more necessary to notice the institutions that lifted Jewish thoughts above family and law, though they were intertwined with both: the tabernacle, the priesthood and the sacrifices. Every thoughtful Jew knew these were shrines of an undeveloped mystery, and he was equally sure that the mystery was implied in all his acts, his family relations, the national order and his obedience to the law. The commandments declared that there was an awful, self-existent Being from whom all law came. The Tabernacle affirmed that He was present among His people and could be approached, in some awful manner. The family covenant witnessed to a relation between Him and His worshippers. The Priesthood, from generation to generation, witnessed that this relation could actually be realised, by the whole people in a representative. The national constitution and its punishments awakened in each person a sense of moral evil. They taught him that it arose from violating his relations with God and his countrymen, and that its effect was practical exclusion from those blessings. The sacrifices signified that the relation was restored when he had given something up, personally and through the priest. That something was not chosen by himself as most fitting or most precious, but appointed by the law, and with it he gave up the self-will that had caused the separation. Such thoughts were wrought gradually into every humble and obedient Jew. His great Lawgiver’s parting instruction brought them home to him, and his later experience, and the teachers who showed him its purpose, confirmed and illustrated them.

The Jewish national polity was in its essence exclusive. We dwell on this as if it cut off all connexion between that polity and the Pagan or modern European ones. But every nation, as such, is exclusive. Athens was exclusive and so was Rome, yet all admit that more of humanity came out in them than ever appeared in the savage tribes that never reached a definite polity. Before we can tell whether Jewish exclusiveness was inhuman, we must find out what it excluded, and the answer is the same as before: it excluded the worship of sensible, natural things, and it excluded choice and self-will. The covenant with an invisible Being made it treason for men to choose their own objects of worship. Worship of the one Being was the bond of the commonwealth, and if it broke, the commonwealth dissolved. The covenant obliged them to see all kings as reigning by virtue of His covenant, representing His dignity and answerable to Him. It obliged them to see every other officer, priestly, prophetical or judicial, as receiving appointment and commission directly from Him. Its first protest affirmed that there is not a set of separate gods over each territory, varying with soil and climate, but one Almighty and Invisible Being who is Lord of all. The God of Israel is declared the God of all nations, and the Israelites are chosen out to witness to the fact. By the second protest the exclusive Hebrew witnessed that no king, priest or judge has a right to think himself possessed of intrinsic power. Each exercises an office under a righteous king, a perfect priest, an all-seeing judge. As he keeps that thought and fulfils his task in its strength, the character of that king, priest and judge, and his own relation to men, are revealed to him. And these offices continue from generation to generation as a witness to the permanence of Him who is Lord of them all and abides for ever.

As in the patriarchal period the Divine Being manifested Himself in family relations, and so showed what they depend on, how they are upheld and where their worth lies, so in the national period He was manifested through all national offices, and so explained their meaning, support and worth. But we must not suppose family relations had less to do with this stage than with the former. They were built into the national institutions, and those institutions depended on them. So their meaning, in connexion with national life, national sins and the Being to whom both witnessed, grew more apparent.

I need not point out to anyone who reads the prophets their constant method of awakening the Jew’s conscience and giving him the highest truths. The Lord is always presented as the husband of the nation, and apostacy and false worship are constantly called adulteries. Great pains are taken to convince us that these are not poetical flourishes or terms of art, by connecting the actual human relation and offence with the spiritual one. The verbal commentator is often at fault because the two seem confused. He cannot decide whether the holy man is denouncing the nation’s infidelity to her God or actual wives’ infidelity to their husbands. Such perplexity is unavoidable for anyone determined to separate the two ideas, who does not see that the prophet’s main object is to show how they bear on one another. They will not enter his mind and feel that human relationships are not artificial types of something divine. They are actually the means, and the only means, by which man rises to any knowledge of the divine. Every breach of a human relation implies a violation of the higher law and also hinders the perception of it, drawing a veil between a man’s spirit and his God.

But how did this idea of a human constitution agree, or collide, with the attempts at universal empire that seemed the necessary end of the ancient polities? The Asiatic monarchies have sometimes been called patriarchal. Beyond doubt the patriarchal feeling, the belief that the king was a father, lay at their foundation and made up all that was sound in the monarch’s acts or the people’s reverence. But if we believe the Bible, the king is more than a father. His position is grounded in the acknowledgment of an unseen, absolute Being whose relations to men lead up to contemplating Him in Himself. The effort to make the paternal relation everything is therefore a false effort and leads to false results. Here the result is plain. The monarch’s power has no safe ground, and soon is revered merely as power. No sense of a law they ought to obey is called forth in subjects or monarch. He may have kindly affections toward them, and they toward him, but that is all. Nothing preserves the existence and sanctity of the family relationship on which sovereign authority is built. Nothing resists the tendency to natural worship, which destroys it, and nothing prevents the monarch from believing he reigns by his own right. So these so-called patriarchal governments awaken neither the energies of the intellect nor the perception of right and wrong, and soon turn into the direct contraries of what they profess to be. The father becomes an oppressor of his own people and a conqueror of others, and all idea of the invisible is swallowed up in reverence for him. At last he is looked up to as God of gods and Lord of lords. It is no false feeling that makes us rejoice when these patriarchal kings were driven back by the little national bands at Marathon or Platæa. Anyone who reveres invisible more than visible strength will not hold back his pæans at their defeat. It is hateful and godless to check them, or to stir up sympathy for the Eastern tyrant. Whoever cherishes that habit of feeling will not really be able to rejoice, whatever he fancies, when Pharaoh and his host sink like lead into the waters, or when Sisera with his six hundred chariots is put to flight by the prophetess of Israel.

In the history of the Jews we find that their distinct polity, all the time it lasted, was a witness against these Babel monarchies. In them the Jew saw concentrated, in its worst form, the world that the covenant with Abraham’s family and the Israelite nation had delivered him from. Yet his evil nature perpetually tended to be like this world, to share its splendours and adopt its worship, and at length he was punished by subjection to its tyranny. But the inclination was resisted by more than punishment. The wish for fellowship with other nations was a true wish inverted. The dream of a human polity was one the true God had sent the Jew, though an evil spirit had taught him how to realise it. The great office of the Hebrew Seer was to bring out the true idea of such a polity: to show how it lay hid in all their own institutions and how it would finally be fully manifested. Beside the vision of a Babylonian kingdom, which he taught his countrymen to see as based on a lying principle, the contrary of their own, and meant to scourge them if they took that principle into their conduct, rose another vision. It was of a king who did not judge by the sight of his eyes or the hearing of his ears, but ruled men in righteousness, and whom the heathen would own. Each new step in the covenant’s history, the call of the patriarch that made them a family and the deliverance under Moses that made them a nation, was joined with a fresh revelation of the Divine King through these different relations, neither displacing the other but taking it into itself. So this glorious vision would have been utterly imperfect without the prospect of a discovery not granted to any former age. The prophet was trained in deep, awed meditation on the law, on his land’s history, and above all on the mysterious services of the temple. As one sin and one judgment after another showed him his nation’s dangers and wants, he came by degrees to see that the heir of David’s throne must be a MAN, in as strict a sense as David was, capable of infinitely greater sympathy with every form of human sorrow. Yet in Him the worshipper must behold GOD, less limited by human conceptions and more in His own absoluteness and awfulness than even in the burning bush or amid the lightnings of Sinai. How could both longings be fulfilled? How could idolatry be abolished by the very manifestation that brought the object of worship nearer to human thoughts? How could belief in a Being nigh to men be reconciled with belief in one dwelling in His own perfection? How does unceasing action for His creatures agree with eternal rest? How could He be satisfied with men and yet incapable of satisfaction with anything less pure and holy than Himself? These were the awful questions that exercised the prophet’s soul. They were answered not at once but in glimpses and flashes of light across the darkness of his own soul and his country’s condition. Even now they startle us as we read, and make us feel that the words are meant to guide us through our own confusions, not to give us notions or formulas for disguising them. One part of his teaching must have come from the polity that was the great contrast to his own. The universal monarchs, the Sennacheribs and Nebuchadnezzars, were Men-gods. They took to themselves the attributes of the Invisible. In the same proportion as they hid from men’s eyes anything beyond humanity, they became inhuman, cut off from their kind, dwelling apart in an infernal solitude.

This black ground brought the perfectly clear, bright object more distinctly into view. The prophets felt that the God-man, in whom the fulness and awfulness of Godhead would shine forth, might therefore have perfect sympathy with the poorest and most friendless. He might at the same time enable them to enter that transcendent region which their spirits had always sought and never been able to penetrate.

Chapter III. The Scriptural View of This Constitution, III

III. When we open the first book of the New Testament, its opening words say its subject is the Son of David and the Son of Abraham. As we read on, we find that the writer believes this person came into the world to establish a Kingdom. Every act and word recorded of Him refers to it. A voice in the wilderness cries that a kingdom is at hand. Jesus comes preaching the Gospel of the kingdom. He goes up a mountain to give the principles of His kingdom. Nearly every parable He tells to the people begins, “The kingdom of heaven is like.” He heals the sick so that the Jews may know His kingdom has come near. His private talks with His disciples, as much as His public discourses, concern the character, establishment, and destiny of this kingdom. He is charged before Pontius Pilate with claiming to be a king, and the inscription on the cross proclaims Him one.

No one will deny that the Evangelists differ in character and style, or that a hundred theories may be offered about their origin, their agreements, and their differences. But it is equally true that this feature is common to all of them. The most sweeping theory of interpolation could not remove it, and discovering how the Gospels were formed would not touch it. The kingdom of Christ is, in one aspect or another, the subject of them all. It will be said that this is easily explained: the writers are Jews, and such language is peculiarly Jewish, the idiosyncrasy of the strangest and most bigoted people on earth. Up to a point this agrees with my own view. I have tried to show that the habit of thought behind this constant phrase is Jewish, and why. But it is a long step from that to the usual assumption that the phrase belongs only to particular accidents and circumstances and has nothing to do with what is essential and human. On my view, the Israelite was taken out of all nations to witness to what is permanent and not a matter of mere mode. He witnessed to the meaning of the relationships he shared with pagans and with us, which every pagan and every peasant among us feels to have a meaning. He witnessed also to the ground and purpose of national institutions and law, which the pagans acknowledged, and most of us acknowledge, as the great distinction between men and brutes. Pagans believed, and we believe, that beneath these relationships and this national order lies another order, not limited like the first and not exclusive like the second. So I cannot see why we should speak of the Jew’s prejudices and idiosyncrasies because he states this universal idea in the simplest and aptest words. The Evangelists say that what was promised and expected is a Kingdom, and that Jesus of Nazareth is the King. Either claim may be denied: it may be said that men do not need a spiritual and universal society, or that this person lacks the credentials He claims. But by all ordinary rules of criticism we must admit that this was the Evangelists’ idea, and in studying an author we should enter into his idea before substituting our own.

I know the objector has an answer, one that current religious opinion will support and many will weigh heavily. He will say we have an excuse for separating the inward sense of the Gospels from their Jewish accidents, in the inconsistency of the very phrases I cite. The Evangelists constantly present the kingdom as outward and visible, like David’s and Solomon’s, or that kingdom restored and extended, ready to replace Herod’s government and perhaps in the end the Caesars’. Yet they also attribute to their Master such words as “The kingdom of God cometh not with observation,” “The kingdom of God is within you,” and “My kingdom is not of this world.” These words show that He commonly taught another doctrine, which became mixed with coarser, more sensual elements. So, the objector says, are we not justified in breaking up the mass and taking out the pure ore?

I think the reader who has come with me this far will not be much shaken by this. The kingdom of David and of Solomon was distinguished from the kingdoms of this world, and it did not come with observation. It stood on a principle other kingdoms set at naught: that the visible king is the type of the invisible one, that he reigns by virtue of a covenant between the invisible king and the nation, and that he is subject to a divine law. All the great nations practically denied this principle, and denied it most emphatically when they became kingdoms, the ordinary and apparently necessary end of them all. The Israelite kingdom existed to enforce it. Throughout its history the nation and its kings tended to disregard the constitutional principle and forget the covenant, but that very tendency proved the truth of the idea it fought against. If so, what contradiction was there in saying that the new kingdom was the one promised to David, the kingdom of his son, and yet in the highest sense a kingdom the outward eye cannot observe, a kingdom within, a kingdom not of this world?

Do I mean that most or all who first heard these announcements found nothing startling in them? If I did, I would be rejecting the Evangelists’ own testimony. They tell us that the leaders of the Jewish commonwealth, and all the popular sects that divided it, were continually perplexed and outraged by this language. But they also tell us that these same people had lost the family and national character of Hebrews. They perverted God’s express commands about honouring father and mother. They felt no fellowship with Israelites as Israelites, and prided themselves on being different from their countrymen in righteousness or wisdom; individual self-exaltation of one kind or the other was their mark. In keeping with this, the Evangelists say these men were never so scandalised as when Jesus spoke of His Father, of coming to do His will, of knowing Him and being one with Him. The idea of a relation between men and their Maker, which the Abrahamic covenant implied, had wholly left them. So they could not cherish the hope of a full manifestation of the ground of that relation, the hope that had sustained every suffering Israelite in every age, the hope of Israel. Their idea of God was the heathen one: a Being in the clouds or spread through the universe, wholly separate from His worshippers, unable to speak through men to men, declaring Himself only by signs like the red sky of morning and the lowering sky of evening. So the king they expected was the counterpart of the absolute Emperor. The awful words “We have no king but Caesar” would not have been spoken at any other moment. It took intense hatred and all the passions then possessing their hearts to make the priests formally abandon the dream of Jewish supremacy, and they probably kept the right to hold one doctrine in the schools and another in the judgment-hall. Still, those words expressed the speakers’ most inward thought: the king of Abraham’s seed whom they wanted was a Caesar and nothing else.

But there were others who, amid much confusion and ignorance, really claimed their place as members of a nation in covenant with God. They had walked blameless in the Lord’s ordinances, finding the deepest reality in every symbol of the Divine Presence that seemed a phantom to the world, and the merest phantoms in what the world called realities. They knew their own darkness but rested on the promise of a light that should rise on their land. They joined public shame to a miserable sense of moral evil and looked for a deliverer from both at once. The Roman soldier oppressed them not because he reminded them of tribute but because he told them the national life was gone, or lived only in their prayers. Under the fig-tree they had begged God to disperse the clouds hiding His face, to remember the poor, and not to let men have the upper hand. Whether or not they could reconcile in their understanding a kingdom ruling over all with a kingdom in their hearts, they inwardly acknowledged that only one fitting both descriptions could answer the cries they had sent to heaven. Whatever they saw of Him who was proclaimed king, and whatever they heard Him say, tended to bring these thoughts into harmony, or at least to make them feel each was necessary. He had power over the elements and over the secret workings of the human body. (I am of course assuming the Evangelists’ story; my aim is to show that its parts are thoroughly consistent when viewed in light of one leading idea.) But He used this power for timid fishermen, paralytics, and lepers. He says His kingdom is like a grain of mustard-seed, the least of all seeds, which becomes a tree where the birds of the air lodge. He says also that the seed of the kingdom is scattered on different soils, and that the right soil is an honest heart. His acts had the most obvious outward effects, yet their main effect was to persuade the prepared observer that a communion had been opened between the visible and invisible worlds, and that the first was under the power of the second. His words were addressed to Israelites as children of the covenant, yet each tended to awaken in them a sense of humanity, a feeling that to be Israelites they must be more. All this general language prepared for the discoveries of the last supper, when, having loved His own who were in the world, He loved them to the end. There He announced that they were all united in Him as the branch is to the vine, that there was a still more wonderful union between Him and His Father which they might come to know through their union with Him, and that a Spirit would come to dwell with them and testify of Him and of the Father. All these discourses are gathered up in the amazing prayer, “That they all may be one, as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they may be one in us.”

Either those words contain the essence and meaning of the whole history, or the history must be rejected as the wickedest lie and most awful blasphemy ever imposed on the world. And if they do contain its meaning, that meaning must be embodied in acts. So the Evangelists go on to record, in calm, simple words, their Master’s death and resurrection. They are related as events, with no comment and few hints of effects to follow. The quiet, accurate detail makes us feel: “He certainly died, He whom we believed to be the Son of God and King of Israel; He actually rose in His body and came among us who knew Him, and spoke and ate with us. This is the accomplishment of the union between heaven and earth; it is no longer a word, it is a fact.” The risen Lord tells His Apostles to go into the world and testify of this fact, and not only to testify but to adopt men into a society grounded on its accomplishment. Linked with that command, as the final basis of the universal society, a name is proclaimed in which the name revealed to Abraham and the more awful one Moses heard in the bush are combined and reconciled.

For someone who has looked at the Gospel merely as a case of great doctrines or fine moralities, the Acts of the Apostles must be utterly unintelligible. The specimens of apostolic preaching it gives refer comparatively little to our Lord’s discourses or parables. They dwell mainly on the great acts of death and resurrection, as evidence that Jesus was the king, as explaining and completing the earlier history of the Jewish people, and as justifying and realising the truth working in the heathen mind, “that we are His offspring.” But a person who really looks on the Bible as the history of the establishment of a universal, spiritual kingdom, the kingdom God always intended for men, of which the universal kingdom then existing in the world was the formal opposite, will find in this book exactly what the earlier records would be meaningless without.

One might expect the narrator of events as transcendent as the ascension of the Son of Man into the invisible glory, or the descent of the Spirit to take possession of the feelings, thoughts, and utterances of mortal men, to stand still and wonder at what he so fully believes. But he does not. He regards these events as the necessary consummation of all that went before and the necessary foundation of the Church’s existence. So he can quietly relate other circumstances, however disproportionate they seem, that were needed for the Church’s outward manifestation and growth, such as the Apostles meeting in the upper room and the completion of their number. If the foundation of this kingdom were the end of all God’s purposes, the kingdom of God among men, its human conditions could be passed over no more than its divine ones. It was as needful to prove that the ladder had its foot on earth as that it had come down from heaven. As we go on, each step of the story leads us to see the Church as the child the Jewish polity had carried in its womb for so many ages. First its filial relation is shown: it is an Israelitic, not a mundane, commonwealth. Then it is shown that, though not mundane, it is essentially human, with a principle of expansion greater than the Roman empire’s.

Here lies the apparent contradiction, and the real harmony, between the two aspects in which the Apostles of the Circumcision and St. Paul viewed this kingdom. The one witnessed to its continuity, the other to its freedom from all national exclusions; these, we may believe, were their respective offices. Yet each, in fulfilling his own, was teaching the other truth most effectively. St. Peter and St. James maintained the Church’s universality while contending for its Jewish character and derivation. St. Paul maintained the national covenant while telling the Gentiles that if they were circumcised Christ would profit them nothing. Take away the first testimony and the Church becomes an earthly, not a spiritual, commonwealth, subject to earthly limits; take away the second and the promise to Abraham is unfulfilled. In another sense, as the canon of Scripture shows, St. Paul was more directly carrying out the spirit of the Jewish distinction by upholding separate ecclesiastical communities according to tribes and countries. And the Jerusalem Apostles were carrying out the idea of the Church’s universality more than he did, by addressing its members as one whole community scattered through different parts of the world.

But we must remember that while this universal society, in the historical conception, grew out of the Jewish family and nation, in the theological conception it is the root of both. Aristotle says, “That which is first as cause is last in discovery.” The letter to the Ephesians turns this formula into life and reality when St. Paul tells them they were created in Christ before all worlds, and says the transcendent economy was gradually revealed to the Apostles and Prophets by the Spirit. In this passage I think lies the key to the whole character of the dispensation and of the books that set it forth. Suppose the Gospel is the unveiling of a mystery hidden from ages and generations, and this mystery is the true constitution of humanity in Christ, so that a man believes and acts a lie who does not claim union with Christ. Then we can understand why the deepest New Testament writings are epistles rather than digests of doctrine. They explain to those already admitted into the Church their own position, bring out the side of it that bore on their circumstances or besetting sins, and show what life is consistent with it and what is at variance. We can understand why the first of these epistles, the one supposed most like a systematic treatise, opens by announcing a Gospel concerning Jesus Christ, who was made of the seed of David according to the flesh and marked out as the Son of God with power, according to the Spirit of holiness, by the resurrection of the dead. The fact of a union between Godhead and humanity is set forth as the one the Apostle was appointed to proclaim, the ground of the message to the Gentiles, and the implicit source of every idea of reconciliation, divine life, justification by faith, and sanctification by the Spirit. We can understand why his great fight with the Corinthians was over their raising certain notions, and certain men as representatives of them, into the place of the Lord of their fellowship, and why pride, sensuality, contempt of others, and abuse of ordinances followed necessarily from that sin. We can understand why he curses so vehemently the false teachers who denied the Galatians the right to call themselves children of God in Christ by the new covenant and sent them back to the old. We may see that his wonderful words to the Ephesians, that they sit in heavenly places in Christ Jesus, are as real and practical as the closing exhortations of the same letter on husbands and wives, fathers and children, and that the second are involved in the first. We may see the connection between his entreaty to the Colossians not to stoop to will-worship and the service of angels and his assertion that Christ is in them the hope of glory, the head in whom dwell all the riches of wisdom and knowledge. We may see how some of the Philippian Church could be enemies of the cross of Christ, their god their belly, their glory their shame, not because they had not been admitted as members of Christ but because they had not pressed on to realize their claim. We may enter a little into the idea of the Thessalonian letters, however we differ on the time or times of its accomplishment: that the mystery of iniquity and the mystery of godliness must be manifested together, and that the two kingdoms, always in conflict, are at certain great crises brought into open collision. We shall not need evidence of the Apostolic authorship of the epistle to the Hebrews to be convinced that it unfolds the relations between the national and the universal dispensation, between the shadow and the substance of a Divine humanity. It contrasts what let the worshipper expect perfect admission into God’s presence with what actually admitted him, and what revealed God as the enemy of evil with what revealed Him as its conqueror. Nor is it inconsistent with what we have seen that its writer should present men’s sin everywhere as unbelief in the blessings they are brought into at each stage of the Divine manifestation. With solemn earnestness, and with warnings of a fearful, hopeless apostacy, he urges his readers to believe that their position is the one all former ages aspired to, and to claim it. From these exhortations the transition is easy to the Catholic epistles, which some have found so hard to reconcile with St. Paul. And surely, if the faith that Romans and Hebrews press on men with such grand promises and dire threats were not faith in a living Being who adopted men into fellowship with Himself so that, being righteous by that union, they might do righteous acts, and so that, having claimed their place as members of a body, the Spirit might work in them to will and to do of His good pleasure, then the assertions that faith without works saves and that faith without works cannot save are hopelessly irreconcilable. But suppose the idea of St. Paul, as much as of St. James, is that faith has all its worth in so far as it unites us to an object and raises us out of ourselves, and no worth at all as a mere property in ourselves. If this is the very principle the whole Bible develops, one hardly sees what either position would be good for without the other. If our Lord came to bring men into a kingdom of righteousness, peace, and joy, grounded on fellowship with a righteous and perfect Being, then the notion that righteousness can ever belong to any man in himself, and the notion that everyone is not to show its fruits in himself, seem equally contradictory. Without this consideration, I believe, we shall be as puzzled by the sketch of a Christian man’s life, discipline, and conflicts in the epistle of St. Peter, and by St. John’s doctrine that love is the consummation of all God’s revelations and all man’s strivings, as by any earlier part of the book. For every verse of the first teaches that men do not gain a kingdom hereafter but are put in possession of it now, and that through chastisements and the oppositions of their evil nature they learn its character and enter its privileges. And the second affirms again and again in express words that love has been manifested to men, that they have been brought into fellowship with it so that by that fellowship they may rise to the enjoyment of it, and that this fellowship is for us as members of a family, so that he who loves God must love his brother also. With such thoughts in mind, I believe we may venture, hoping for the deepest instruction, on the study of the last book of the Bible. We may not be able to decide which of the chronological speculations about it is least untenable, or whether it speaks of the future or the past, or whether it describes a conflict of principles or of persons. But of this we shall have no doubt: at one period or through all periods it shows a real kingdom of heaven upon earth. Its principle must always be the same, and all kingdoms are meant to be subject to it. It maintains itself against an opposing tyranny whose ultimate law is brute force or unalloyed selfishness. And it must prevail, because it rests upon a name that expresses perfect Love and ineffable Unity, the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.
