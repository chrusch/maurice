Part I. On the Principles of the Quakers and of the Other Religious Bodies Which Have Arisen Since the Reformation, and on the Systems to Which They Have Given Birth

Chapter III. Unitarianism

Rooted in Protestant dogmatism and Lockean empiricism, Unitarianism’s sincere witness to God’s unity and universal love curdles, on its own logic, into a system denying the atonement and emptying the name Father of sense.

Modern English paraphrase. Maurice's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

Connexion of Unitarianism with pure Protestantism, with Natural Philosophy, and with the System of Locke—Its positive side—Its negative side—Final results.

I said that the early Quakers accepted many doctrines other Christians accepted, but that the sense in which they held them was fixed by their own distinctive tenets. The same cannot be said of the Reformers. The doctrines they did not originate, which their Romanist opponents professed, and which the Eastern as well as the Western Church held with certain important differences, were the Trinity and the Incarnation. These stood prominently in the Protestant confessions. Luther at least regarded them as the primary doctrines of Christianity, and regarded his own great principle as the link joining them to the distinct personality of each man.

But what was not true, or only partly true, of the founders was emphatically true of their successors, whether of the spiritual or the dogmatic school. The spiritual school always named Election, Justification by faith, and the authority of the Written Word as the vital truths, those belonging to personal religion. When they mentioned the Trinity, it was to say that every true Christian must recognise a Creator, a Redeemer and a Sanctifier. These offices were needed for his salvation, so he must ascribe them to distinct agents, and hence the principle had to be admitted. But a thought would arise: these offices are certainly distinct, but does it follow that there is a distinction of persons? Might that notion be a mere attempt to explain a variety of operations, which some less difficult hypothesis could account for? The humble and pious might repel the suggestion, but bolder spirits would voice it, and what the fathers dreaded as a temptation the sons welcomed as a discovery.

The dogmatic school spoke differently. They held that Scripture teaches the doctrine, that it belongs to the confessions, and that it is as necessary as any other part. But another difficulty arose. Were the texts cited for so strange and incomprehensible a doctrine really adequate? Had not the Romanists helped keep the belief alive through their traditions? Was it consistent with Protestantism to accept such help? These questions were asked, and the answers from the doctors of the Evangelical and Reformed Churches grew daily fainter and more incoherent.

I have already shown how, in the Calvinistic bodies from the first, and in the Lutheran so far as they took on the purely Protestant character, the Incarnation lost the place it held in the Church’s older divinity. The condition of humanity was determined by the fall. Only the pure, elect body had any concern in the Redemption, so that redemption could only be seen as a means God devised to deliver one portion of His creatures from the law of death that bound the race. The divinity of the most purely Protestant writers consisted in explaining how this redemption worked and in justifying its limits. For that purpose they used the arguments from the schools and the illustrations from the market-place that I described in the last chapter.

Meanwhile a great change had come over men’s notions on subjects not obviously theological. The experimental philosophy in physics promised students real knowledge of things by freeing them from the impressions of the senses and from the notions the understanding generalises out of those impressions. It had already borne its noblest fruit: the Astronomer had asserted a principle as true which most flatly contradicted the senses and every conclusion drawn from them.

But if experimental philosophy was the great means to such discoveries, did it not follow that Experience was the one source of knowledge? The conviction grew stronger: “There is no other, there can be no other.” Clever men then explained how many false systems came from supposing some other foundation of knowledge, and each exposure drew from the enlightened world another peal of laughter at its forefathers’ absurdities. Thoughtful men in various parts of Europe did notice that the new doctrine, which seemed to have grown up alongside the great experiments, had led to exactly the opposite result. Physical science had advanced, or rather been found possible, only so far as it freed itself from sensible impressions and the notions deduced from them. Yet moral science was believed to be nearing perfection by accepting those same impressions and notions as the only standard of truth. Such remarks were little heeded. It became the first tenet of philosophical orthodoxy, and the most dangerous to dispute, that sensible experience is the foundation of all belief and knowledge.

This philosophical theory rose historically alongside a great political theory, which also meant to displace all that went before. To make men tremble at doctrines that contradicted their experience, it was said, one had to make them tremble at the authority that delivered them. A mystery was supposed to attach to the origin of society as well as to the origin of knowledge, and both ideas were equally false. Knowledge comes in the simplest way, through eyes and ears; society likewise grew up in the simplest way, by compacts and conventions. Experience was the root of both. Men either felt the miseries of fighting or dreamed of the blessings of government. They gave up their privilege of being independent units and either yielded passively to someone stronger or made terms with him to rule them until his rule became burdensome.

We must weigh all this if we want to understand the temper of the last age and the scheme that gained so much secret or open acceptance in it. To suppose that Unitarianism has nothing positive in it, and owes all its popularity to its denials, is a plausible but serious mistake. Earnest minds embraced it who could never have sympathised with a system merely because it rejected what other men believed. I do not say they felt no delight in that peculiarity, or that being different from the common mass did not flatter them, as it flatters the evil nature of everyone. Their opponents confused the positive and negative elements of their minds, and in the end they may have confused them hopelessly themselves. But I maintain that something deeper and more solid lay beneath their not-believing, and that it matters to know what it was, for their sake and ours. The only way to extricate a man from a falsehood is to do justice to his truth; and by this course the history of the Church and God’s plans, so far as we may examine them, become far more intelligible.

1. The natural philosopher was free alike from the dogmatic tendencies of one class of Protestant theologians and from the disposition of another class to exalt and all but deify the modes and experiences of their own minds. If he was a simple, humble man, trained from youth in worship and in connecting deep and holy thoughts with God’s presence, his vocation would not lessen his awe and reverence. It would call them forth; he might even believe they were first given him when the marvellous distinctions and interwoven harmonies of creation opened to him. At any rate he was in a new, freer, and seemingly more real world than that of experiences and notions, one that bore more immediate and naked witness to a Divine Being. Only afterwards did this witness take the form of arguments and demonstrations. A scientific mind naturally clothes all thoughts in the forms it is used to, and treats the idea of God as one of the certainties those forms suit. But the heart and conscience spoke first. The testimony was received where it was needed before the slow machinery of proofs was built to justify the assumption, and the spirit bowed and worshipped, with mingled fear and joy, hearing in the world outside the echoes of a nearer and deeper voice.

So nature spoke to one raised in a Christian atmosphere as it might have spoken to some wondering sage of Greece or India. It seemed to bring news of a simpler, earlier, more universal faith, which belongs to all and which all may receive. Other testimonies might be added to confirm or restore it, but no true testimony could set it aside or contradict it. Therefore our Scriptures were to be prized, and all Shasters, the sacred books containing the mythologies of the old or new world, were to be rejected. The first were affirming and re-establishing this primary testimony; the others outraged it. At the root of all their teaching lay the belief in a Being not manifested in outward forms but in His works, and not divided according to the variety of His operations but one. Since Christianity’s universality had superseded the narrowness of Judaism, this belief must be asserted all the more clearly. It would be strange if the universal religion were more wrapped up in particular notions than the ancient one, which testified so strongly against idolatry as a limit on the Divine Presence and a division of His essence. It would be strange if the more perfect religion threw us back on the very notions the imperfect one had freed its faithful followers from. By such feelings and arguments the idea of God’s unity gradually rose up in the last age against the faith recognised in Christendom for seventeen centuries. Where lay their force? Surely in the strong inward conviction that the unity of God is a deep, primary truth. No words can explain it away, no experiences of ten thousand minds make it unreal, no dogmas of ten thousand generations turn it into nothing. It has held its ground against all of these, and everything true in men’s teaching has tended to bring it into clearer light. Joined to this was another conviction, less clearly drawn out but perhaps stronger for being latent: that the unity of God must somehow be the ground of all unity among men, and the root of any universal religion. No one should dare trifle with such convictions. Rather he should labour to draw them out into strength and clearness, using all history, and especially the history of Unitarianism in the last century, to illustrate them.

2. A natural philosopher trained to reverence, free from petty vanity, and aloof from vulgar excitements is likelier than most to have a calm, cheerful temperament. His mind is not turned in on itself, so the evil within does not keep reminding him of its presence. His circumstances do not make him contemplate the sins of the world, and he is constantly occupied with serene and unchanging objects. The lessons he learned in childhood of a Being of perfect love and purity will return to him with special delight; every new fact will bring them home, and all nature will seem to beam with them. But then he will remember other childhood lessons that seemed to contradict these, about justice, vengeance, and schemes for removing or appeasing wrath. Of a Being with such attributes, who must be approached in such a way, nature says nothing. There may be tempests and volcanoes, but all her operations, so far as we can penetrate them, obey fixed laws, and these too will be found to obey a law. He to whom we refer all creation and all laws must have a mind perfectly at one with itself, subject to no change, the same yesterday, today and for ever. Here again the pure original testimony to God’s universal love has been darkened by human conceits and systems. But our Bible reasserts that testimony, and is distinguished by it, as much as by its assertion of the divine unity, from all pretended revelations. The Old Testament denounces the heathens for setting it aside by their cruel inventions. Jesus Christ, by His words and acts, condemned the Jews for not honouring God as the God of mercy and love. His dispensation banishes every other idea; the beloved disciple affirms in words that God is love; all sacrifices and institutions that interfere with that notion are expressly abolished. By such feelings and arguments thousands in the eighteenth century, openly or secretly, came to believe that the idea of Atonement, assumed for seventeen centuries to be Christianity’s radical idea, was a wretched, inconsistent graft from some other stock. Where lay their strength? In the conviction, it seems to me, that the love of God is an absolute primary idea. It cannot be reduced under any other or explained away by any other. No records, experiences or dogmas, though they have lasted a thousand generations, can weaken or contradict it; it must be the foundation of all thought, theology and human life. I believe it is as dangerous to trifle with this conviction as with the one about the divine unity.

3. I have described the natural philosopher as withdrawn from the evils of the world around him, and to a great extent from his own, and disposed by his circumstances to a kindly view of things. How pleasant for such a man, coming from his study and his problems with a mind fixed and abstracted but not out of harmony, to look at his children and recall what he was told in the nursery: that He who made the sun and moon was their Father. How pleasant, when he had time to think of all the generations who had looked on that sun and moon, to believe the same of them. But what jarring thoughts from the same nursery would intrude! All these children of men, all these generations, have undergone a fall and are under a curse. Of only a few, and how few if divines’ calculations are right, can we think “these are God’s children.” All the rest we can only call doomed, and apparently not for their own sin but by inevitable necessity. Surely this too is one of the wretched interpolations into the old, simple faith. Nature teaches no such lesson: the same sunshine and rain for all, the whole universe claimed for its Creator. And the Bible does mean this, must mean it, whatever divines say to the contrary. Somehow it does reconcile man’s existence with its witness to God’s love; in this way it cannot. By such feelings and arguments the doctrine of a Fall was driven from the hearts of thousands in the eighteenth century. All Christendom had admitted it for seventeen centuries, and the Protestant sects counted it the central doctrine of Christian divinity.

Against these convictions the orthodox of the day, especially the Calvinists, offered many plausible arguments. Belief in God’s unity, they said, was somehow compatible with belief in the Trinity, but we should not trouble ourselves to reconcile them. The subject was profoundly mysterious, and we could not hope for light on it when we know next to nothing about so many matters of common life. The Bible required the belief, numerous texts could be explained on no other hypothesis, and abandoning it meant abandoning much more, even truths it was Protestantism’s peculiar glory to assert.

The principles about the Divine character were disposed of in a similar way, only with more logical and metaphysical subtlety. It was questioned whether the names given to the attributes of one wholly divine and incomprehensible mean the same as those names do when they describe qualities in us. It was said that we depend wholly on revelation for our knowledge of God, and that if the Bible ascribes certain acts and feelings to Him we must simply accept them and wait for their explanation in a future state. It was maintained that justice is as essential to God’s perfection as mercy.

To the notions about the fall, various answers were given. First, many passages of Scripture were said to assert the universality of human depravity; second, experience attested it; third, it was not incompatible with a natural conscience in man; fourth, it was the consequence of a wilful act by the first man; and fifth, the visiting of that act’s consequences on his posterity could be defended by many human analogies.

These arguments came in various forms and with varying ability, but two barriers stood against them that they were powerless to break down. The first was the strong feeling I have described, that these principles about God and man are great ultimate principles. They cannot be denied without denying everything true and solid, and unless they receive the most distinct and solemn acknowledgment, all the rest of our belief becomes confused and false. To raise any specific argument or set of texts against them was to lose, for the sake of life, the very reasons for living: to set aside by reasoning the first witness of reason, and by Bible passages the very end and substance of the Bible. This was the irresistible protection of the Unitarian system in the minds of earnest men who had embraced it. A second, equally secure defence served persons of the opposite character. It lay in the doctrine Locke proclaimed. They did not hold it with a full, clear, steady view of the doctrine and all its results, for then Hume’s conclusion stared them in the face: “There is no warrant for acknowledging any being, eternal, immaculate, invisible; for of no such being do the senses, or experience arising from the senses, furnish an indication.” Rather they held a loose, popular impression about the doctrine, a feeling spread through society that experience was in general the only root of knowledge and that you were not to believe much that you could not establish by it. Both those who assailed Unitarianism and those who defended it commonly accepted this philosophy, and it made the assailants’ arguments look strangely inconsistent. They were simply affirming that something was revealed to man which, by his nature as they understood it, could not be revealed to him. He was to learn and receive what he could not learn and receive. Even the Papists, the Unitarians rightly said, had not demanded that; in the days when Popery made its great demands on men’s faith, this doctrine of experience was not understood. And you urge this demand on us, they went on, at a time when the very idea of a body able to make it has been scattered to the winds. A Catholic Church, if there could be such a thing, a body with divine and mysterious endowments, might with some consistency claim assent to a mysterious dogma. But your Confession of Augsburg has dispersed that dream. You have set up societies you may call churches or what you will, but they do not and cannot claim authority over the world. They may lay down what canons they please for their own members, with the same right of private legislation, however absurd, that any other corporation has. But the only warrant for imposing an inexplicable creed on mankind is gone. Each portion of mankind has its own habits, maxims and opinions, and each man his own judgments, which he has a right to exercise and defend against the world. There are a few common principles admitted alike by saint, savage and sage, but these, far from being identical with the incomprehensible doctrines, are the very reverse of them.

Surely no opinions more plausible were ever promulgated, and none that seemed to carry a more natural and less painful demonstration. To divines they seemed a deliverance from the strangest intellectual confusions; to easy, comfortable men, the removal of an inexplicable burden from their consciences; to those who wished to be philosophical, the satisfaction of their longing; to those who disliked extremes, a convenient refuge from the difficulties of belief and the dreariness of infidelity. But the more such persons crowded into Unitarianism’s ranks, generally not by openly renouncing their former creeds but by habitually and practically confessing disbelief in them, the more startled and confounded were those who had adopted it on the other, more positive grounds. With deep awe they had acknowledged the Unity of God as the unfathomable foundation of thought, faith and being. Now they heard that unity asserted not as mysterious and unfathomable but as the escape from mystery. It was a purely material notion, and every argument for it came from the impossibility and contradiction a Trinity presents when considered materially. But where, they asked themselves, is unity in matter? Is not matter infinitely divisible? Can this be the way to escape contradictions, or to be rational? We throw aside everything but materialism and dismiss every thought beyond it, and then we are asked to recognise an idea which matter hardly even indicates, let alone realises! Such thoughts brooded in their minds and led them, by slow processes and bitter conflicts, to the conviction: “If the Unity of God is to be asserted it must be asserted on quite different grounds from those the so-called Unitarians have chosen, and the true assertion of it may possibly be contained in those creeds which we have rejected.”

On the popular supporters of Unitarianism such arguments made no impression. They probably received them with indignation. What! they would have said, do you suppose we meant a metaphysical unity? We meant to escape all subtleties; the Bible is written for simple people. I have hinted already that this language was unfortunate. They appealed unto Caesar, and unto Caesar they must go. They wished to be tried by simple people, and it remained to be seen whether anything in their scheme drew a response from simple people’s hearts. But before that experiment was made, the more thoughtful disciples began to notice another strange contradiction between the principles on which Unitarianism rested and the system that embodied them. Unitarians were the great assertors of God’s absolute, unqualified love, against all the mythologies and theologies that had gone before. Yet Unitarianism was the first theology or mythology to deny that the Almighty, in His own person, by some act of condescension and sacrifice, had stepped in to redress the evils and miseries of His creatures. Every pagan religion had acknowledged the need of an incarnation. The modern Jew and Mahometan, though nominally rejecting it, continually dream of it and testify to its necessity. It was left to this religion to make it the greatest proof of love in a Divine Being that He merely pardons those who have filled the world with misery. He has never shared in it or wrestled with it, and has devised no means of delivering mankind from it except sending a wise teacher.

Again, consider a man who really cherished the thought “God is a universal Father; His creatures cannot be merely subjects of a curse.” What a strange reflection must have come to him: “Yet according to the doctrines I hold, He is not and cannot be a Father. The word means nothing; it is a lazy, inappropriate synonym of Maker. It is the very glory of my creed to do what no other has done: first, to deny that there is any human bond between men and God; second, to deny that they have in themselves any capacity, different from an animal’s, of receiving impulses from God.”

Once more: according to Unitarianism, Christ came to purify men’s false notions of morality, to establish religion on the basis of morality, and to reveal another world than the present. These were the objects the reformers of His doctrine were to keep in sight, and for them they were to desire the removal of all systems and institutions that had kept alive a false faith and a distorted notion of God’s character. “But who,” the disappointed disciple of this school asked, “are the great helpers in this work of reformation? Who show most longing for its accomplishment? Are they men of deep thought and high devotion, who have pored sadly over the condition of society and cried out in solitary chambers, How long, O Lord? Are they even poor men, not aiming at some high standard, but feeling the burden and oppression of the universe, and believing that God could not have meant so many of His creatures to live and die without comfort or hope? Or are they not rather, for the most part, men of a quiet, decent, level tone of mind and character, from the easy, respectable, prosperous classes, who are actually impatient of anything that reminds them of an elevated, supersensual morality or a society based on self-sacrifice?”

Alas! he will have said, is it only for this that I have parted with all the dreams of my childhood? In my infancy I thought a kingdom of righteousness, peace and joy had been set up in the world, and that I was to wait and hope until it ruled over all. The glorious discovery of my manhood is that there is no such kingdom here. There is nothing but a world, in which men are to observe certain rules of behaviour toward one another, to keep within the rules of prudence for their own sakes, and to cheer themselves with the prospect of a future world, unknown and undefined, where they will be rewarded if they have not broken social decorums and forgiven if they have.

Such a picture of the system’s tendencies and ultimate results must often have presented itself to those who had embraced it with affection, as a deliverance from the dryness and narrowness of Calvinism and a witness to the unity and love of God. But these thoughts would have stirred powerfully in only a few minds if a series of strange movements had not taken place in European society. Some of them must have seemed most promising to Unitarians, but they really destroyed the whole credit of the system. They deprived it of the patronage of nobles and prelates, and gave it no substitute in the sympathies of either the thoughtful or the poor.
