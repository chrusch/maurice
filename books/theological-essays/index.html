The essays began as sermons preached to Maurice’s own congregation between Quinquagesima and Trinity Sunday, recast as essays to meet a request left in the will of a friend, once a Quaker, that he write something for Unitarians, the community in which he had grown up. They move from charity, sin and the evil spirit, through the Incarnation, the Atonement and the Resurrection, to the Judgment Day, the unity of the Church and the Trinity.

The concluding essay’s argument that “eternal” in the New Testament describes the life of God, not endless duration, led to Maurice’s dismissal from King’s College, London. The book is dedicated to Tennyson, and this edition includes the preface Maurice wrote for the third edition in 1871.

Contents

The one-line summaries are guides drafted with the help of AI, not Maurice’s words.

  1. Dedication

    Maurice dedicates the Essays to Tennyson, whose poetry taught him to enter human thoughts and feelings, and to Tennyson’s infant son, hoping controversies now raging will have ceased before the boy is grown.

  2. Advertisement

    A bequest from a lady who had once been a Quaker, meant to bring about a book addressed to Unitarians, is at last answered in these essays, grown from sermons preached between Quinquagesima and Trinity Sunday.

  3. Preface to the Third Edition

    Replying to Tennyson’s wish for a ‘larger hope,’ Maurice argues that only the old Creeds, and a Trinity rightly ordered from Father to Son to Spirit, save men from despair over sin and hell.

  4. Contents
  5. Essay I. On Charity

    Because Scripture and the Prayer-book set Charity above Faith, theology should begin not from disputed dogmas but from the belief that God himself is Charity, the ground of the whole created order.

  6. Essay II. On Sin

    Sin is not a breach of law or social rule but the felt presence of an inescapable inward tormentor, from which a man can only be freed by confessing ‘Father, I have sinned against Thee.’

  7. Essay III. On the Evil Spirit

    Weighing three explanations of evil—bad circumstances, inherited corruption, a personal tempter—the Gospel’s belief in an Evil Spirit of selfishness answers conscience’s sense of bondage without making God the author of sin.

  8. Essay IV. On the Sense of Righteousness in Men, and Their Discovery of a Redeemer

    Job’s paradox—confessing deep sin yet clinging to an unshaken righteousness—is resolved when he cries that his Redeemer lives, a Christ present in every man as the root of light before Bethlehem.

  9. Essay V. The Son of God

    Though every mythology has its sons of gods, the true universal Redeemer whom Job knew and whom John calls the only-begotten Son alone secures the freedom that pagan demigods only promise.

  10. Essay VI. The Incarnation

    Against Gnostic teachers who denied God could take flesh, be tempted, or care for the whole race, the Incarnation alone lets us know God, proves righteousness stronger than temptation, and redeems body with soul.

  11. Essay VII. On the Atonement

    A theology built from guilty consciousness bred Rome’s penances and a wrathful legal scheme alike; true Atonement is Christ’s sinless sacrifice reconciling man to a Father whose will and love never changed.

  12. Essay VIII. The Resurrection of the Son of God from Death, the Grave, and Hell

    Christ’s death, burial, descent into hell, and resurrection are facts, not ideas: sharing our death ends its isolation, and his rising promises body as well as soul redeemed, not mere relics reassembled.

  13. Essay IX. On Justification by Faith

    Rome’s despair over post-baptismal sin and Calvinism’s divide between elect and reprobate both arose from sorting the righteous from the wicked; true Justification rests on God raising Christ as the root of every man’s righteousness.

  14. Essay X. On Regeneration

    Answering Combe’s physiology and completing Butler’s moral philosophy, Regeneration means neither a substituted nature nor mere restored order but Christ’s actual kingship over every man’s conscience, securing it the throne Butler could not give it.

  15. Essay XI. On the Ascension of Christ

    Galilean fishermen, far less imaginative than the Greeks, proclaim Christ’s actual ascension in the body, and the Church grows earthly and superstitious whenever it trades that fact for a mere symbol.

  16. Essay XII. The Judgment Day

    Christ’s judgment-seat is not set up for the first time on some distant day but is one before which every heart stands now, and his coming again means his being manifested as he is.

  17. Essay XIII. On Inspiration

    The same Spirit who inspired the prophets and apostles now inhabits every praying believer, so Scripture’s inspiration cannot be walled off from the Spirit given to the whole Church at Pentecost.

  18. Essay XIV. On the Personality and Teaching of the Holy Spirit

    Sin, righteousness, and judgment are convicted in every generation by a real, personal Comforter, whose steady presence alone explains why Christian history holds both its darkest crimes and its highest sanctity.

  19. Essay XV. On the Unity of the Church

    Rome, the Protestant nations, and the sects each betray the unity a Pentecostal Spirit already gives them, not because that Spirit was withheld but because none has truly believed it was given.

  20. Essay XVI. On the Trinity in Unity

    The Father’s righteous will, revealed in a Son who unites men to himself, and made known through a Spirit who imparts holiness, together ground true prayer and constitute the eternal life that is knowing God.

  21. Concluding Essay Eternal Life and Eternal Death

    Eternal life and eternal death mean the presence or loss of the knowledge of God, not unending ages of bliss or torment, so treating endless punishment as rigid dogma betrays the gospel of love.

  22. Note on the Athanasian Creed

    Maurice defends keeping the Athanasian Creed, arguing its severe language condemns not honest doubt about the Trinity but the inward denial of the Father, Son, and Spirit that is eternal death itself.
