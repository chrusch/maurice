The Religions of the World and Their Relations to Christianity

The Boyle Lectures

Maurice delivered these eight lectures under the terms of Robert Boyle’s will, which endowed sermons “for proving the Christian Religion against notorious Infidels.” He takes the charge in an unexpected direction. Rather than cataloguing the errors of other faiths, the first four lectures try to understand Mahometanism, Hindooism, Buddhism and the “defunct religions” of Egypt, Persia, Greece and Rome from within, asking what truth each lived by and what gave it its power over men.

The second four lectures set each beside Christianity, arguing that the Christian faith fulfils what each was reaching for rather than simply refuting it. The preface discusses the sources Maurice drew on, from the Royal Asiatic Society’s transactions to Carlyle’s lecture on Mahomet.

Contents

The one-line summaries are guides drafted with the help of AI, not Maurice’s words.

  1. Contents
  2. Preface

    Maurice explains how these Boyle Lectures were prepared for print and weighs his debts to writers on the same ground, among them Forster and Carlyle on Mahomet, Colebrooke and Rammohun Roy on Hindooism, and Hodgson on Buddhism.

  3. Lecture I. Mahometanism

    Boyle founded these lectures from his own struggle with doubt, and Mahometanism spread not through conquest or imposture but by proclaiming one living, commanding God—a truth that curdles into fatalism once severed from love.

  4. Lecture II. Hindooism

    Hindooism’s caste system rests on the Brahmin’s claim to commune with Brahm, but the heart’s craving for a caring, suffering god produced Vishnu, Sheeva, and Krishna, proving that man needs revelation, not self-generated religion.

  5. Lecture III. Buddhism

    Buddhism grew alongside Brahminism from the same root, replacing caste with reverence for the human intellect, and in China filled the gap left by Confucian order—rightly heard, it is an unspoken prayer for revelation.

  6. Lecture IV. Defunct Religions

    Persia’s Ormuzd and Ahriman, Egypt’s hidden Ammon, Greece’s Apollo, Rome’s Jupiter, and the Gothic Odin all bear witness to one divine ground crying to become a person, though each vanished before that need was met.

  7. Lecture V. Judaism and Mahometanism

    The Old Testament’s living, speaking God underlies Judaism and Mahometanism, but where the Jew hardens into ritual and the Mahometan into sovereignty, Christianity completes their hope in a King who reigns by sacrifice, not will.

  8. Lecture VI. Relations of Hindooism with Christianity

    The Hindoo’s twice-born Brahmin, seeking union with Brahm, and his terror before Sheeva the Destroyer find fulfilment in Christ, who reconciles Preserver and Destroyer and turns sacrifice into a son’s offering to a loving Father.

  9. Lecture VII. Relations of Buddhism with Christianity

    The Buddhist’s reverence for a universal spirit manifest in the Lama has its truer counterpart in the Spirit given at Pentecost, though Buddhism starts from man’s assumed divinity, Christianity from the Father’s free gift.

  10. Lecture VIII. How Christianity Assailed the Defunct Systems

    That Mahometanism and Hindooism stand after centuries of Christian preaching is not a disproof but a rebuke, exposing truths—God’s dominion over the visible world, the reality of an unseen kingdom—that Christians have forgotten.
