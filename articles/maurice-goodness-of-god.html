The Goodness of God in F. D. Maurice

This is a companion to The Goodness of God in George MacDonald on the George MacDonald Books site. F. D. Maurice builds on the same conviction as MacDonald: God is love, and the Father is exactly what the Son shows him to be. Maurice states it as the foundation of theology rather than as tenderness. His favourite text is “God is light, and in Him is no darkness at all,” and he holds to it against all the darkness of the world. The passages come from his sermons, lectures and essays, and each links to its paragraph in these editions.

Love the ground of everything

Maurice’s first claim is about method. The Church does not reason up to God’s love from our need of it. It starts from that love and reasons down.

The Church starts from the highest ground, does not work its way up to that ground from the lowest… her first duty is to assert the love of God absolutely, apart from all consequences that may flow out of it, from all contemplation of it or returns to it by the creature… The passages, “God so loved the world,” “God is love,” are not rapturous utterances of a devotional sentiment; they are the scientific foundation of a Catholic and Evangelical Theology.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Passion Week; its Dangers and Blessings”, ¶9

‘God is love.’ This is not an accident of His character, but its essence; not an aspect which it wears at certain times or to some fortunate persons, but that which is the same yesterday, to-day, and for ever, that which has no respect of persons.

— The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XV, “Perfected Love”, ¶8

Faith, because it is Faith, must have something to rest upon. And that something is Love. If Love was not the ground of all things, there could be no Faith; there must be everlasting distrust…

— The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XVI, “Faith and Love”, ¶11

In a prayer for those killed in the Indian Mutiny, he says that God’s love does not depend on our believing it:

Thanks be to Thee, Thy fatherly love to me is not determined by my sense of it, my belief of it, else what would become of me! It cannot be determined by the sense of it, by the belief of it, in any creature. It must be higher than heaven, deeper than hell,—how can I grasp it? I am content to cast myself upon it, to cast all upon it.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”, ¶6

The Father seen in the Son

As in MacDonald, the Father is just like Jesus. Maurice says it most plainly to his village congregation:

Jesus, the Son of Man, came to show us who the true God is. He came, as He said from God to do the works of God. He said that God was not a tyrant, but a Father; and that in Him they might see the Father. And it is in Him, brethren, that you and I must see the Father, and only in Him. If we try to think of God without thinking of the Son of Man, we shall get all astray, and suppose Him to be another Being altogether from what He is.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon VI, “The Sick of the Palsy”, ¶13

He was the express image of His Father. All the brightness and goodness and loving-kindness of His Father came forth in Him. Every act of His showed forth something of this brightness and goodness and loving-kindness. He went about, as it is written, doing good.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”, ¶7

The perfect Son, by His obedience, had revealed the perfect Father; the Absolute Goodness had come forth in all the relations and sympathies of the man. One act of self-assertion, of self-glorification, would have dimmed the image, would have made the vision of truth and love which was presented to the creature imperfect. There was no such act.

— The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XIV, “Christ’s Sacrifice a Power to Form Us After his Likeness”, ¶10

Because the Father is like the Son, there can be no conflict of attributes in God and no reluctant Judge for the Son to win over:

Jesus said, “He that hath seen Me, hath seen the Father.” Did any one see in Him that warfare of Truth with Mercy which we have so rashly dreamed of in the eternal Mind?… He showed that Mercy and Truth were divided only by the evil which seeks to destroy both.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God or in the Transactions of Men”, ¶12

The mysteries of Christmas Day and of Passion Week are not severed from each other: one as much as the other is presented as proceeding from the Eternal Absolute Love of the Father… He is wringing no hard consent from a reluctant Judge, to pardon rebels. He is bringing down into this nature of man that love which had created man,—that love from which man by selfwill had become separated.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Passion Week; its Dangers and Blessings”, ¶13

Goodness that stoops and seeks

For Maurice the test of goodness is whether it can come down to us:

We want to see absolute Goodness and Truth. We want to know whether they can bend to meet us. That which cannot do this is not what we mean by Goodness. It is not what we should call goodness in any man… The goodness which can stoop most, which becomes, in the largest sense, grace,—the truth which can speak to the inmost heart of the dullest human creature, is that which has for us the surest stamp of divinity.

— Theological Essays, Essay VI, “The Incarnation”, ¶9

The Bible, he says, is the story of God looking for us, not of us looking for God:

They do not set forth the history of a man seeking for God, but of God seeking for men.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “National Holiness”, ¶8

They did not know that it was a God of perfect love from whom they had separated themselves. They did not know that He was their Father. They did not know that He was seeking after them to bring them into reconciliation. They did not know that all their yearning after Him was awakened by Himself.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon VII, “The Marriage Feast of the King’s Son”, ¶8

He, from whom they were turning away, to shun whose presence they would have taken the wings of the morning, or have made their bed in hell, presented Himself to them as one who was seeking after them, claiming them as His children, sending His own Son to earth, to death, to the grave, to hell, that He might fetch them home… It was the love of a Father, exhibited as Love only can be exhibited, in sacrifice; it was the love of a Brother, reflecting that love, consummating the sacrifice in death.

— The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XIII, “Christ’s Sacrifice the Peace-Offering for Mankind”, ¶7

Light with no darkness at all

This is Maurice’s characteristic text, and the first principle of his ethics:

This proposition, ‘God is light, and in Him is no darkness at all,’ is the proposition from which all others start… That demonstration in a Son leads on to the next proposition, that this divine light is not merely hidden inaccessible light; that it has shone out in a Person Who makes us understand what He is by doing human acts and bearing human sorrows.

— The Epistles of St. John, Lecture II, “The Ground of Christian Ethics”, ¶16

In St John’s vision the throne of God is not power but brightness:

It is not a revelation of crushing power, but of perfect, untroubled brightness, of light with no darkness at all.

— Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture IV, “The Vision of Heaven”, ¶6

Against the evidence

Maurice does not hold this because the world looks bright. A village sermon imagines St John, his master crucified, his brother James killed, Jerusalem falling, asking: “Must He not have some hard thought to us? Can He be altogether light, that and that only?” (¶5). Then he gives John’s answer:

I know that through all these things He held on; that He trusted, altogether trusted, in His Father’s goodness and truth; through all that darkness He trusted in Him because He knew Him, because He knew what His will was, because He was sure it was the same will always, even when He could not see it, even when it seemed to be crushing Him.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon XXIII, “The Light of the World”, ¶7

I mean to get my thoughts of the Eternal God from this Jesus Christ who has made Him known to us; from Him and from no one else, and from nothing else. Things may look very dark and black, but I am sure the darkness and blackness are not from God… Death is the great darkness; all light seemed to be put out by it. It never was so dark as when it closed over the Son of Man, but He went through it; He despised it. His Father brought Him from the grave and hell. Therefore death and hell shall not be my teachers about God.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon XXIII, “The Light of the World”, ¶8

He gave Himself to show that His Father was not the cause of the misery of the world, but that it was because it was at war with Him. He worked to show that death and the grave and hell were His Father’s enemies, and that His Father could and would vanquish them.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”, ¶7

After Cawnpore

On the day of humiliation for the Indian Mutiny in 1857, Maurice faced the massacre of innocents directly. He grants that no theory of optimism survives such news: “A general maxim or theory of the universe does not meet individual cases. It breaks down the moment the particular instance occurs to which we need that it should be applied” (¶7). His answer is not a theory but the Father:

Whence comes this horror of such evils? this consciousness of something directly, absolutely opposed to them?… Is there not, must there not be a Father of Spirits from Whom they issue forth, in Whom they dwell perfectly, absolutely? Is there not One Who is Justice, Righteousness, Truth, Love? I will arise and go to Him…

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”, ¶8

Thus, I believe, may the universal doctrine of optimism, being changed into the belief of a Father Who is Light and in Whom is no darkness at all, be reconciled with and confirmed by all those individual experiences which seem at first to contradict it.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, same sermon, ¶9

Reading Romans 8 on the suffering of the whole creation, he comes closer to MacDonald’s position than anywhere else. “The innocent, the Holy One” is Christ:

…all the sufferings to which the earth and those that inhabit it are liable, are permitted and designed for the education of those who bear the nature which the Son of God bore… that no trial or suffering which contributes to this end is, in the judgment of the All-good and the All-wise, excessive or wasted,—not even the sufferings and death of the innocent, the Holy One; but that this end being attained… all the forms of physical evil will also be overcome; the involuntary creation will be delivered from its fetters and its shame…

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Glory and Deliverance of the Animal Nature”, ¶14

The Father’s joy

God is not a cold receiver of homage. He is glad over his children:

Have we the least ground for thinking that He accepts coldly and distantly the homage of men’s hearts, and acts, and words; that He is not well pleased with it? Is not the joy with which any parent receives the free habitual service of a child striving to be dutiful, and the submission of one who has been undutiful, the faint image of the joy with which the Father of lights receives him who wishes to dwell at home, as well as the prodigal who has just recollected that he has a home to which he may return?

— The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon II, “Noah’s Sacrifice”, ¶11

We may remember that the Angels of God rejoice over one sinner that repents, because God rejoices. We may be sure that He, without whom a sparrow does not fall to the ground, does not lose sight of a soul which He has made.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Sealed Nation and the Universal Family”, ¶28

The father in the parable goes out to the elder brother too, and offers him his own joy:

‘Son, I have called thee to know my goodness and loving-kindness. I have called thee to be a dispenser of that knowledge to the children of men. I can give thee no greater treasure. I can make thee partaker of no higher bliss than my own… But before thou formest that terrible resolution I will come out and entreat thee. I will urge thee to partake of my festival. I will vindicate thy right to it. I will conjure thee to enter into thy Father’s blessedness.’

— The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XIX, “The Joy of the Kingdom of Heaven”, ¶19

And the goodness of God is not a memory but a present power, at work in us now:

But that goodness of God which is with us, is not merely something which we may recollect, by which we may profit. It is an active, vital power. It is the one power which can act upon spirit. It is acting continually upon our spirits. This goodness is the very Spirit of God; that Spirit Who, being goodness, grieves for our want of goodness; being the forbearance of God, bears with us; being the longsuffering of God, will not suffer us to destroy ourselves.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “God’s Government of Us; its Ground and its End”, ¶21

The Giver of daily things

Maurice is at his most homely when he preaches on the lilies and the ravens to a country congregation:

Believe you have not a cruel hard mammon-god over you, but a gracious, loving, tender Father, the very same that you pray to in the temple… So you see it must be God who is providing for you every minute, every hour. It must be He who is causing your babies to grow. It must be He who is giving you all that you want. Cannot you trust Him?

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon XVII, “God and Mammon”, ¶8

Yes, verily, brethren, and it is not me only who preaches to you of this Father. The birds of the air preach of Him, the flowers of the field preach of Him; all these say—‘The mammon-god did not create us; the Father of the Lord Jesus Christ created us. His love has called us into existence. His love watches over the life of every man and woman and child.’

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon XVII, “God and Mammon”, ¶12

He contrasts Christ’s preaching with the preachers who belittle life to frighten people into religion:

Our Lord takes the opposite course. ‘How glorious is life; how much grander than the mere meat which sustains it! How grand is the body; how much nobler than the raiment with which you clothe it! And do you think God is indifferent either to the food or the raiment of His creatures? Does He not feed the ravens? Does he not clothe the lilies? Are you afraid that He should ever grudge you what you want?’

— The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XVI, “The Fire Which is to Baptise the Nation and the King”, ¶20

The miracle of every morning as it rises out of night has testified to these. Not a child has been born, not a parent has died, in whom we might not have seen the traces of them. And the New Year is as certain to be full of them as the Old. It too will have its thunders to raise us out of sleep; it too will have its silent, hourly memorials of an unvarying, considerate, vigilant loving-kindness.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “God’s Government of Us; its Ground and its End”, ¶20

Forgiveness belongs to the same giving nature as rain:

He who taught us to look up to God as a Giver, not as an Exactor, and to pray for the bread which is needful for us, further commands us to ask for this freedom. He must mean that rain and fruitful seasons are not more a sign to men of what He is, than remission; that one is as much an utterance of His disposition and purpose as the other…

— The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI, “Forgive Us Our Debts”, ¶6

Love itself is received fresh each day, like dew:

From Him comes the original capacity to love, by Him it is daily renewed. The loving man has drawn his first breath of life from Him; he receives a fresh breath from Him each morning, as the flowers and trees receive a fresh birth with the rising sun and a fresh baptism in the dew.

— The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XIV, “Spiritual Powers in Old Times and in Modern Times”, ¶25

Love that must give itself

Maurice opens his Theological Essays with the claim that everything follows from:

“Charity is the ground and centre of the Universe: God is Charity,”…

— Theological Essays, Essay I, “On Charity”, ¶12

He argues from the Trinity that such love cannot keep to itself:

It is wonderful that the inmost life of God should be communicated; but it would be a contradiction that it should not be communicated. We cannot think of a Being of perfect love as wrapt up in Himself, as dwelling in the contemplation of His own excellence and perfection: we can as little think of His being satisfied with any lower excellence or perfection.

— Theological Essays, Essay XVI, “On the Trinity in Unity”, ¶15

And it is a love without boundaries:

…mine rests on the Eternal Love which overlooks all distinctions, which embraces the universe.

— Theological Essays, Essay VII, “On the Atonement”, ¶26

I believe that there was in the Apostle’s mind, at this time, a sense of opposites… of the absolute love of God, of the discovery of that love to man in the Mediator, of the working of that love in man through the Spirit, of the fellowship of the poorest creature of flesh and blood on earth with the spirits in Heaven, of a canopy of love above and of an abyss of love beneath compassing the whole creation, which could only find its expression in a prayer.

— The Unity of the New Testament, “Epistle to the Ephesians”, ¶21

Not a tyrant, and not the dark spirit

The false god Maurice fights most often is the god of mere power:

We cannot hallow the Name of God if we suppose power to be His most essential characteristic, or the manifestation of power to be His chief delight. This notion of Him is evidently fashioned out of our own low appetites and base fancies; it is the notion which lies at the root of the dark fables of heathenism.

— The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon IV, “Thy Will Be Done”, ¶6

Just what the Mahometan wants to understand is, that the God Who calls for his devotion and his perpetual warfare, is not a tyrant, but a Father; not One Who is hopelessly separated from His creatures, but One Who is eternally united with them; not One Who seeks their death, but their life…

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “A Straitened Faith and a Narrow Gospel Not the Strait Gate and the Narrow Way of Christ”, ¶20

We keep God at a distance, he says, because we want him there:

We desire that God should be at a distance from us—therefore we persuade ourselves that He is… But the Light is about us, however we may shut our eyes to it. And when we believe that God is not the tyrant we have taken Him to be—that He is that Father of whom Christ said: “He that hath seen Me hath seen the Father,”—when we believe this, we turn to the Light from which we have been turning away…

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, Sermon XLI, “Repentance and Conversion”, ¶14

His sharpest words are for the reading of Luke 12 in which the one to be feared, “which after he hath killed hath power to cast into hell,” is God himself:

We are come to such a pass as actually to suppose that Christ tells those whom He calls His friends not to be afraid of the poor and feeble enemies who can only kill the body, but of that greater enemy who can destroy their very selves, and that this enemy is—not the devil, not the spirit who is going about seeking whom he may devour, not him who was a murderer from the beginning,—but that God who cares for the sparrows! They are to be afraid lest He who numbers the hairs of their head should be plotting their ruin! Does not this interpretation… show us on the edge of what an abyss we are standing, how likely we are to confound the Father of lights with the Spirit of darkness?

— Theological Essays, Concluding Essay, “Eternal Life and Eternal Death”, ¶25

His doctrine of the devil exists to protect this goodness. Once we confess a perfectly good Being, evil cannot be woven into the nature of things:

Evil and good are so mixed in this world of ours, that it is a most natural thing for us to suppose them mixed by an eternal law. So soon as we begin to confess a Being of perfect goodness, that thought becomes blasphemous and incredible.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Devil and God”, ¶7

Goodness at war with evil

Maurice almost never names God’s goodness without naming its war on evil. He thinks love without it is not love at all:

Only think to what the idea of a Being of perfect love must reduce itself—to what it has actually reduced itself—when men have contemplated these two Divine attributes as contrary to each other. What does love become, but a weak, contemptible tolerance of that which is unlovely, a merciless mercy, which now and for ever can permit the creatures it has formed to be as sinful, that is to say, as miserable, as they will.

— The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Quaker”, ¶28

The apostles, he says, found God both nearer and more awful once they knew him in Christ:

The name God with us had become the dearest of all names to them. And yet the reverence for God, as the perfect Goodness, as the great enemy of all that was not good in them and the universe, had become immeasurably deeper than it had ever been. Why? Because the Father had sent the Son… the Son in whom that Being of absolute goodness delighted; because they had learnt to call Him, in Christ, their Father.

— The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XV, “Perfected Love”, ¶4

His version of the consuming fire is close to MacDonald’s:

The perfect goodness and purity will wage war against all that is evil and dark… That consuming fire which is in the lightning has another purpose than one of destruction. It is illuminating and life-giving. It comes forth from the Divine Being, the perfect Love…

— Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture IV, “The Vision of Heaven”, ¶9

The intense certainty that there is a God of Truth and without iniquity, and that He must mean to sweep all falsehood and all injustice from this earth, will make you desire, above all things, that He should search you and try your ways,—that He should detect whatever in you is yours and not His, and should burn that with unquenchable fire… The acknowledgment of a God who beareth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things,—who has been long-suffering with all His creatures and long-suffering with us,—will make us tremble to deal harshly with the struggles and doubts—how much more with the convictions—of our fellow-beings.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Hatred Necessary to Love”, ¶12

Where Maurice and MacDonald differ

On the substance they agree. God is love in his essence. The Father is exactly like the Son. There is no conflict between his justice and his mercy, and no darkness in him at all. The differences are of emphasis.

  • The key it is written in. MacDonald’s favourite word for God’s goodness is tenderness; Maurice’s is light. Maurice calls “God is love” the scientific foundation of theology, and argues for it from method, from the Trinity, and from conscience.
  • Goodness and righteousness. Maurice rarely names God’s goodness without his war on evil, and he warns that love without it becomes “a merciless mercy.” MacDonald says the same in “Justice” and “The Consuming Fire,” but he leads with the tenderness.
  • Homeliness. Maurice has less of MacDonald’s homely God, though not none. He is most homely in the Country Churches sermons, where the birds and flowers preach of the Father and God “is causing your babies to grow.”
  • Evil. Both deny that evil comes from God. Maurice protects God’s goodness by naming an Evil Spirit, so that evil is never part of the order of things. MacDonald protects it by holding that even pain serves the cure, so that “hell is God’s and not the devil’s.” Maurice’s sermon on the animal creation shows that he too can say that no suffering is “excessive or wasted” in the judgment of the All-good.
  • Suffering of the innocent. Maurice faces it most directly in the 1857 sermon after Cawnpore. He grants that no general theory survives such news. His answer is the same as MacDonald’s: turn to the Father whom Christ has shown, and let “death and hell” not be our teachers about God.
