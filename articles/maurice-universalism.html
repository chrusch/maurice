Is F. D. Maurice a Universalist?

In 1853 Maurice was removed from his professorship at King’s College, London, after the last of his Theological Essays, the one on eternal life and eternal death. He later reported, without disputing it, Dr. Candlish’s account of the case, in which he had been brought “before a college tribunal for corrupting the minds of young men about the one point of everlasting punishment” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter, ¶18). In that concluding essay he had written that he was “obliged to believe in an abyss of love which is deeper than the abyss of death”. Elsewhere he wrote that “we may trust” that the Father “will at last draw all men to Him”. Was he a universalist?

Verdict. If a universalist is someone who teaches as doctrine that every man will in the end be saved, Maurice is not one. He refuses to pronounce on it, and he names the reason: no one can measure “the possibilities of resistance in a human will to the loving will of God”. Nor does he hold the universalism his opponents feared, in which a set term of suffering pays off the sinner’s debt. Pain, for him, makes no amends for sin. He does hold, and says in more than one place, that suffering after death may change a sinner.

But he is not undecided. Everything he refuses to deny points the same way. God wills that all men be saved. That this will “shall one day be done” is language Maurice will not contradict, though he leaves it to God to justify. Nothing in God’s power over the will “fixes death as the limit of it”. If grace can overcome Maurice’s own resistance, no one may conclude that another man’s obstinacy is too strong for it. At its boldest the hope becomes a trust “that no sin, no selfishness, shall at last be found stronger than the divine love”. Maurice is a hopeful universalist in the strict sense of the phrase. He is sure of God’s love and will not pronounce on man’s end. He holds the hope as a duty, since to give it up would be to concede the universe to evil. He will not make it a dogma, just as he will not let its denial be made one.

1. What Maurice was fighting for

The concluding essay opens with the objection as Maurice’s critics put it. The Church, they say, “maintains the notion of everlasting punishment after death”, and on that notion the great majority of mankind must “be hopelessly doomed” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay: Eternal Life and Eternal Death”, ¶1). Maurice answers that this belief, placed at the centre of the Gospel, turns the Gospel upside down:

The message which, according to this view of the case, Christ brings from Heaven to earth is, “Your Father has created multitudes whom He means to perish for ever and ever. By my agony and bloody sweat, by my cross and passion, I have induced Him in the case of an inconceivably small minority to forego that design.” Dare we state that proposition to ourselves,—dare we get up into a pulpit and preach it?

— Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶26

But what he asks for is freedom, not a counter-doctrine. The Forty-Two Articles of Edward VI’s reign (1553), which Maurice calls “the first draft of our Articles” (¶16), condemned one form of the belief in a final restoration, and the Elizabethan revision struck that Article out. Those who wanted the Church to “dogmatise most peremptorily” on the subject explained the Article as a measure against the Anabaptists, meant only for its time. Maurice thinks the explanation reasonable, but he says plainly that it is theirs: “This statement is not mine.” Then he turns it against them: “Taking their explanation, the evidence that the members of the Church of England have perfect freedom on this subject, is irresistible” (¶18). His protest is against making endless punishment “the tenet of the Church to which every other is subordinate”. He wants it to remain a tenet “which any one is at liberty to hold”, provided no one forces it on others (¶32). A man who defends his opponents’ liberty to hold their view is not imposing the opposite view.

2. “Eternal” does not settle the question

Maurice’s main argument concerns the word eternal. He will not let it mean one thing of life and another of punishment, and he faults “the Unitarians and Universalists of the last age” for changing its force to suit themselves (¶7). He asks that it mean in every case what it means of God:

If it is right, if it is a duty, to say that Eternity in relation to God has nothing to do with time or duration, are we not bound to say that also in reference to life or to punishment it has nothing to do with time or duration?

— Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶10

Eternal life is “the knowledge of God, who is Love”, and eternal death is “the loss of that knowledge”, the state men “choose for themselves, and which consists in being at war with His Love” (¶19). Six years later, answering Henry Mansel’s Bampton Lectures, Maurice draws the consequence for the question of a final restoration:

There is a deliverance from eternal death, in the strict Scriptural sense of eternal death. Whether that deliverance is only in this world, whether the threescore years are barrier beyond which Christ’s Gospel does not reach, is a question which cannot be determined by the use of that word.

— What is Revelation?, Letter X, “Morality and Evil”, ¶41

This cuts both ways. The phrase “eternal punishment” proves neither that any man is lost for ever nor that any man will be restored. Maurice clears the ground, and he says the question still has to be studied “in the most serious temper” (¶41). He does not build a universalism on the word.

3. What Maurice rejects, and what he does not

The Edwardian Article condemned the view “that all men, be they never so ungodly, shall at length be saved, when they have suffered pain for their sins a certain time appointed by God’s justice” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶16). Maurice never condemns that view in his own voice. He calls the Article “expressed in the most moderate terms” (¶16). The explanation he gives of it, before he reports his opponents’ explanation, is the Reformers’ own principle. It was natural, he says, that men who had entered into the spirit of the Reformation should be anxious

to show that pain did not make amends for sin, and that the misery of sin does not consist in an arbitrary penalty affixed to it by God, who has sent His Son to make men righteous.

— Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶17

“On these grounds,” he adds, the Divines “might easily have excused themselves” for adopting the Article (¶17). The opening words of ¶17, “After what I have said of the character of the Reformation”, point back to his own account of Luther, who taught “that a man requires and desires punishment, not indulgence, when he has done evil” (¶14). The objection is Maurice’s as much as the Reformers’, and so far as the condemned view makes pain pay for sin, he thinks it applies. His opponents feared the same thing in its popular form: “a vague Universalism”, which would let sinners think “a certain amount of punishment will be enough to clear off their scores” (¶28). His survey of the Fathers is even-handed. It lists “a Universalist who dwelt on the possibility of men being restored, after ages of suffering, to the favour of God” among teachers who each caught one aspect of eternity, without singling that one out for blame (¶11).

Pain is not a payment. But that does not mean pain cannot heal. In the same essay Maurice writes with sympathy of the medieval Doctors who were “afraid to limit the love which they felt had been so mighty for them and for the world”, and who “would rather think of material punishments which might, elsewhere as here, be God’s instruments of acting upon the spirit to awaken it out of death”. He does not excuse the system that grew from this. The same conviction, he says, let them “wink with a dangerous ‘economy’” at the abuses of Purgatory. But he reads Dante’s Purgatory as “the ascent, not out of material torments, but out of moral evil, into a higher moral state” (¶13). Elsewhere he calls punishment “one of God’s blessed instruments for making us aware of sin” (What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶44), and section 6 shows him applying this beyond death. What he rejects is suffering as a price. Suffering as a cure he accepts.

What he will not allow is a day when God simply stops judging. On death and hell being cast into the lake of fire, he asks whether this means that “by some arbitrary decree of God, evil ceases to bring its curse and doom”:

I can form no conception which is so frightful as that. Evil can never be less accursed, till good becomes less blessed. Asking God not to judge, is asking utter destruction to ourselves. The victories of God are victories over the will of men. You and I can only be saved from curses and punishments by being saved from sin.

— Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XX, “The Millennium”, ¶25

Maurice holds as firmly as his opponents that “punishment is inseparable from evil, that God will never withhold His punishments while evil lasts” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XX, “The King Dismissing his Steward”, ¶28). Mansel had argued that sin may perpetuate itself beyond the grave. Maurice replies that he has “always taken for granted” that “sin perpetuates itself” and “that prolonged misery must be the offspring of prolonged guilt” (What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶44). Whatever Maurice hopes for, then, it cannot be release from punishment while the sin remains. Nor is it “a vague dream of bliss hereafter, into which righteousness and goodness do not enter” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶27), which he thinks no more able to awaken men to a better life than the old terror was to keep them from evil. It can only be the sinner’s will set right.

4. What Maurice will not pronounce

Because everything turns on the will, Maurice’s uncertainty lies there. Mansel had said, tentatively, that we do not know how a man can be rescued without the co-operation of his own will. Maurice answers that he knows “most certainly” that he cannot be: “no man can be rescued out of the state of sin and suffering without the cooperation of his own will; because Sin is the very revolt of man’s will from the true will” (What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶42). And he cannot see how far a will may go in refusing. The central paragraph of the concluding essay begins with that admission:

I ask no one to pronounce, for I dare not pronounce myself, what are the possibilities of resistance in a human will to the loving will of God. There are times when they seem to me—thinking of myself more than of others—almost infinite.

— Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶31

He does not doubt that a man can refuse to the point of mortal sin. Sin “may bring him into a condition of mind in which lying becomes the element of his being, in which truth is absolutely closed from his eyes. The possibility of this sort of mortal sin I cannot doubt” (The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, “Signs of a Spiritual Society”, ¶34). He adds at once that such a state “could not change the fact”. The fact in question is the man’s union with Christ, which the paragraph has been defending. So what is lost is the man’s life in that union, not the union itself. Maurice reads St. John’s “He that is unjust, let him be unjust still” as a judgment that was “at hand” in the Apostle’s own age, and he gives the Judge’s sentence: “Thou hast chosen thy path, abide in it. Thou lovest wickedness: feel and know what wickedness is. Thou lovest the right, the pure, the good; feel and know what the right, the pure, and good, is” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XXII, “The Blessing and the Curse”, ¶16). Then he states the law without softening it:

God does more than pardon in our poor sense of that word. He gives repentance. All our experiments to measure and confine that grace of His are weak and impious. But it is the grace of turning us from darkness to light, not the cruel mercy of permitting us to go on in darkness, not the impossible mercy of giving us the blessing of a light which we hate. … We pray—Christ prays—that that will may prevail altogether. But so long as there is resistance to it, so long there is misery.

— Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XXII, ¶17

“We pray … that that will may prevail altogether.” Maurice prays that it will. He does not report that it has. The paragraph’s last words lean toward the hope without stating it: God is bringing all contrasts into manifestation, “just as He is bringing all that He has created into harmony” (¶17).

5. What Maurice will not deny

Each admission is met by something Maurice says he cannot give up. The paragraph of the concluding essay continues straight from “almost infinite”:

But I know that there is something which must be infinite. I am obliged to believe in an abyss of love which is deeper than the abyss of death: I dare not lose faith in that love. I sink into death, eternal death, if I do. I must feel that this love is compassing the universe. More about it I cannot know. But God knows. I leave myself and all to Him.

— Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶31

The two infinites are not equal. The possibilities of resistance are “almost infinite”, and the love “must be infinite”. Maurice does not set them side by side. He puts one beneath the other: the abyss of love is “deeper than the abyss of death”. He does not say the deeper abyss will win. He says that if he stopped believing it is deeper, he would himself sink into eternal death.

A single paragraph of the Apocalypse lectures has the same shape. Maurice begins from the vision. There the lake of fire is “no longer as out of His dominion, beyond the circle of His grace and love. It is His, to be used for His Divine purposes”. Then he states the contrary view, “our first thought”: whoever “has chosen death instead of life—must continue for ever in that condition”. He grants its force: “Reasoning upon the nature of a spirit, arguing about its immortality, I can find no escape from that conclusion, though I feel that it withers my energies as well as my hope.” And he sets it aside: “The revelation of God gives me that which all my speculations cannot give.”

I know that He can act upon spirits; I know that He can and does raise them out of death. I know that the process of rising out of death into the consciousness of life, is and must be an agonising one. … If I am told that whosoever hath not life is not left in that utter wretchedness, but is cast into a lake of fire—if I am told that death, hell, and whatever divides us from God is cast into the same lake—may I not accept that as a message from above, which meets all the wants, fears, longings of my heart?

— Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XXI, “The New Heaven and the New Earth”, ¶3

Speculation points one way and revelation, as Maurice reads it, the other, and he sides with revelation: “Must I set up the miserable measure of my own intellect against the word of God, merely because it brings me, as it always does, the divinest consolation, in the midst of the divinest warnings?” On this account the hope comes from Scripture and the doubt comes from speculation.

The letters against Mansel, written for a student preparing for orders, state the hope as a rule for preachers:

If it sounds dangerous to say that God willeth all men to be saved, and to come to the knowledge of the truth, and that His Will shall one day be done, we must leave Him to justify the language; since we cannot, at least let us not contradict it.

— What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶41

The preacher need not prove the hope, and he may not deny it. Two paragraphs later Maurice removes the two props a denial would need. The first is that death is the limit. The power by which God acts on the will “has nothing in its nature which fixes death as the limit of it, but everything which defies and breaks through that limit” (¶43). The second is that some sinners are beyond the reach of grace:

If we think God’s grace can overcome us, I am certain there is no obstinacy or rebellion in others which we can conclude must prove too mighty for it.

— What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶43

Maurice is “certain” here, but what he is certain of is a negative. No one may conclude that any man is beyond recovery. Mansel had argued that the real riddle is that evil exists at all, not how long it lasts. Maurice takes this to mean that evil, because it exists, “may just as well be immortal” (¶47). Against that he holds to the hope of the “restitution of all things”, because giving it up leads somewhere he will not go:

I believe, if we lose that hope altogether,—if God gives us up, as He may, for our rebellions, to such an entire extinction of it as there never has been in any period of the world yet,—we shall not stop at Mr. Mansel’s point; we shall be certain that Evil must reign for ever and ever, must drive out all that is opposed to it. We shall praise thee, O Devil, we shall acknowledge thee to be the lord.

— What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶48

The concluding essay had already drawn the same line. Men fall into eternal death “because they resist a power which is always at work to save them from it” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶29). And an article declaring “that there cannot be a deliverance from eternal punishment” would, Maurice warns, set “the whole Gospel of God” aside (¶30). He will not affirm the deliverance as doctrine. He will not let anyone deny it as doctrine either.

The hope of restitution, taken alone, could be fulfilled by evil being destroyed rather than by evil men being changed, and Maurice does speak of “the overthrow and destruction of all Death, Disorder, War” (What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶48). The step from restitution to the restoration of persons is this note’s, not a rule Maurice states. But his own words support it. In a Lincoln’s Inn sermon on Romans 8 he says the least St. Paul’s language can mean is “that the redemption of the will, the restoration of those whose state is that of God’s children to their true position and inheritance, was that which He sought by His death and resurrection” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Glory and Deliverance of the Animal Nature”, ¶14). The next paragraph goes on: “If there is to be a restitution of all things” (¶15). For Maurice, then, restitution centres on wills set right. And since God’s power over the will “has nothing in its nature which fixes death as the limit of it” (What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶43), nothing in his account confines that restoration to this life.

6. The hope at its boldest

In a few places Maurice goes beyond “let us not contradict it”. The boldest is his reading of the rich man in the parable of Lazarus, in lectures on St. Luke published in 1864. He takes the parable as true of what happens after death:

If I take our Lord Jesus Christ as my guide through mysteries which I cannot penetrate, if I suppose that He knows more than we know, I must conclude that a man who has been proof against all loving influences here, who had appeared to grow harder and more cruel under those very influences, may yet find the fatherly chastisement which will break his stubbornness and lead him to contrition. He who began to cry earnestly that those whom he had left behind him might not come into that place of torment, might have still a gulf separating him from Abraham. But that gulf must have been beginning to close up.

— The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XX, “The King Dismissing his Steward”, ¶27

The lecture’s subject is Israel. Maurice turns to the rich man because “the relation between the past and future of the Jewish nation may be most fittingly set forth in the past and future of a man” (¶27). The next paragraph keeps both in view:

It is not, my friends, by quenching this hope for any individual man, or for any fallen nation, that we shall make ourselves purer, or shall deepen the awe of our minds. We may believe, we ought to believe, that punishment is inseparable from evil, that God will never withhold His punishments while evil lasts. Israel cannot be saved till it is saved from its distrust and hatred of God. When St. Paul says ‘All Israel shall be saved,’ he expresses his conviction that it will one day be brought to own that the perfect friend of man is the true image of God. The assurance that its disobedience will be at last overcome, cannot make it safe for the Christian Church to imitate the sin of Israel, and so to come into its torments. The trust that no sin, no selfishness, shall at last be found stronger than the divine love, cannot lead any man to cultivate that which is to be vanquished, or defy that which must prevail. Despair is the devil’s instrument, not God’s. He is the God of Hope.

— The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XX, ¶28

The hope for the nation rests on St. Paul’s promise that “All Israel shall be saved”. But Maurice does not keep his strongest words for the nation. Of the rich man in ¶27 he has already said, “I cannot reject the assurance of so mighty a moral change”. In ¶28 the claim widens to “no sin, no selfishness”, and to “any individual man”, and what is trusted there “must prevail”. Even so, the sentence is a defence, not an announcement. Maurice is answering the objection that such trust encourages sin: it “cannot lead any man to cultivate that which is to be vanquished” (¶28). Here, and in a sermon quoted next, Maurice comes closest to a forecast.

A Lincoln’s Inn sermon on the Eucharist grounds the same trust in the sacrifice: “If there has been a full, perfect, and sufficient sacrifice, oblation, and satisfaction made for the sins of the whole world, we may trust that the Father who has accepted that sacrifice will draw our spirits, however sunken they may be in evil, to His Son; that He will at last draw all men to Him” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered as a Participation in the Unselfish Life of Christ”, ¶24). The preface to the lectures on St. Luke reads the divine fires the same way. If eternal life is the life of God, “the eternal punishment must be that which has been, and is, and will be directed to the extirpation of the great curse of moral evil” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Preface, ¶21). He grants that we are “distinctly warned” of fires “not purifying but destructive”, and of punishments that aim “at the perpetual prolongation of evil”. He connects these with the Tempter and the Destroyer, and does not ascribe them to God (¶22).

Even mortal sin falls within the hope. Commenting on St. John’s “sin unto death”, Maurice turns to St. Paul’s sentence on the incestuous Corinthian, delivered to Satan “that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord”:

The man has committed a mortal sin. By treating it with righteous severity, it may ultimately be found not mortal. God may remove it, though we cannot.

— The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XVIII, “The Nature of Prayer—Venial and Mortal Sins”, ¶23

The Church’s most solemn exclusion “is not to be regarded as cutting him off finally, but as a means of saving him finally” (¶24).

None of these passages breaks the rule of section 3. The rich man is not let out of the fire. He is changed in it, and the gulf narrows because he is “nearer in heart and mind to Abraham”. When death and hell are cast into the lake of fire, Maurice says, it is because God “has done His work; because He has seen of the travail of His soul, and been satisfied” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XX, ¶25). In the paragraph before, he reports St. John’s verdict on the judgment, “And life, he says, conquers” (¶24), and his comment in ¶25 shows he takes it as his own. What conquers is life in the will, not a suspension of judgment.

7. Why the hope never becomes a doctrine

If Maurice can say “must prevail”, why will he not say that all will be saved? The texts give two reasons.

The fear is for himself. The “almost infinite” possibilities of resistance are what he sees “thinking of myself more than of others” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶31). The letters against Mansel turn the same self-knowledge toward hope: “The fear—I must repeat it—is for ourselves. Those who have seen and known most of the love of God, must feel most what there is in their wills which resists it” (What is Revelation?, Letter X, ¶43). If grace can overcome that resistance in him, he cannot conclude that it must fail in another. The close of the concluding essay turns the warning on religious teachers. Christ’s words about the damnation of hell were spoken “to religious men, to doctors of the law” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶33), and Maurice applies them to his own order: “I am certain that we who are in continual contact with eternal things do require to remind ourselves what danger we are in of losing these things. Spiritual pride is the essential nature of the Devil. To be in that is to be in the deepest hell” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶34). Maurice knows his own will well enough to fear it, and he does not claim to know more of anyone else’s. To announce that every will must yield would be to speak about the one thing he says he cannot see. He can say that no one may conclude any will is beyond grace. He cannot say that every will will yield to it.

Judgment belongs to God. In his note on the Athanasian Creed, Maurice argues that because eternal life is the knowledge of God and its loss is inward, “we have no power of saying, in any case, who has lost it, or incurred the state which is opposite to it” (Theological Essays, “Note on the Athanasian Creed”, ¶7). There the restraint concerns naming the lost. In the concluding essay he puts the same restraint on the outcome as a whole, and hands it over: “More about it I cannot know. But God knows. I leave myself and all to Him” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay”, ¶31).

8. What kind of universalist

The question is where Maurice stands between two positions.

  • A convinced universalist says that all will be saved and holds this as part of the faith. Maurice never says it. Of the one thing it would require him to know, he says, “I dare not pronounce”.
  • A hopeful universalist hopes that all will be saved, holds the hope as part of faith in God, and will not make it a doctrine about men.

Maurice is the second. But he is no shrugging agnostic within it. To lose faith in the deeper abyss is for him to “sink into death, eternal death”. To lose the hope of restitution is to “praise thee, O Devil”. And at his boldest he comes close to the line, when he speaks of a trust that no sin “shall at last be found stronger” and of “that which must prevail”. What keeps him on the hopeful side is that his confidence is always about God’s love and never a claim about the end of any human will.

His hope is not a soft one. It rests on what he is surest of: that God is love, that God wills all men to be saved, that the sacrifice was made “for the sins of the whole world”, and that God’s victories are “victories over the will of men”. It speaks in the language of trust: “may I not accept”, “we may trust”. It obliges the preacher not to contradict it, and forbids him to turn it into a sentence on anyone. Nor does it comfort the obstinate. No man is restored except by having his will set right, and “so long as there is resistance … so long there is misery”. All the hope holds out to them is the fire that may yet change them.

In short: Maurice is not a universalist in the sense his accusers meant. He teaches no restoration in which pain pays for sin, and he pronounces nothing about how far a human will can resist. But he is a universalist in hope, and his hope is firm. He believes in “an abyss of love which is deeper than the abyss of death”. He holds that no one may conclude any sinner is beyond grace, here or after death. And at his boldest he calls it a trust “that no sin, no selfishness, shall at last be found stronger than the divine love”. He is sure of God’s love and refuses to pronounce on man’s end, and that refusal is what keeps the hope from becoming a doctrine.
