F. D. Maurice on the hope that everyone will be saved

November 23, 2026 · revised November 24, 2026

F. D. Maurice believed that God wants everyone to be saved and that Christians have a duty to hope for everyone. For Maurice, salvation means a change of heart. Even after death, suffering may bring someone who has resisted love all their life to repent and begin to love what God loves. Only God knows how long a person can go on resisting. Maurice was a hopeful universalist in this sense. He trusted God to draw everyone to himself, but he declined to teach as an article of faith that every person would finally be saved.

George MacDonald went further than Maurice. The article Was George MacDonald a Hopeful Universalist?, on the George MacDonald Books site, explains why he is better called a convinced universalist.

What was at stake for the Church

In 1853 Maurice lost his professorship at King’s College, London, after writing about eternal life and eternal death. He asked what picture of God a preacher gives by making everlasting punishment the heart of the Christian message. It would sound as though the Father had created multitudes intending them to perish, and Christ had persuaded him to spare a few. For Maurice, Christ reveals the Father’s own love. In a sermon on Holy Communion, he says that because Christ offered himself for the sins of the whole world, “we may trust” that the Father “will at last draw all men to Him”.1

In Edward VI’s reign (1553), one of the Church of England’s articles of faith condemned the belief that everyone would be saved after suffering for a set time. Maurice could see why the Reformers might have adopted it. They had good reason to insist that “pain did not make amends for sin”.2

The Church later dropped the article. Those who wanted a firm ruling on everlasting punishment explained that the article had been a temporary measure against the Anabaptists. Maurice took them at their word. If the article had served its purpose, Anglicans were free to differ. Those who believed in endless punishment could hold that belief without requiring it of others. Maurice likewise asked no one to profess universal salvation.3

What it means to be saved

Maurice begins with what Christians mean when they call God eternal. God’s life is beyond any measure of time. He argues that the word must carry the same meaning when Christians speak of eternal life and eternal punishment. Eternal life is knowing God, who is love. Eternal death is losing that knowledge by setting oneself against his love. Both are possible now. Someone who turns from hatred to love has already begun to emerge from what Scripture calls eternal death. The word eternal by itself cannot tell us how long anyone will resist God or whether everyone will finally be saved.4

Maurice speaks of someone so accustomed to lying that they can scarcely recognize the truth. Even this, he says, “could not change the fact”. In context, he appears to mean that the person remains united to Christ, however completely they have lost sight of that bond.5

What could punishment do for such a person? No amount of pain would make amends for the lies. Suffering might bring them to see their own deceit and want to tell the truth. For Maurice, God judges evil for as long as it persists, and the misery it brings lasts as long as we cling to it. God can give us the desire to change. Our own willing response matters because what needs healing is our will.6

Maurice saw this kind of change in Dante’s picture of Purgatory. Though he objected to abuses associated with the belief, he valued Dante’s account of people rising out of the evil that had held them. He saw a similar purpose in Paul’s decision to exclude a man from the Christian community for a grave wrong. Even so severe a judgment aims at the man’s recovery. God may yet free him from the very sin that threatens to destroy him.7

How punishment after death may bring a change of heart

In a lecture on Israel’s past and future, Maurice turns to Jesus’ parable of the rich man and Lazarus. What happens to one man helps him explain what God’s judgment can do for a whole people. In life the rich man ignored Lazarus, the poor man at his door. After death, in torment, he begs Abraham to send Lazarus to warn his brothers so that they might be spared what he is suffering.

Maurice takes Jesus as his guide here. “I must conclude”, he writes, that even someone hardened against love in this life “may yet find the fatherly chastisement which will break his stubbornness and lead him to contrition”. The suffering itself can bring the rich man to sorrow for his cruelty. Maurice hears the first sign of that change in the plea for his brothers. In caring what becomes of them, the man has already drawn nearer in heart to Abraham. Maurice writes, “I cannot reject the assurance of so mighty a moral change”.8

Returning to Israel, Maurice reads Paul’s promise that “All Israel shall be saved” as a promise that God will overcome the people’s distrust. He extends the hope to individuals, trusting “that no sin, no selfishness, shall at last be found stronger than the divine love”. Some feared this would encourage wrongdoing. As Maurice sees it, someone who hopes that God will cure cruelty has no reason to cultivate it.9

When Maurice reasons from the immortality of the spirit, he concludes that someone who has chosen spiritual death must remain in it forever. On those grounds alone he sees “no escape from that conclusion”, though the thought “withers my energies as well as my hope”. Scripture gives him reason to think again. In the book of Revelation, death and hell are themselves thrown into the lake of fire. Even that fire, as Maurice reads the vision, is within God’s power to use for good. God can awaken someone from spiritual death, however painful that awakening may be.10

In a sermon on Paul’s letter to the Romans, Maurice says that Christ died and rose to restore God’s children by healing their wills. Read with that sermon, the “restitution of all things” holds out the hope that people who do evil may themselves be restored. The phrase alone leaves each person’s fate open.11

Why Maurice hopes so firmly and speaks so carefully

When Maurice says that human resistance can seem “almost infinite”, he is thinking chiefly of his own. Of God’s love he speaks with certainty. It must be infinite. He believes in “an abyss of love which is deeper than the abyss of death”. If he trusts God’s grace to overcome his own stubbornness, what grounds has he for deciding that it must fail with someone else?12

Maurice takes Christ’s warnings about hell to heart as a preacher. Jesus addressed some of his severest words to religious teachers, and Maurice saw how easily a teacher like himself could fall into spiritual pride. Only God knows another person’s heart well enough to judge their relationship with him.13

To give up hope of restoration altogether would, Maurice feared, be to concede that evil will rule forever. Writing to a future preacher, he reminds him that Scripture says God wills everyone’s salvation. If it seems daring to add that God’s will shall one day be done, Maurice urges him to let the words stand and “leave Him to justify the language”. In his own account of eternal life and eternal death, he writes, “More about it I cannot know. But God knows. I leave myself and all to Him.”14
