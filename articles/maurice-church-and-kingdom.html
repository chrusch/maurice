The Church and the Kingdom for All Men in F. D. Maurice

November 20, 2026

Readers of Maurice soon meet a puzzle. He calls the Church the kingdom, speaking of “a Catholic Church or Kingdom of Christ in the world” (Kingdom of Christ I, ¶32). Yet he holds that the kingdom belongs to every man, and that the Gospel to be preached “to Christians, to Hindoos, to Mussulmans, to all people whatsoever” is “that God is the Father of all in Jesus Christ, Who is the head of every man” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons II, ¶3). If the Church is the kingdom, how can those outside the Church belong to it?

Verdict. Maurice saw the problem and answered it with one principle: Christ is the Head of every man, and is in every man, whether that man knows it or not. Kingdom names that order, set up for the whole race in Christ; Church names the society received into it under its sign and sealed with its Name. The sign is baptism, which Maurice calls the “first sign” of the kingdom (later in The Kingdom of Christ he treats the creeds, forms of worship, the Eucharist, the ministry and the Scriptures as further signs). Baptism truly admits, and Maurice defends baptismal regeneration, but it admits a person only to what Christ has already won for all, so it “can confer no separate or independent grace upon any creature.” What he leaves open is narrower than it first looks: not what the unbaptized lack, which he names, but what difference knowing and owning one’s place makes before God.

1. The Church is the kingdom, and the kingdom is for mankind

Maurice does identify the Church with the kingdom:

I have stated why I look upon Baptism as the first sign of the existence of a Catholic Church or Kingdom of Christ in the world.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, “Signs of a Spiritual Society”, ¶32

And in the same chapter he says that this kingdom is for everyone:

I have maintained, upon the authority of Scripture, that the Catholic Church is emphatically a kingdom for mankind, a kingdom grounded upon the union which has been established in Christ between God and man.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, ¶14

The two claims fit together because of what the kingdom rests on. It does not rest on the Church’s membership. It rests on the union of God and man in Christ, which is a fact about the human race.

2. Christ is in every man

That fact is the ground of everything else, and Maurice states it without qualification. In the letter that opens The Doctrine of Sacrifice he describes how he came to it through St. Paul:

Was that Son really in Saul of Tarsus, and did he only become Paul the converted when that Son was revealed in him? Could he preach to the Gentiles, who were bowing to gods of wood and stone, Christ is in you?

— The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter, ¶14

His answer is yes. Saul did not lack Christ; he lacked Christ revealed. Two paragraphs later Maurice calls “this principle, that Christ is in every man” his own “reconciliation” of the church parties who quarrelled over the baptismal service (¶16). Elsewhere he writes that “there is with every man the Christ, who would quicken his spirit” (Theological Essays, ¶13). And he warns against shrinking the doctrine to the baptized:

And we see, how every theory which limits this doctrine to the Church, and so prevents the Church from being the witness of it to the world, destroys its meaning and effect.

— The Unity of the New Testament, “The Epistle to Philemon”, ¶2

3. Baptism admits only to what is already ours

If Christ is in every man, what does baptism do? Maurice does not say it does nothing. He says it admits, and then sets a limit on what admission can mean. His clearest statement is in a sermon for Innocents’ Day:

They may claim to be sons of God by adoption, because the Only Begotten has made Himself their Brother. If Baptism admits one and another into this condition, if it gives one and another the filial name and the filial privilege, it does so because Christ had first vindicated that name and those privileges for all human beings, by taking their flesh. It cannot do less or more than the charter from which it derives its force authorizes it to do. It can confer no separate or independent grace upon any creature. It can only say: “Thou belongest to the Head of thy race; thou art a member of His body …”

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”, ¶13

The admission is real, but its ground is the Incarnation, and the Incarnation concerns “all human beings.” What baptism says to one person is true of everyone, which is why it can say it. The essay on regeneration puts the same point from the side of knowledge:

But his perception does not make the fact which he perceives. … Baptism declares this to be their proper and divine constitution in Christ. All who receive it claim the kingdom which God has declared to be theirs. They take up their rights as spiritual beings.

— Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”, ¶16

Maurice reads St. Paul the same way: Paul took baptism as God’s “declaration of that which is true concerning men, of the actual relation in which men stand to Him” (Theological Essays, ¶10).

The claim that counts runs from God to men, not from men to God. On the children brought to Jesus, Maurice paraphrases what Christ’s words have been taken to mean: “These children are Mine. Whatever you may think of them, I claim them. For your faith does not make Me your Lord” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, ¶12). It is on this ground, he says, “that the Church rests her Infant Baptism.” In infant baptism, one may add, the person baptized claims nothing at all.

4. The baptized have no privilege the unbaptized lack a right to

Maurice raises the obvious objection himself, putting it in the mouth of “modern Philosophers”: “Baptism cannot be the sign of a Universal Society, for it excludes Pagans and Mahometans” (Kingdom of Christ I, ¶25). His reply:

But, be it remembered, this is not the idea of Baptism as it is expressed in any one formulary which is recognised in any part of Christendom. That idea assumes Christ to be the Lord of men; it assumes that men are created in Him; that this is the constitution of our race; that therefore all attempts of men to reduce themselves into separate units are contradictory and abortive. … According to our doctrine we must say to Jews, Pagans, Turks, “There is a fellowship for you as well as for us. We have no right to any spiritual privileges to which you have not as complete, as indefeasible a right.” …

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, ¶26

An indefeasible right is one that cannot be taken away. In the second volume he puts the whole position into one paragraph, which shows how the universal covenant and baptismal admission fit together:

… and yet it does not treat any living man as lying beyond the pale of God’s covenant. … Baptism declares man’s true and right constitution to be that of union with God, and separation from Him to be a violation of that only order according to which, as reason and experience alike shew, he can live. It is a fact that men are living anomalously; it is their own testimony that in doing so they are following their natures. Baptism declares that those who will are taken out of that inconsistent condition to which they are prone, and are taken into a reasonable condition, in which they may live so long as they remember the covenant of God.

— The Kingdom of Christ II, “Part II. (Continued) of the Catholic Church and the Romish System”, ¶3

The covenant covers every living man. What baptism admits to is a condition: living according to the covenant instead of against it.

5. A distinct Church, not a rival one

If the kingdom belongs to everyone, why keep a separate body at all? Because a witness has to stand apart from what it witnesses against. Men naturally deny that they belong to God and to each other, so the body that says otherwise must be visible. Maurice admits this sounds paradoxical:

The other body, therefore, the Church, being especially the witness for these facts which it is natural to us to deny, must be a distinct body. In losing its distinctness it loses its meaning, loses to all intents and purposes, though the words may at first sound paradoxical, its universality.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, ¶1

The Church’s distinctness serves the kingdom’s universality. It is not a fence around a select few. It exists to tell the world what it is:

The Universal Church, constituted in its Universal Head, exists to protest against a world which supposes itself to be a collection of incoherent fragments without a centre, which, where it reduces its practice to a maxim, treats every man as his own centre. The Church exists to tell the world of its true Centre, of the law of mutual sacrifice by which its parts are bound together.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The World and the Church”, ¶10

The Church protests against the world’s false idea of itself, but it is not the world’s rival. The Father loves the world, the Son dies for it, the Spirit convinces it; is the Church, Maurice asks ironically, to do the opposite, “to set itself up as a rival competitor to the world, to plot against the world, to undermine the world!” (¶8).

6. Church and world run through everyone

Maurice’s definition of the two makes them one human society in two states, not two kinds of people:

The world contains the elements of which the Church is composed. In the Church these elements are penetrated by a uniting, reconciling power. The Church is, therefore, human society in its normal state; the World, that same society irregular and abnormal. The world is the Church without God; the Church is the world restored to its relation with God, taken back by Him into the state for which He created it.

— Theological Essays, “On the Unity of the Church”, ¶26

Entering the Church does deliver a person from the world: “he who enters this society is emancipated from the World” (Kingdom of Christ I, ¶30). But the world is defined there as subjection to “the evil selfish tendencies of each man’s nature,” and those tendencies do not stay outside the font. There is, he says, “a natural heart in all of us, baptized and unbaptized,—Christian, Jew, Turk, Pagan” that resists God (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons VI, ¶7). And no one belongs to the world by right:

… that world to which we should assure every man, however much he may have caught its colour and its habits, that he does not of right belong. … every society has in it an element of the Church as well as of the world …

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The World and the Church”, ¶22

This is not the “two kingdoms” idea Maurice attacks elsewhere, in which the Evangelicals of his day set a merely nominal kingdom of the baptized against the real kingdom of the converted (Kingdom of Christ I, ¶23). For him there is one real kingdom, made visible in the Church and true of all, and a baptized man who lives badly is a rebel within it: “We do not … cease to be children because we are disobedient children” (¶37).

7. Entering means living as what you are

If the kingdom belongs to everyone, what do the Gospels mean by entering it? In a sermon on the strait gate Maurice faces the objection in its sharpest form: “How can we proclaim that as a blessing to all which we are told is found only by a few?” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons II, ¶3). His answer, spoken in Christ’s person, is that the gate is already open. The narrow part is believing it:

I, Who open and none can shut, have opened this gate for you; in your nature I have claimed for you the rights of Sons of God. … You cannot find life if you refuse to own the Father Whom I have declared to you; you cannot have any life but the life of dependent, trusting children. Assume that state to be yours, wonderful as it is.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “A Straitened Faith and a Narrow Gospel Not the Strait Gate and the Narrow Way of Christ”, ¶10

The few who find the way are those who believe they are what they are. The Church’s own temptation is to forget the many: it has believed in Christ “as its own King” but not “as a Son of Man—as the King of Men” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, ¶5).

8. What is left open

None of this makes baptism a mere announcement for Maurice. He defends baptismal regeneration, while expecting it to win him no friends: a man would be “a silly dreamer if he fancies that he shall conciliate High Churchmen because he defends Baptismal Regeneration” (Theological Essays, ¶34). His language of admission is strong:

  • “The first Baptism declared that the Kingdom of Heaven was near, and was about to declare itself. The second Baptism admitted men into it” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons VI, ¶17).
  • In baptism “the man or the child is actually adopted into union with a Being above himself, and that the Spirit of Life, of Power, of Wisdom, is given to him” (Kingdom of Christ I, ¶27).
  • Of the baptized: “He bestows His Spirit upon them that they may enjoy these rights” (Theological Essays, ¶16).

Admission implies an outside, and in a note on how Augustine taught catechumens, Maurice speaks of the heathen as “those who have not yet been received into His covenant” (Kingdom of Christ I, Notes, ¶5). Yet the point of that very note is that we are not warranted “in treating Quakers as if they must receive baptism, before we can speak with them respecting the principles and end of the Gospel.” And the gifts of the Spirit are not confined to the baptized either: his Gospel is “that all gifts with which men are endowed are gifts of the Holy Spirit, and are signs of His presence” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons II, ¶3).

So Maurice does locate the difference. The unbaptized lack the revelation, the acknowledgment, the fellowship and the seal, not Christ’s presence. The question he leaves open is narrower: what difference knowing and owning one’s place makes before God, for a man in whom Christ already is. On that he holds two claims together and will not reduce either to the other: that baptism truly admits and gives, and that it gives nothing which is not already every man’s.

In one sentence: for Maurice Christ is already in every man, and the Church is the society received under its sign into the kingdom that is the truth about every man, appointed to say so; baptism truly admits, but only to what Christ has already won for all, so membership in the Church marks the sealing and acknowledgment of the kingdom, not exclusive possession of it.
