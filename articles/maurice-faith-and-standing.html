What Faith Changes in F. D. Maurice

November 20, 2026

  1. 1. What faith does not make
  2. 2. Faith in faith
  3. 3. The language of becoming
  4. 4. Two truths about every man
  5. 5. Repentance is a change of mind about God
  6. 6. How the Creator “becomes a Father”
  7. 7. What is left open

Maurice says two things that seem not to fit. He says that every man is already a child of God in Christ, and that no belief or act of ours makes him so: “Thy fatherly love to me is not determined by my sense of it, my belief of it, else what would become of me!” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons II, ¶6). He also says that when a man learns to pray “Thy will be done”, “by this the Creator becomes a Father, and the creature a child” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons IV, ¶12). If the man is a child already, what is there for prayer to make him? The long essay records this as a question it does not settle, and the note on the Church and the kingdom ended at the same place: what difference knowing and owning one’s place makes before God.

Verdict. Maurice is more consistent here than his sentences suggest. He uses son, righteous and justified in two registers, and he keeps them apart. In the first they name what God has pronounced about the race in Christ. That is fixed, it is true of every man, and faith, baptism and conversion do not make it. In the second they name the life a man is actually living. That depends on trust, and it really changes. The two meet in one principle: there are two truths about every man. In himself he is condemned, and in Christ he is justified. Faith does not change either verdict. It decides which of the two the man is living as. So the language of becoming is not a slip. Maurice uses it for the will and for the man’s life, never for the relation. What he leaves open is the man who never trusts at all.

1. What faith does not make

Maurice’s plainest statement of the fixed side comes in a Lent sermon on Romans 5:18. He reads the verse as saying the same thing about everyone:

It is not said that all were condemned and some were justified, but that all were condemned and all were justified. However much we may be surprised by the assertion, it stands written in the passage which is the authoritative one upon the subject: if we accept the teaching of the Bible, we accept this teaching.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Sin of Man and the Justification of Man”, ¶15

He also rules out the idea that the justification began when it was seen. The assertion baffles us, he says, “if we suppose that Christ only became the Head of it when He was shown by His death and resurrection to be the Head of it” (¶16). If showing a thing does not make it true, then a man’s seeing it does not make it true either. The essay on regeneration says so in five words:

He sees that Eternal Life which was with the Father, and which in the Divine Word is manifested to us; he becomes an inheritor of it. But his perception does not make the fact which he perceives.

— Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”, ¶16

He offers an analogy in a sermon on Hebrews 11. Faith “conversed with invisible things; with things which were; which it could not create more than the eye created the things which made themselves evident to it”. The analogy cuts both ways, and he lets it:

The eye being blind, there is no revelation to it, though all the Universe is revealing itself. Faith being absent in a man, there is no revelation of God to him; though God is ever revealing Himself.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Three Things Which Abide”, ¶7

The eye does not make the world, but a blind man does not see it. That is the shape of the whole doctrine. The rest of this note follows it out.

The fixed side is pastoral as well as exegetical. Preaching on a national day of humiliation, with the dead of the Indian Mutiny in view, Maurice puts the words of the prodigal in every mouth: “None can say, ‘I must make myself a son; I must establish my relationship to God before I claim the full sense and virtue of them.’” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”, ¶6). For Maurice the consolation depends on this. If the relation waited on the mourner’s faith, it could not comfort him.

2. Faith in faith

The fixed side also explains Maurice’s most pointed charge against the Evangelicals of his day. He honours the Clapham men, whose faith bore fruit in honest commerce and in public life. He asks why their heirs show no such fruit:

Is it not because they believe justification by faith, instead of believing in Christ the Justifier? Is not the whole principle changed? Is not the formula which represents the principle doing duty for it?

— Theological Essays, “On Justification by Faith”, ¶17

He finds the same fault in the new Unitarians who had learned from Carlyle to praise faith: “that of making Faith itself an object of trust, almost of worship” (¶20). If faith made the relation, the believer would have to look at his own faith to know where he stood, and his trust would rest on his trust. Maurice’s doctrine closes that loop. Faith looks at Christ, and what it sees was there before it looked.

3. The language of becoming

Even so, Maurice uses the language of becoming freely, and three sentences show it at its strongest.

The first is the one already quoted from the sermon on Psalm 119: “by this the Creator becomes a Father, and the creature a child.” The second is in the passage on regeneration that denies perception makes the fact. One clause before that denial, the man who looks to the cross “becomes an inheritor” of eternal life. The third is in The Kingdom of Christ, which turns become against itself:

Man does not, therefore, by Baptism, by faith, or by any other process, acquire a new nature, if by nature you mean, as most men do, certain inherent qualities and properties. He does not by Baptism, faith, or by any other process, become a new creature, if by these words you mean anything else than that he is created anew in Christ Jesus, that he is grafted into Him, that he becomes the inheritor of His life and not of his own.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, “Signs of a Spiritual Society”, ¶34

That sentence is the key. Something does become. A man becomes an inheritor, and he lives from Christ’s life and no longer from his own. What does not become is a new property in him. The man changes, the ground does not, and the change is that he stops claiming any ground of his own.

Maurice is just as plain that the change is real. He endorses the Reformers’ testimony: “You are not free till you trust Him; till you are free, you cannot do the works of a freeman, but only those of a slave” (Theological Essays, “On Justification by Faith”, ¶7). The Thessalonians, he writes, “were sons of the light and of the day then; they were not to become so afterwards. But they were to expect blessings which they had not received” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The First and Second Epistles to the Thessalonians”, ¶18). Sonship is already theirs, and freedom and salvation are still to come. In that one sentence both registers stand side by side.

4. Two truths about every man

How can a man be justified and still need to be set free? Maurice’s answer is that two opposite verdicts are both true of him, and both come from God, whose judgments “are according to truth”:

If He pronounces or adjudges us sinners, it is because we are sinners; if He justifies or pronounces us righteous, it is because we are righteous. … He is depraved, utterly depraved, in himself. Find me the man who is utterly shut up in himself, who never rises out of himself, and I say, “In that man is no good thing; he is buried in his lost Adam-nature; by the conditions of the universe, by the eternal law of God, he is simply, nakedly evil.” Find me such a man.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Sin of Man and the Justification of Man”, ¶17

The commentary on Romans gives the same doctrine in its most compressed form. Maurice voices St. Paul’s cry in Romans 7, and then the answer that came to him: “But thou art not evil; thou art righteous; Christ is in thee; thou art one with Him.” Readers usually stop there. Maurice goes on:

This is surely the history of a discovery made to the Apostle at a certain time. A truth was revealed to him which was good for all his after years. He had no need to repeat the same terrible process again; God claimed him as a new creature in Christ. He was so then; He would be so always. But that revelation brought with it another. “With my flesh I serve the law of sin.” That is not altered by my conversion or my baptism; my conversion and baptism both declare it. In you, your flesh, your separate self, they both alike say, there dwelleth no good thing. Only while you assert the privilege of a spiritual creature, the privilege of not being shut up in self, of being married to a living Lord, only then art thou righteous, only then art thou alive.

— The Unity of the New Testament, “I. The Epistle to the Romans”, ¶20

The clause “only then art thou righteous” is the strongest conditional Maurice writes, and it has to be read beside “thou art righteous” four sentences earlier. It does not take back the first sentence. The righteousness is never the man’s own in either case. It is Christ’s, and it is his only as he is “married to a living Lord.” Without that he is still what he is “in himself.” The next paragraph states the rule: “man in the flesh, in himself, is condemned, hopelessly condemned … man in the spirit, in Christ Jesus, is justified, redeemed, renewed” (¶21). Both verdicts stand over every man, and trust decides which one his life is lived under.

This is why Maurice thinks it truer to speak of “individual depravity” than of universal depravity: “By individual depravity I mean my own” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons V, ¶18). What is universal is the justification. The depravity is what each man finds when he lives as if he were his own.

5. Repentance is a change of mind about God

If faith does not make the relation, what is repentance for? Maurice answers in a village sermon on Acts 3:19:

To Repent is to change our minds; to think quite otherwise of some matter or some person than we have been used to think.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, “Sermon XLI. Repentance and Conversion”, ¶5

The mind that changes is the mind about God. Men took him for an enemy to be fled or bribed. Repentance learns from Christ that he is a Father. Maurice then makes a claim that seems backwards: only now does the man know he has sinned. “For now first they would feel that they had sinned. … No one says that till he feels that he has been disbelieving, distrusting, disobeying a Father” (¶8). The sense of sin follows the discovery of the relation. It does not come before it as the condition of entering.

The change is real. To all of St. Peter’s hearers, including those who had cried “Crucify Him!”, the same hope is held out: “Your sins may be blotted out. You may be made new and pure creatures” (¶9). And the call is made to people who are already children. Maurice asks his own congregation: “Do we trust Him who has taken us to be His children, who has declared that we belong to the Body of His Own Son, who has sealed us with His Spirit?” (¶12).

In The Kingdom of Christ he turns this against the Evangelicals of his day. If a man may not call himself a child of God until something has happened to him, the prodigal cannot repent:

If we are not allowed to call ourselves children of God, how can we be told to arise and go to our Father? If we are not to do this, what does our repentance mean? It can be nothing but a sinful selfish struggle after the blessings of corn and wine and the fatness of the earth, which we think we have lost; not a humble confession that we have made light of our birthright, and are no more worthy to be called sons.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, ¶35

So repentance does not make a man a son. It is the son’s confession that he has slighted his birthright, and a man can only make that confession if the birthright is already his.

6. How the Creator “becomes a Father”

The sentence from the sermon on Psalm 119 can now be read in context. The sermon is about the will. The man whose “soul cleaveth unto the dust” finds that the failure is not in his circumstances but in himself. Only then does he ask for the one thing worth asking:

As long as that weakness can be referred to anything but the man’s own will; as long as he feels the bondage to be in something else than that, he may ask for rains or fruitful seasons, for the relief from some pain, for the gift of children or of friends. But he will not say, Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven; he will not understand that this is the greatest blessing which the creature can ask or the Creator can give; that by this the Creator becomes a Father, and the creature a child. … That lore comes when he perceives that his will can only be free and loving, when the free and loving Will that formed his has subdued it to itself.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer”, ¶12

“By this” refers to the doing of God’s will on earth: the man’s will subdued to the Will that formed it. What becomes, then, is fatherhood carried out in a willing child, not fatherhood pronounced over the race. A father whose son runs from him is still his father. A father whose son’s will is one with his own is a father in a fuller sense. That is the only sense in which Maurice can mean “becomes” without contradicting the Day of Humiliation sermon, which says the Father’s love “is not determined by my sense of it”. The two sermons were preached a year apart, in 1857 and 1858. This is my reading, and Maurice does not gloss the sentence himself. But the paragraph that follows supports it. The prayer, he says, “invokes that absolute Divine Will … to give him the free agency which, left to himself, he wants” (¶14). What God gives in answer is a renovated will. He does not give a new relation.

7. What is left open

The note can now answer the question the Church note left open. Knowing and owning one’s place makes no difference to God’s pronouncement, which already covers every man. It makes all the difference to the man’s life. It decides whether he lives as the justified man he is in Christ or as the condemned self he is “in himself”, free or slave, “alive” or “shut up in self”. In this Maurice treats faith exactly as he treats baptism. Both declare, and neither creates. Both are also the point at which a man stops refusing what he has been given.

Refusal is where the question remains open. Maurice defines sin itself as refusal: men’s sin “consists in their voluntarily refusing the blessings of God’s covenant” (Kingdom of Christ I, ¶34). He will not hide from any man “the good news that he, too, is a subject of this kingdom … or the warning that if he chooses another condition than this, he is declaring war with his Creator, with his fellows, and with himself” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”, ¶19). A man can refuse a relation without ending it. Maurice never says what the relation amounts to for a man who refuses it to the end, a son who never comes home. He takes up that question elsewhere, under eternal life and eternal death, and there he declines to pronounce. The doctrine of this note does not settle it. It only fixes what is at stake. Whatever happens to such a man, he does not stop being someone for whom Christ is Head. He goes on refusing what is true of him.

One honest qualification is needed. Maurice’s two registers are not labelled in his text. The same words, righteous, son and new creature, move between them, sometimes within a single sentence, as in “he becomes an inheritor of it. But his perception does not make the fact”. A reader who picks up one sentence will find him on one side, and a reader who picks up another will find him on the other. That explains how the long essay could find him “on both sides of that line”. But the passages taken together show a single structure, and Maurice states it himself in the Romans commentary.

In one sentence: for Maurice faith does not make a man God’s child or make him righteous, because God has already pronounced every man justified in Christ and condemned in himself; faith decides which of those two truths the man lives under, so becoming belongs to his will and his freedom and never to the relation, and what he leaves unanswered is what the unchanged relation means for a man who refuses it to the end.
