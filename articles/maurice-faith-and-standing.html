What Faith Changes in F. D. Maurice

Maurice says two things that seem not to fit. He says that every man is already a child of God in Christ, and that no belief or act of ours makes him so: “Thy fatherly love to me is not determined by my sense of it, my belief of it, else what would become of me!” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons II, ¶6). He also says that when a man learns to pray “Thy will be done”, “by this the Creator becomes a Father, and the creature a child” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons IV, ¶12). If the man is a child already, what is left for prayer to make him? The same question can be put to faith, baptism and repentance: what difference does knowing and owning one’s place make before God?

Verdict. Maurice is more consistent here than his sentences suggest. He speaks of sonship and righteousness at three levels, and he keeps them apart:

  • The fact. God has pronounced every man condemned in himself and justified in Christ. That is fixed. Faith, baptism and conversion do not make it, and sin cannot unmake it.
  • Admission. Baptism really adopts a man. From that moment he is a member of the society that knows the fact and claims it. But it adopts him only into what was already true of him.
  • The life. Whether a man lives as the justified man he is in Christ or as the condemned self he is in himself depends on trust, and it really changes.

Maurice’s language of becoming belongs to admission and to the life, never to the fact. That holds even when, following St. John, he says that a sinner “makes himself a child of the devil”. What he leaves open is not the man’s standing. It is whether a refusal of it can last for ever.

1. What faith does not make

Maurice’s plainest statement of the fact comes in a Lent sermon on Romans 5:18. He reads the verse as saying the same thing about everyone:

It is not said that all were condemned and some were justified, but that all were condemned and all were justified. However much we may be surprised by the assertion, it stands written in the passage which is the authoritative one upon the subject: if we accept the teaching of the Bible, we accept this teaching.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Sin of Man and the Justification of Man”, ¶15

He also rules out the idea that the justification began when it was seen. The assertion baffles us, he says, “if we suppose that Christ only became the Head of it when He was shown by His death and resurrection to be the Head of it” (¶16). If showing a thing does not make it true, neither does seeing it. The essay on regeneration says so in one sentence:

He sees that Eternal Life which was with the Father, and which in the Divine Word is manifested to us; he becomes an inheritor of it. But his perception does not make the fact which he perceives.

— Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”, ¶16

A sermon on Hebrews 11 supplies the analogy. Faith “conversed with invisible things; with things which were; which it could not create more than the eye created the things which made themselves evident to it”. The analogy cuts both ways, and Maurice lets it:

The eye being blind, there is no revelation to it, though all the Universe is revealing itself. Faith being absent in a man, there is no revelation of God to him; though God is ever revealing Himself.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Three Things Which Abide”, ¶7

The eye does not make the world, but a blind man does not see it. That is the shape of the whole doctrine.

The point is pastoral as well as exegetical. Preaching on a national day of humiliation, with the dead of the Indian Mutiny in view, Maurice puts the prodigal’s words in every mouth: “None can say, ‘I must make myself a son; I must establish my relationship to God before I claim the full sense and virtue of them.’” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”, ¶6). The consolation depends on this. If the relation waited on the mourner’s faith, it could not comfort him.

2. Faith in faith

The same principle lies behind Maurice’s most pointed charge against the Evangelicals of his day. He honours the Clapham men, whose faith bore fruit in honest commerce and public life, and asks why their heirs show no such fruit:

Is it not because they believe justification by faith, instead of believing in Christ the Justifier? Is not the whole principle changed? Is not the formula which represents the principle doing duty for it?

— Theological Essays, “On Justification by Faith”, ¶17

He finds the same fault in the new Unitarians who had learned from Carlyle to praise faith: “that of making Faith itself an object of trust, almost of worship” (¶20). If faith made the relation, a believer would have to inspect his own faith to know where he stood, and his trust would rest on his trust. Maurice’s doctrine closes that loop. Faith looks at Christ, and what it sees was there before it looked.

3. Two truths about every man

If every man is justified, why does anyone need to be set free? Maurice’s answer is the principle the rest of this note depends on. Two opposite verdicts are both true of every man, and both come from God, whose judgments “are according to truth”:

If He pronounces or adjudges us sinners, it is because we are sinners; if He justifies or pronounces us righteous, it is because we are righteous. St. Paul declares both facts as facts for all men, just as we have discovered them to be in our own experience. But he does not leave us in the bewilderment in which that experience leaves us. He clears the contradiction; he shows how that which is true if we look upon Man in one aspect, is false if we look upon him in another. He is depraved, utterly depraved, in himself.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Sin of Man and the Justification of Man”, ¶17

The commentary on Romans puts it most compactly. Maurice voices St. Paul’s cry in Romans 7, and then the answer that came to him: “But thou art not evil; thou art righteous; Christ is in thee; thou art one with Him.” He continues:

A truth was revealed to him which was good for all his after years. He had no need to repeat the same terrible process again; God claimed him as a new creature in Christ. He was so then; He would be so always. But that revelation brought with it another. “With my flesh I serve the law of sin.” That is not altered by my conversion or my baptism; my conversion and baptism both declare it. In you, your flesh, your separate self, they both alike say, there dwelleth no good thing. Only while you assert the privilege of a spiritual creature, the privilege of not being shut up in self, of being married to a living Lord, only then art thou righteous, only then art thou alive.

— The Unity of the New Testament, “I. The Epistle to the Romans”, ¶20

The clause “only then art thou righteous” is the strongest conditional Maurice writes. It has to be read beside “thou art righteous” earlier in the same paragraph, and it does not take that back. In neither sentence is the righteousness the man’s own. It is Christ’s, and it is his only as he is “married to a living Lord”. Without that he is still what he is “in himself”. The next paragraph states the rule: “man in the flesh, in himself, is condemned, hopelessly condemned … man in the spirit, in Christ Jesus, is justified, redeemed, renewed” (¶21).

Both verdicts stand over every man. Trust decides which one his life is lived under. This is why Maurice prefers to speak of “individual depravity” rather than universal depravity: “By individual depravity I mean my own” (Lincoln’s Inn Sermons V, ¶18). The justification is universal. The depravity is what each man finds when he lives as if he were his own.

4. What baptism adds

Faith perceives. Baptism, for Maurice, does more than perceive. In his own voice he praises “the clear, simple belief of the Reformers that we are adopted into Christ by Baptism and are therefore children of God” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”, ¶21). From the Gospels and Acts he concludes that the Apostles took baptism to be

the sign of admission into Christ’s spiritual and universal kingdom, and consequently that every person receiving that sign was ipso facto a member of that kingdom.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, “Signs of a Spiritual Society”, ¶4

The heathen convert who answered the Apostles’ message “was received into the true human family” (¶14). After baptism God “bestows His Spirit upon them that they may enjoy these rights” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”, ¶16). These are not the words of a man for whom baptism only announces something.

What baptism admits a man to, though, is the fact, now owned and sealed. In the same paragraph about the Apostles, Maurice describes how the first converts understood their baptism, and one passage holds all three levels together:

His baptism said to him, This is your position; according to the conditions of it you are to live. … Your foes are not hindering you from obtaining a blessing; they are hindering you from entering into the fruition of one that has been obtained for you; they will laugh at you for pretending that it is yours; they will tell you that you must not claim it.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, ¶4

The blessing “has been obtained for you”: that is the fact. Baptism tells the man it is his and makes him a member of the body that says so: that is admission. What remains is “the fruition”: that is the life.

Later in the chapter Maurice states the condition on which adoption by baptism makes sense: “We do not, as I have again and again urged, cease to be children because we are disobedient children. If therefore Baptism were looked upon as the adoption into the state of children … the notion we are considering would be impossible” (¶37). The notion he means is that a baptized man can lose his baptismal state. The adoption is real, but it is adoption into a sonship that disobedience cannot cancel, because it was never the man’s own possession.

5. The language of becoming

Maurice uses the language of becoming freely. Two cases show it at its strongest. One is the sentence from the sermon on Psalm 119: “by this the Creator becomes a Father, and the creature a child.” The other is in the essay on regeneration. One clause before it denies that perception makes the fact, it says that the man who looks to the cross “becomes an inheritor” of eternal life. The Kingdom of Christ sets the limit on both:

Man does not, therefore, by Baptism, by faith, or by any other process, acquire a new nature, if by nature you mean, as most men do, certain inherent qualities and properties. He does not by Baptism, faith, or by any other process, become a new creature, if by these words you mean anything else than that he is created anew in Christ Jesus, that he is grafted into Him, that he becomes the inheritor of His life and not of his own.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, ¶34

Something does become. A man becomes an inheritor, and he lives from Christ’s life and no longer from his own. What never becomes is a new property in him. The man changes and the ground does not, and the change is that he stops claiming any ground of his own. Maurice insists the change is real. He endorses the Reformers’ testimony: “You are not free till you trust Him; till you are free, you cannot do the works of a freeman, but only those of a slave” (Theological Essays, “On Justification by Faith”, ¶7).

Maurice does not label the levels. The same words, son, righteous and new creature, move between them, sometimes within one sentence, as in “he becomes an inheritor of it. But his perception does not make the fact”. A reader who picks up one sentence will find him on one side, and a reader who picks up another will find him on the other. That is how he can seem to stand on both sides of the question. Read together, the passages show a single structure, and in the sermon on Romans 5:18 Maurice states it himself.

6. “I choose another parent”

The hardest test is Maurice’s lecture on 1 John 3, because there he uses the language of parentage for the life. He has told his hearers “that it is the duty of every man to believe that he is born of God”. Then he faces St. John’s sentence that whoever is born of God does not sin:

But the evil acts which I do, proceed from me. I cannot shift them upon any one else. How then can I say that I am born of God? I do not say it; that evil act is a disclaimer of my heavenly birth; here is the greatest proof that it is a lying act. I make myself another man. I choose another parent.

— The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XII, “The Devil and His Works”, ¶13

The next paragraph goes further: “a man identifying himself with that hellish seed makes himself a child of the devil, though he ought to count himself a child of God” (¶14). On its face this lets sin change a man’s parentage, which the verdict says it cannot do. But Maurice’s words keep the fact intact. The sinful act is a “disclaimer” and a “lying act”, and a man can only lie about something that is true. He “ought to count himself a child of God” even while he makes himself a child of the devil. The paragraph ends: “I have a right to say that God is my parent.” The sinner’s other parentage is real as a life and false as a fact. That is the life in its starkest form. The practical inference is the prodigal’s: “every one who has disclaimed his true birth by an evil act should reclaim it” (¶13).

7. Repentance is a change of mind about God

If faith does not make the relation, what is repentance for? Maurice answers in a village sermon on Acts 3:19:

To Repent is to change our minds; to think quite otherwise of some matter or some person than we have been used to think.

— Sermons Preached in Country Churches, “Sermon XLI. Repentance and Conversion”, ¶5

The mind that changes is the mind about God. Men took him for an enemy to be fled or bribed. Repentance learns from Christ that he is a Father. Maurice then makes a claim that seems backwards: only now does the man know he has sinned. “For now first they would feel that they had sinned. … No one says that till he feels that he has been disbelieving, distrusting, disobeying a Father” (¶8). The sense of sin follows the discovery of the relation. It is not a condition of entering it.

Forgiveness tests the scheme hardest, because forgiveness is a word about standing, and Maurice makes it conditional: “Your sins may be blotted out. You may be made new and pure creatures” (¶9). The same paragraph shows where the condition falls. First, “God owned them in Christ as His children.” They might go on as “selfish creatures, foes to God and to each other. But they might claim to be members of Jesus Christ … Thus their sins could be blotted out.” The owning comes first and is unconditional. The blotting out comes as the man claims what he has been given. In this sermon, then, forgiveness belongs to the life. The man is freed from what he has done because he stops living as the self that did it.

In The Kingdom of Christ Maurice turns this against the Evangelicals. If a man may not call himself a child of God until something has happened to him, the prodigal cannot repent:

If we are not allowed to call ourselves children of God, how can we be told to arise and go to our Father? If we are not to do this, what does our repentance mean? It can be nothing but a sinful selfish struggle after the blessings of corn and wine and the fatness of the earth, which we think we have lost; not a humble confession that we have made light of our birthright, and are no more worthy to be called sons.

— The Kingdom of Christ I, Part II, ch. IV, ¶35

Repentance does not make a man a son. It is the son’s confession that he has slighted his birthright, and only a man whose birthright it is can make that confession.

8. How the Creator “becomes a Father”

The sentence from the sermon on Psalm 119 can now be read in context. The sermon is about the will. The man whose “soul cleaveth unto the dust” finds that the failure lies not in his circumstances but in himself. Only then does he ask for the one thing worth asking:

As long as that weakness can be referred to anything but the man’s own will; as long as he feels the bondage to be in something else than that, he may ask for rains or fruitful seasons, for the relief from some pain, for the gift of children or of friends. But he will not say, Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven; he will not understand that this is the greatest blessing which the creature can ask or the Creator can give; that by this the Creator becomes a Father, and the creature a child. … That lore comes when he perceives that his will can only be free and loving, when the free and loving Will that formed his has subdued it to itself.

— Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer”, ¶12

“By this” refers to the doing of God’s will on earth, the man’s will subdued to the Will that formed it. What becomes is fatherhood carried out in a willing child, not fatherhood pronounced over the race. A father whose son runs from him is still his father. A father whose son’s will is one with his own is a father in a fuller sense. That is the only reading of “becomes” that does not contradict the Day of Humiliation sermon, preached the year before, which says the Father’s love “is not determined by my sense of it”.

Maurice does not gloss the sentence, but the same sermon comes close. To men who feel too unfaithful to call themselves God’s “faithful people”, he says: “let us remember that the moment we begin to ask new life of God, confident that we are asking according to His Mind, that moment we return to our true condition, as faithful, trusting creatures” (¶14). Prayer returns a man to a true condition. It does not admit him to a new one. The Kingdom of Christ supplies the rest: “We do not … cease to be children because we are disobedient children” (¶37). What God gives in answer to prayer is a renovated will, not a new relation.

9. What is left open

The question this note began with now has an answer. Knowing and owning one’s place makes no difference to God’s pronouncement, which already covers every man. It makes all the difference to the man’s life.

Maurice also says more about the man who refuses than one might expect. Sin is refusal: men’s sin “consists in their voluntarily refusing the blessings of God’s covenant” (Kingdom of Christ I, ¶34). Refusal can go all the way. In the same paragraph Maurice says that sin “may bring him into a condition of mind in which lying becomes the element of his being, in which truth is absolutely closed from his eyes”, and he cannot doubt that such mortal sin is possible. But he says at once what it does to the fact: “supposing this awful condition had actually taken place in any man, it could not change the fact in the least degree; it would establish the fact.” The essay on regeneration says the same from the side of judgment: “this will be the only condemnation, that they do not come to it, that they shut the eyes of their spirit to it” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”, ¶16). So the refuser’s standing is settled. He is a child of God living a lie, and his condemnation is the lie itself.

What stays open is how long the lie can last. On that Maurice will not pronounce: “I ask no one to pronounce, for I dare not pronounce myself, what are the possibilities of resistance in a human will to the loving will of God” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay: Eternal Life and Eternal Death”, ¶31). But he is not neutral. Reasoning about the nature of a spirit, he says, “I can find no escape” from the conclusion that the man who has chosen death stays in it. Against that he sets the revelation that death and hell are themselves cast into the lake of fire, and asks: “may I not accept that as a message from above, which meets all the wants, fears, longings of my heart?” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XXI, “The New Heaven and the New Earth”, ¶3). His argument leans one way and his hope the other, and he will not let either decide. That question belongs to a note on eternal death.

In short: for Maurice, faith does not make a man God’s child or make him righteous. God has already pronounced every man justified in Christ and condemned in himself. Baptism really adopts a man into the society that owns that fact, and trust decides which of the two truths he lives under. All becoming belongs to admission and to the life, never to the fact, which even a final refusal “could not change … in the least degree”.
