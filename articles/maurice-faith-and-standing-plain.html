What Faith Changes, According to F. D. Maurice

At the heart of F. D. Maurice’s understanding of Christianity is a distinction between what God has already made true about us and what happens when we come to recognize and live according to that truth.

Maurice believes that every human being is already related to God through Jesus Christ. God is not waiting for us to believe in him, repent of our sins, or undergo baptism before he can become our Father. He is our Father already. Christ is the head of humanity, and every person has a place in him. This relationship does not depend on anything we have done or believed. Its foundation is in God, not in us.

Yet Maurice also insists that faith, baptism, repentance, and obedience bring about real changes. He can speak of a person becoming an inheritor of eternal life, being made a new creature in Christ, or entering into a genuine relationship of trust and obedience in which the Creator becomes a Father and the creature a child. How can these things happen if our relationship with God is already established?

The answer lies in distinguishing three things: the relationship that God has established, our admission into the community that acknowledges it, and the life we actually lead in response to it.

These distinctions help make sense of Maurice’s thought, although he does not formally organize his teaching under these three headings.

Faith recognizes a reality it does not create

Maurice compares faith to sight.

Opening our eyes does not bring the visible world into existence. The world was there before we saw it. Yet sight makes an immense difference. A blind person cannot see what is nevertheless present all around him.

The same is true of faith. God does not begin to love us when we believe in him. Christ does not become the head of humanity when we acknowledge him. Faith enables us to recognize a spiritual reality that was true before we recognized it.

And just as sight allows us to find our way through the world, faith allows us to begin living according to the reality it reveals.

This explains why Maurice objects so strongly to making faith itself the object of our attention. He criticizes Christians who seem to believe more in the doctrine of justification by faith than in Christ who justifies them.

If our relationship with God depended on the strength or sincerity of our faith, we would continually have to examine ourselves to discover whether we had believed sufficiently. Our confidence would rest on our confidence in ourselves.

Maurice wants to direct our attention away from our own believing and toward Christ, who remains trustworthy regardless of the condition of our faith.

Faith does not make Christ our Saviour. It enables us to trust the Saviour who is already ours.

Two truths about every human being

But if everyone is already related to God through Christ, why does anyone need to be saved?

Maurice answers by insisting that two apparently contradictory judgments are true of every human being.

Considered in ourselves, apart from Christ, we are sinners and stand condemned. Considered in Christ, we are righteous, redeemed, and justified.

These are not judgments concerning two different groups of people, the wicked and the righteous. Both apply to everyone.

Maurice understands St. Paul to teach that all humanity is condemned in Adam and all humanity is justified in Christ. He does not take the second judgment to mean that our sins are unreal or unimportant. God judges us sinners because we really are sinners. But God also recognizes a truth about us that we could never establish for ourselves: we belong to Christ, and in him we are righteous.

The distinction is between the isolated self, which attempts to live independently of God, and the person as he belongs to Christ.

When we look at ourselves apart from Christ, we discover selfishness, weakness, distrust, and bondage to sin. We cannot find in ourselves the righteousness that would set us free. But this isolated existence is not the whole truth about us. We belong to Christ, and his life and righteousness are ours to share.

This distinction is Maurice’s own. He explicitly argues that what is true of humanity in one respect may be false in another.

It also explains why he can say both that we are righteous in Christ and that we are righteous only while living in union with him.

The second statement does not cancel the first. Our righteousness is never an independent possession. It belongs to Christ, and we share in it through our union with him.

Faith does not manufacture that righteousness or persuade God to attribute it to us. But only as we trust Christ and live in union with him do we actually enjoy its freedom and live according to it.

We can therefore live in two radically different ways: as though we were isolated beings dependent on our own resources, or as people whose true life is in Christ.

What is true of us in Christ does not depend on our faith. Whether we live according to that truth makes all the difference.

What baptism changes

Maurice also believes that baptism makes a real difference. It is not merely a ceremony announcing something that was already true.

He follows the Reformers in teaching that we are genuinely adopted into Christ through baptism. Baptism admits a person into Christ’s spiritual kingdom, the Church. From that moment the baptized person is a member of that society, with the rights and privileges belonging to its members.

But how can baptism adopt someone into Christ if Christ is already the head of every human being?

Maurice’s answer depends on the distinction between the universal relationship established through Christ and a person’s actual admission into the society that openly acknowledges and lives by that relationship.

The Church is the community that recognizes Christ as its head and claims the blessings that belong to humanity through him. Baptism genuinely receives a person into that community. It establishes his membership and gives him a recognized place in its common life.

Yet baptism does not create God’s fatherly relationship with him. It adopts him into a sonship whose foundation already exists.

The person who is baptized is told, in effect: This is your place. These are the blessings secured for you. You have been received into the society that acknowledges them. Now live according to what you have been given.

Maurice distinguishes between obtaining a blessing and enjoying one that has already been obtained. Baptism admits us into the Church and its privileges; it does not make Christ’s saving work true for the first time. And admission is not the same thing as enjoying the blessings into which we have been admitted.

Baptism is therefore a real act of adoption and admission, but it does not make God our Father for the first time.

This also explains why Maurice insists that disobedience cannot abolish our sonship. A disobedient child remains a child. He may deny his birthright and refuse to enjoy its blessings, but his refusal does not undo the relationship on which those blessings rest.

What sin changes

Maurice’s understanding of sin follows from this account of our relationship with God.

Sin is not merely the breaking of an external rule. It is a refusal to live according to the truth of our own existence.

When we act selfishly, distrust God, or seek to establish ourselves independently of him, we deny in practice that we belong to Christ and have God for our Father.

Maurice takes this denial with the utmost seriousness. Following St. John, he can even say that a sinner makes himself a child of the devil.

Yet this does not mean that sin successfully destroys our original relationship with God and establishes another in its place.

Maurice calls a sinful act a denial of our heavenly birth, but also a lying act. The sin is truly ours, and we are responsible for it. Nevertheless, the identity we assume through sin contradicts who we really are.

A person who makes himself a child of the devil by identifying with evil remains someone who ought to acknowledge himself as a child of God.

There is therefore something both real and false about the sinner’s chosen condition. It is real as a way of living, with real consequences for the person and for others. But it is false as an account of the person’s deepest identity.

Sin is the attempt to live as someone other than the person God has made us to be.

The way out of sin is not to establish a relationship with God that we previously lacked. It is to abandon the false life we have chosen and return to the truth we have denied.

What repentance changes

This understanding gives repentance a distinctive meaning.

Maurice begins with the ordinary meaning of repentance: a change of mind. But the most important change is in how we understand God.

We may have imagined God as an enemy, a threatening judge, or a distant power whose favour must somehow be purchased. Christ reveals that God is our Father.

When we begin to recognize this, we also begin to understand our sins more deeply.

Before recognizing God’s fatherhood, we may know that we have acted badly. We may regret the consequences of our actions or fear their punishment. But when we discover that God is our Father, we begin to understand that our wrongdoing has been a refusal to trust and obey someone who has loved us all along.

For Maurice, our recognition of God’s fatherly love does not follow from our repentance. Rather, it is the discovery of that love that makes genuine repentance possible.

He finds a particularly clear illustration in the prodigal son.

The prodigal does not set out to persuade his father to become his father again. He returns because he has a father. His repentance consists in acknowledging a relationship he has neglected and returning to the home that is rightfully his.

If he were not already a son, he could not repent of having despised his birthright.

Repentance is therefore not an effort to make God willing to receive us. It is the acknowledgment that we have distrusted and disobeyed the Father who has never ceased to claim us.

This does not mean that forgiveness and renewal are merely changes in our thinking.

Maurice speaks of sins being blotted out and of sinners becoming new and pure. These are real changes, not simply a more reassuring interpretation of our condition.

But the order matters. God first acknowledges people as his children in Christ. They are then called to claim that relationship, turn away from their selfish lives, and receive the freedom that belongs to them.

The relationship is already established. The forgiveness and renewal that transform the sinner’s actual life are received as he turns toward it.

How we become children of a Father who is already ours

One of Maurice’s most puzzling statements occurs in a sermon about prayer and the human will.

He says that when a person truly learns to pray, “Thy will be done,” the Creator becomes a Father and the creature becomes a child.

At first this appears to contradict his insistence that God is already our Father, regardless of whether we know or acknowledge him.

But Maurice is speaking here about the transformation of our wills.

We are created to live in harmony with God’s will, yet we experience our own wills as divided, selfish, and enslaved. We imagine that freedom means independence: the ability to do whatever we choose without submitting to anyone else.

Maurice believes that this supposed independence is actually a form of bondage. When we try to live from our isolated selves, we become slaves to the very desires and impulses we think express our freedom.

Our wills can become genuinely free and loving only when they are brought into harmony with the free and loving will of God.

This is what we ask for when we truly pray, “Thy will be done.”

We are not asking God to establish a fatherly relationship that did not previously exist. We are asking him to make us capable of living as his children.

A father remains a father even when his child rebels against him. But the relationship becomes something different in the child’s experience when the child begins to trust him, love him, and willingly share his purposes.

Similarly, God’s fatherhood is an established reality, but it becomes active and fulfilled in our lives as our wills are brought into agreement with his.

This is the sense in which the Creator becomes a Father and the creature becomes a child.

Something genuinely changes. The person’s will is renewed. His desires, choices, and actions begin to express the life that belongs to him through Christ.

But the change is not that God has become someone different, or that the person has acquired a new, independent source of righteousness.

The change is that the person begins to live from Christ’s life rather than from his own isolated self.

We do not become God’s children by learning to do his will. We learn to live as his children when our wills are brought into harmony with his.

Can someone refuse this relationship forever?

Maurice recognizes that people can resist God’s love, and he does not minimize how terrible this resistance may become.

A person may deliberately reject the truth of his relationship with God. He may become so attached to falsehood that he loses the ability or willingness to recognize what is true. Maurice regards this as a real and dreadful possibility.

Yet even such a refusal cannot change what God has established in Christ.

The sinner may deny his sonship, but he cannot make God’s fatherhood untrue. His condemnation consists in refusing the truth and remaining imprisoned in the false life he has chosen.

This raises a final and difficult question: Can a human being persist in this refusal forever?

Maurice does not offer a definite answer.

His uncertainty is not simply a matter of seeing arguments on both sides and being unable to choose between them.

When he reasons about the nature of a spiritual being and the consequences of freely choosing death, he finds himself driven toward the conclusion that someone who has made that choice must remain in it. He says that he can find no escape from this conclusion.

Yet Christian revelation confronts him with something his reasoning alone cannot establish. In the Book of Revelation, death and hell are themselves cast into the lake of fire, and those who have not found life are cast there also. Maurice does not understand this fire as a power independent of God. It is God’s fire, to be used for his purposes. He therefore finds grounds for hoping that even those who have rejected life may not be abandoned forever to the condition they have chosen. He cannot establish this hope by reasoning about the human will, but neither can he dismiss what revelation seems to hold before him.

Thus his reasoning presses him toward the terrible possibility of everlasting resistance, while revelation opens before him a hope that he cannot dismiss.

He refuses to turn either into a certainty.

He will not declare that any human being must finally be lost, but neither will he declare that every human being must eventually be restored.

What remains certain for him is the distinction on which his whole account depends: even the most terrible refusal cannot change the truth of what God has established in Christ.

The question is whether a person may forever refuse to live according to that truth.

The meaning of faith

Maurice’s understanding of Christianity reverses a familiar way of thinking about salvation.

We might imagine that human beings begin as strangers to God, separated from him until something happens to change their standing. They must repent, believe, be baptized, or undergo conversion before God can receive them as his children.

Maurice begins instead with God’s action in Christ.

God has already established the relationship. Christ is already the head of humanity. Every human being is already claimed by the Father through him.

The Christian message is therefore not that we can persuade God to become our Father, but that God is our Father, and that we are called to recognize and live according to this truth.

Faith enables us to recognize and trust what is already true. Baptism genuinely admits us into the Church, the society that acknowledges and claims this truth. Repentance is the recognition that we have denied our true relationship with God. Forgiveness and renewal free us from the sins and selfishness that have governed our lives. In learning to do God’s will, we begin to enjoy the freedom of children who trust their Father.

These are real and profound changes. But none creates the underlying relationship upon which they depend.

The distinction is between having a place in Christ and living according to that place.

The first is established by God and does not depend on our response. The second concerns the transformation of our actual lives.

We can be truly God’s children while living in rebellion against him. We can be righteous in Christ while finding nothing but sin and selfishness in our isolated selves. We can possess a birthright that we refuse to acknowledge and blessings that we refuse to enjoy.

The tragedy of sin is that we deny and resist what is most deeply true of us. The freedom of faith is that we begin to recognize, trust, and live according to that truth.

For Maurice, then, the great change brought about by faith is not that we become people whom God can at last acknowledge as his children. It is that we discover the Father who has always claimed us, recognize how profoundly we have contradicted his love, and begin through Christ to live as the children we truly are.
