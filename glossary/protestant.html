A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

Protestant

Protestantism · pure Protestantism · Romanist

Frequency in the corpus: Protestant and Protestantism in about 230 paragraphs. Chapter II of The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”, is the main source, with Theological Essays and the Lincoln’s Inn sermons.

In brief. Maurice sees Protestantism as positive, not merely negative. The Reformation asserted three great principles: justification by faith, election (the Absolute Will at the root of all things) and the Bible as the revelation of that Will, with a fourth, the religious ground of the nation. These were true witnesses against Roman denials. But when Protestants made them into systems for deciding who was justified or elect, they split the Church into sects, reduced the Bible to dry propositions, and lost the common object of faith. Protestant and Romanist each hold half a truth, and the Catholic Church, which belongs to both, is the reconciliation of the two.

Shades of meaning

  1. A standing-point of its own. Against those who think Protestantism only a protest, Maurice holds “that Protestantism has a standing-point of its own; that it is not merely condemnatory, merely negative; and that so far as it keeps within its own proper and appointed province, it denounces and condemns only that which is itself negative, and which sets at naught something that is needful for the life and being of man” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). The abuses of Rome “were not excesses; they were essentially evil; they had their root in a great denial and unbelief” (same). See principle and fact.

  2. The positive principles. “If then the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith be the first, and the doctrine of election, as formally asserted by Calvin, the second”, a peculiar estimate of the Scriptures is the third (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). “These three principles seem to me, in the strictest sense, positive principles. They are not the less so because they were brought forth in opposition to certain popular notions and current practices” (same). They answered each man’s new sense of being an individual; the fourth answered the new sense of a distinct national position. Luther taught that “the only safe, free, true position of a man, is not a position of rebellion, but of allegiance” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). See justification, Bible and nation.

  3. The Absolute Will. The deepest Protestant assertion is the Will of God as ground. “The idea of an absolute will, with which man must be brought into reconciliation by a Mediator, lay at the base of all Luther’s thoughts” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). Its explicit acknowledgment “gave the Reformation all its moral strength and grandeur, and above all, which imparted to the Protestant doctrine respecting the Bible all its meaning” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). See will and Absolute.

  4. Protestant systems. Maurice calls the principles good and the systems built on them ruinous. “Sectarianism has been the effect of the schemes which Protestants have adopted for the purpose of defining who have a right to be members of Christ’s Church, and who have not” (Theological Essays, “On Justification by Faith”); “they have sought to be wiser than God, and God has confounded their vanity” (same). The Bible itself suffered: “The Bible, in the hands of its orthodox teachers, was reduced into a set of dry propositions, about the limitations of which they were perpetually fighting” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Of the Catholic Church and the Romish System”). Justification became “a notion or theory about something which you call by this name” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). See system, sect and opinions.

  5. Protestant and Romanist. Each side bears a witness and each enfeebles it. The Protestant Dissenter “had done a good work, in asserting Protestantism to have a positive worth of its own, distinct from our Anglicanism, —though it seemed to me that he had failed to realize its worth” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter), while the Romanist bore witness for a Universal Church. The preaching of the Cross “is neither the Romanist nor the Protestant preaching, as they stand out in opposition to each other”, yet “it does justice both to the Romanist and to the Protestant method” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The First Epistle to the Corinthians”). The common Whitsuntide faith of Romish and Protestant lands has been truer “than the limitations which Romanists and Protestants have each discovered for it” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 2, “Universality of the Whitsuntide Message”). See Catholic and Church.

  6. Together for the world. If “the most resolute Protestant and the most resolute Catholic accept this justification and this unity” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 2, “The Faith of Englishmen”), they can go together to those perishing of luxury and of toil, and call both to rise up as men. The test of the Church is service, not its name. See society and brotherhood.

Neighbouring words

  • Romanist · Romish system (counterpart). The other half-witness, for a Universal Church, spoiled by a system of its own.
  • Catholic (reconciliation). The Church that belongs to Protestant and Romanist alike.
  • Reformation · Reformers (origin). Luther, Calvin and Zwingli, the bearers of the positive principles.
  • Dissenter · sect (outcome). What Protestant principle becomes when it hardens into a scheme of membership.
  • Anglicanism · English Church (Maurice’s own position). Held, in his view, without system, between both.

Scripture. The just shall live by faith (Romans 1:17). For other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ (1 Corinthians 3:11).
