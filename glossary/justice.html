A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

justice

just · do justly · justice and mercy

Frequency in the corpus: justice, just and unjust in about 230 paragraphs. The main sources are “Theology and Morality” and “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God” among the Lincoln’s Inn sermons, the essays “On the Evil Spirit” and “On the Atonement”, Lecture XIII of The Epistles of St. John and Sermon X of The Doctrine of Sacrifice.

In brief. Maurice’s chief quarrel about justice is with the idea that justice and mercy are at war in God and needed a scheme to reconcile them. He calls that idea revolting to the conscience and says it comes from the Evil Spirit, who tempts men to be both unjust and hard-hearted. In God, justice and mercy are one nature, and God’s justice is shown in forgiving and cleansing, not in a satisfaction that lets some men off a penalty. Justice is not made right by a bare decree, nor discovered by philosophy alone: the command to do justly needs the revelation of a Just Being who shows what He is by what He does, and Christ is that revelation. Among men the just and the merciful are not two kinds of character; each fails without the other. Human justice, the law court and the punishment of wrongdoers, keeps its dignity as a witness that righteousness rules the earth.

Shades of meaning

  1. No war in God between justice and mercy. “The notion, so often put forward in popular as well as scholastic writings, of a conflict in the Divine Mind between the attributes of Justice and Mercy, and of a scheme by which they are brought into reconciliation, would have been too revolting, I am sure, for the consciences of good and wise men, if they had recollected Who it was of Whom they were daring to speak” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God or in the Transactions of Men”). In prayer, he says, they could not have borne such a picture of “the Being Whose name is holy, Whose nature is one” (same). Christ shows them together, so that men “need not dream of qualities which the shock of the Fall had separated in their minds—mercy and justice, freedom and obedience—as having a corresponding conflict in the Eternal Mind” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon II). See mercy and Atonement.

  2. The division comes from the Evil Spirit. The old divines believed in an Evil Spirit tempting them “to be both unjust and hard-hearted” (Theological Essays, “On the Evil Spirit”), and so had a practical witness against the theory. “They could not but feel that to be in a healthful moral state they must be both just and merciful; that there must be a perfect unity and harmony between these qualities; that whatever puts them in seeming division comes from the Evil Spirit” (same). “The God who was to deliver them from this strife could not Himself be the subject of it” (same). See evil spirit.

  3. A notion of justice that outrages conscience. In “On the Atonement” he gives the objector’s case against the popular doctrine in full: that God’s justice could not be satisfied without infinite punishment, that Christ bore it as substitute, and that this rests on a notion of justice which men would not accept from one another, defended at last by saying we cannot know what God’s justice is (Theological Essays, “On the Atonement”). He grants the protest. On St. Paul’s words about God being just and the justifier, the popular reading means that “God does not manifest His righteous will and purpose, His righteous character, in the death of His Son; but, through the agency of that death, a certain notion of justice, quite distinct from the righteousness with which St. Paul uniformly identifies it, is satisfied” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon X). See righteousness and justification.

  4. Justice that forgives. God’s justice is the sinner’s refuge, not his danger. “His faithfulness and justice are the enemies of our sins; therefore to them we may turn from our sins. They are the refuges from the darkness that is in us. A faithful and righteous Being is therefore a forgiving Being. We are unforgiving because we are unfaithful and unrighteous. But His forgiveness is not tolerance of an evil; such tolerance is cruelty” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture III). The Mind “which demands justice and denounces evil, is one with the Mind in which lives all mercy and forgiveness” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon VII). See forgiveness.

  5. Do justly, not talk about justice. The Prophet bids men do justly, “not, Talk about Justice” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Theology and Morality”); love mercy, not admire it; walk humbly, not discourse about humility. The demand is a life, against tendencies “to be unjust, unmerciful, proud” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Theology and Morality”), and the hearer looks for a way out: “Is there no making up with long prayers for the lack of Justice, Mercy, Humility?” (same). Without the source of strength, doing justly may shrink to the code of a class, which means “not stealing from other men’s hoards when you have plenty of your own” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Theology and Morality”). See morality and duty.

  6. Justice needs a Just Being revealed. Is justice made right by a decree, as the priest says, or found in the nature of actions, as the philosopher says? “The conscience in men cries out for a Ruler; therefore it gives heed to the priest. It exists only in the affirmation that right and wrong are eternally opposed; therefore it gives heed to the philosopher” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Theology and Morality”). Maurice’s answer: “If you would have the command Do justly in place of a weight of rules and observances and ceremonies, you must have Justice set before you, not in words, formulas, decrees, but livingly, personally, historically. You must be taught what the Just Being is by seeing what He does—what He does for you” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Theology and Morality”). See revelation, conscience and knowledge.

  7. Justice and mercy in men. Men are sorted into the rigidly just and the tender-hearted, but “the contradiction is solely the effect of our imperfection; it has no existence in the nature of things” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XIII). “The just man becomes unjust from the want of that sympathy which enables him to understand the degrees of criminality in different men; the merciful man becomes unmerciful, because he is without a standard to which he can refer his own acts” (same). The philosopher who protests against dividing them still “sets Justice above Mercy, as the conscious sinner sets Mercy above Justice; he cannot teach us how we may both do justly and love Mercy” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Theology and Morality”). See love.

  8. Wavering human justice. The practical compromise between severity and leniency fails in the law court as in the nursery: “A criminal jurisprudence which is partly severe, partly lenient, misses the security of rules and the grace of gentleness” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God or in the Transactions of Men”). The theology that matches the compromise he calls “even more terrible” (same). See punishment.

  9. The justice of the Father in the ruler and the judge. Christ “reveals the royalty and the justice of the Father, from whom His royalty and justice are derived” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Sermon on the Mount: Its Teaching Respecting Forgiveness and Punishment”). The Sermon on the Mount does not abolish the magistrate: with Christ’s Spirit men may “vindicate His retributive law, to inflict on the wrongdoer punishments so stern, even, impartial, as shall be a lesson to all that Righteousness, and not Chance or Violence, rules in this earth of God’s” (same). Speaking to lawyers, he places the call of Christ “in the hall of justice, in our daily intercourse with each other, as citizens, as fathers, brothers, husbands” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Book of Proverbs Illustrated by St. John”). See king, law and nation.

Neighbouring words

  • righteousness (the Scriptural word he prefers). St. Paul identifies God’s justice with His righteousness, not with a satisfied claim.
  • mercy (its inseparable partner). One nature in God; each fails without the other in men.
  • satisfaction (the theory he rejects). A notion of justice satisfied apart from God’s character.
  • forgiveness (justice in act). A just Being is a forgiving Being, though never tolerant of evil.
  • punishment (human justice). Stern and impartial punishment is a witness that righteousness rules.
  • law (its outward form). Justice set forth in rules is not enough without a Just Being revealed.
  • humility (the third requirement in Micah). Do justly, love mercy, walk humbly together.

Scripture. What doth the Lord require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God? (Micah 6:8). If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins (1 John 1:9). That he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus (Romans 3:26). Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other (Psalm 85:10).
