A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

hope

the God of Hope · despair · the sure and certain hope

Frequency in the corpus: hope in over 400 paragraphs. The main sources are Lecture XI of The Epistles of St. John, “Hope; Its Ground, Object, and Effect”, and the Lincoln’s Inn sermons “The Gospel of Hope”, “The Misery of Limiting Our Hope in Christ” and “The Perfect Revelation”.

In brief. Hope for Maurice is a human privilege, like memory and trust, not an animal one: it binds man to man. Its ground is not anything in us but God, whom St. Paul dares to call the God of Hope, and its object is the full revelation of Christ. The Scriptures are Scriptures of hope for every nation, not a balance of hope and fear. Despair is the only utter perdition, because it binds a man in the prison of his evil nature; so we ought to stir up hope in every human being. Hope is often gained through despair, when we find we have no fountain of it in ourselves. And it is not limited to this life: a Christianity without hope beyond the grave would leave men more miserable than the heathen.

Shades of meaning

  1. A human privilege. St. Paul wants his children to exercise their human privileges. “What are these? To eat, to drink, to sleep? or, to remember, to trust, to hope? The first belong to us as animals; the others belong to us as men” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XI). The first support the life each has apart; the others “are exercises of the life which we have in common, which bind us as man to man” (same). “The more memory, the more trust, the more hope the better. The fear is lest they should wither and perish” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XI). See mankind and faith.

  2. Hope in Him, the Fountain of Life. Hope would wither without the message of “a Fountain of Life—a divine life from which human life is derived, by which its springs may be renewed” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XI). St. John’s words are “the simplest encouragement to remember that Christ is this Fountain of Life for every man, to trust in Him, to hope in Him” (same). St. Paul “knew that he was lost in his own selfishness till he began to believe in God, till he began to hope in God. Faith and Hope lifted him out of that abyss” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 1, “The Three Things Which Abide”). See life and love.

  3. The God of Hope, found through despair. “We too may gain it through despair. We may find that we have no fountain of hope in ourselves; that the springs which at one time we fancied were inexhaustible, are almost dry” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Gospel of Hope”). Then we learn what St. Paul meant by the phrase God of Hope, which is “absolutely incompatible with all theories, and with all high-sounding abstract titles, by which we have circumscribed the Divine Nature” (same). See Absolute.

  4. Scriptures of hope. St. Paul met both Jewish and Gentile objections with “this one principle, that the Scriptures were Scriptures of Hope” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Gospel of Hope”). They do not keep a balance of hope and fear. Their warnings against distrust are themselves “the most unbounded encouragement to trust and hope, to the continual acknowledgment of God as the Inspirer of Hope, as the Fulfiller of Hope” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Gospel of Hope”). See Bible and fear.

  5. Despair the only perdition. “It is an old commonplace of divinity, which we are strangely forgetting, that despair is the only utter perdition; because despair binds a man in the prison of his evil nature, and fastens the chain of the Evil Spirit upon him; because all hope points upwards to God, and is the response of our spirit to His Spirit. Therefore I say it again, We ought to stir up hope in every human being” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 1, “The Perfect Revelation”): hope for present help against sin, and hope that the glory of God the Deliverer shall be fully revealed. See evil spirit and Deliverer.

  6. Not in this life only. Those who said a spiritual resurrection already possessed was all Christ had given “divided the present from the future” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 5, “The Misery of Limiting Our Hope in Christ”). They taught “that Christ had come to confound and set at nought the dim hopes of all nations” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 5, “The Misery of Limiting Our Hope in Christ”), and so left Christians poorer than the heathen, whose visions of the future had at least some glimpses of hope. See body and eternal.

  7. Sowing in hope. Men who receive the Gospel of Christ’s birth “will not like savages wait upon accident, they will not wish to live by fruits that grow of themselves; they will sow in hope and plough in hope” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “Christmas Charities”). See society.

Neighbouring words

  • faith · trust (companion). Faith is the substance of things hoped for.
  • despair (opposite). The prison of the evil nature.
  • fear (false balance). The Scriptures are not a balance of hope and fear.
  • memory (sister privilege). Memory, trust and hope are the exercises of the common life.
  • expectation · Advent (form). The waiting for Christ’s full revelation.

Scripture. Now the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing (Romans 15:13). If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable (1 Corinthians 15:19). Every man that hath this hope in him purifieth himself, even as he is pure (1 John 3:3). We are saved by hope (Romans 8:24).
