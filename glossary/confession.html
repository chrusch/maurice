A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

confession

confess · confession of sin · confession of the Name

Frequency in the corpus: confess and confession in about 600 paragraphs, in two main senses. The main sources are Lectures III, XIV and XIX of The Epistles of St. John, the Lincoln’s Inn sermons for Lent (“The Body and the Members”, “The Vine and the Branches”) and for the Day of Humiliation of 1857, and “The Bond of Peace”.

In brief. Maurice uses the word in two senses that he keeps close together. To confess sin is not to list faults against an imaginary standard but to own that one has departed from the actual law under which all men live, and above all to own a broken relationship: no one says we have sinned until he feels he has been distrusting a Father. Confession of sin is therefore a renunciation of the sin, made to God because the truth in us is His. It is grounded on God’s faithfulness, it is individual and also social and national, and it needs no confessor’s casuistry. To confess the Name, or that Jesus is come in the flesh, is to acknowledge a fact and a Lord: one Mediator between the visible and the invisible, a government to bow to. The two senses meet because confession of sin is always confession of the God from whom we have turned.

Shades of meaning

  1. Confession as renunciation. “The confession of the sin that is in us is nothing less than this disclaimer, this renunciation, of the sin. But to whom do we confess the sin? who, besides ourselves, do we conceive is interested in that renunciation? We confess the sin to God” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XIX). We confess to God because the truth in us, St. John’s phrase, is His truth, there because we are His children. “The sin is the denial of this relationship; the setting up to be independent of Him” (same). See sin and truth.

  2. Confessing to a Father. Men may feel restless, ashamed or remorseful, “but they will not have said We have sinned. No one says that till he feels that he has been disbelieving, distrusting, disobeying a Father; that he has been fighting against One who was always caring for him and doing him good” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XLI, “Repentance and Conversion”). The Psalmist’s confession is made to “the Forgiver of sin and the Deliverer from sin” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer”). See repentance and Father.

  3. Grounded on God’s faithfulness. Confession is not hard doctrine, for God is faithful and just to forgive: “His faithfulness and justice are the enemies of our sins; therefore to them we may turn from our sins” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture III). In the Christian sense, when a man confesses his sins, “i.e. his separation from Christ and His sacrifice,—he asks that he may be restored to the blessing of that sacrifice” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). See justice and sacrifice.

  4. Lent: departure from the actual law. Lent “is not a time for confessing that we have departed from some high and imaginary standard of excellence. It is a time for confessing our departure from the actual law under which we and all human beings are placed. It is a time for individual confession” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Body and the Members”). Asked what we most have to confess, we find no distinct answer, only “a vague sense of duties omitted that might have been done; of tempers indulged that might have been repressed” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Vine and the Branches”), and most of all a barrenness. See law and duty.

  5. National confession. Moses confessed his people’s sin as the forgetting of their holy state, “because they had gone out of the right way, forgetting that they were a nation, each man preferring a selfish way of his own” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “National Holiness”). On the Day of Humiliation for the Indian Mutiny he bids each hearer, before blaming the Company or the Government, to confess his own indifference “to the well-being and well-doing of my fellow-countrymen and countrywomen in India” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”), and to own that England “has had a mighty empire committed to her, for which she must render an account” (same). See nation and holy.

  6. No confessor’s casuistry. Peter’s question how often to forgive was “an opening for the refinements of a Confessor or Spiritual Director! How accurately the degrees, qualities, occasions, times, objects, places of forgiveness, might be set down!” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Sermon on the Mount: Its General Principle”). “In our Lord’s answer not a single subtlety” (same). In the quarrel over the confessional he takes neither side, and asks instead what the Prayer Book’s exhortation before Communion really means (see absolution). See forgiveness and system.

  7. Confessing that Jesus is come in the flesh. “To confess that Jesus the anointed is come in the flesh, is to confess that there is a medium of spiritual communications between the visible and the invisible world, between earth and heaven. It is to confess that there is one Mediator for all men” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XIV). The Spirit at Corinth was “leading them to confess a Lord, to bow down their spirits to a government, and to confess Jesus the crucified to be the Person who is exercising that government” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The First Epistle to the Corinthians”). See Mediator, Incarnation and king.

  8. A united confession of the Name. “A united Confession of the Name, a united Worship of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit,—such a Confession,—such a Worship as the past contains only a dim shadow and prophecy of,—we have a right to look for” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Bond of Peace”). “But the confession will only be united when we cease to confound our feeble expressions of trust and affiance, our praises and adorations, with Him to Whom they rise” (same). See Name, Creed and unity.

  9. The confessors. The old creeds were once “the strength and nourishment of confessors and martyrs” (Theological Essays, “On the Son of God”), not school divinity. The belief in an invisible world united to the visible “was the strength in which the Christian confessor lived and died” (The Religions of the World, Lecture VIII). See witness.

Neighbouring words

  • absolution (its answer). The declaration of forgiveness that confession seeks.
  • repentance (its root). The change of mind toward a Father.
  • renunciation (what confession is). The disclaimer of the sin as no part of us.
  • witness and testimony (confession of faith). Bearing witness to the Name.
  • Creed (the Church’s confession). The confession of the Name, not a school formula.
  • confessional (the system he distrusts). Casuistry of degrees and occasions.
  • humiliation (national confession). A day for owning a nation’s sin.

Scripture. If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness (1 John 1:9). Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God (1 John 4:2). Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee (Luke 15:18). That every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father (Philippians 2:11). Confess your faults one to another (James 5:16).
