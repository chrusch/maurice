A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

glory

glorify · glorying · self-glory

Frequency in the corpus: glory and its forms in about 570 paragraphs. The main sources are Sermon IX of The Lord’s Prayer (on the doxology), Lecture XIII of The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, “The Glory and Humiliation of the King”, and three country sermons: “The Glory of the Cross”, “The Eternal Weight of Glory” and “Suffering and Glory”.

In brief. God’s glory, for Maurice, is the glory of a Being whose name is Love, and so it is the opposite of self-glory. It does not crush men or compete with their good; God raises His creatures above themselves by giving them Himself to behold. That glory was shown in human weakness, in a cradle and a carpenter, and it could only come forth fully in suffering and the Cross. So the glory a man may rightly seek is never his own: it is a common glory, like the sun’s, which all may see and none may own. The opposite is glorifying oneself, on one’s nation, one’s sect, one’s gifts or even one’s faith, which is the root of party and persecution and which no religious system protects us from. St. Paul’s glorying in the Cross is the reversal: the Cross nails down every boast of man against man and binds him to all men. Glory to come is the full manifestation of Christ, the fruition of God for which every nation sighs.

Shades of meaning

  1. The glory of Love. If the glory is His whom we call Father, “we dare not believe for an instant that it is a Self-glory of which we are speaking. It must be that which is the eternal opposite and contradiction of Self-glory; the glory of a Being whose name and nature is Love” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon IX). God would not be seeking the good of His creatures “if He did not raise them above themselves; if He did not give them a perfect absolute object to behold, and to dwell in” (same). Without that object man is left to be a Narcissus, finding no object so worthy of his adoration as himself. See love and Father.

  2. Glory in weakness. The Kingdom of God is connected with “an Epiphany or manifestation of the divine glory in human weakness” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XIII). The Son of Man was showing forth His glory and His Father’s, and “He was shewing that glory not to be destructive, but life-giving and restoring” (same). “The glory of God revealing itself, not in a leader of armies, a philosopher, a poet, but in a carpenter—could anything be more revolting?” (Theological Essays, “The Incarnation”). See manifestation and Incarnation.

  3. Only the Cross could make it known. “The glory of the Christ, the Son of God, could not be manifested in the most august words. It could only come forth in suffering. Only the Cross could make it known” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XIII). The disciples, disputing who should be greatest, “could not enter into the humiliation of the Son of Man; therefore they could not enter into His glory” (same). St. Paul wanted the Corinthians to see “how God’s glory is the humiliation and passion and death of Jesus Christ” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXIX, “The Eternal Weight of Glory”). See sacrifice and Son of Man.

  4. Glorying in the Cross. St. Paul had given up glorying in his privileges as a Jew, but “he had not given over glorying because he did not glory in being unlike the rest of men” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XVIII, “The Glory of the Cross”). The Cross is what fastens him to all men: “The nails of this Cross go through the world’s opinions, and my own” (same). Those who glory in it join the song of heaven: “Worthy is the Lamb that was slain to receive glory, and dominion, and praise, and honour” (same). See Atonement and sacrifice.

  5. A common glory. The weight of glory “was not a glory that belonged to him, or which ever could belong to him. The sun rose in great glory this morning” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXIX, “The Eternal Weight of Glory”), and all over Europe men might behold it and rejoice. St. Paul had once desired a glory of his own; “Then it was His glory which he desired to behold hereafter, which he desired that all should behold” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XL, “Suffering and Glory”), the common glory “which had been coming forth through all man’s own darkness” (same). See Catholic and mankind.

  6. Self-glorification. “I believe that we are one and all haunted by this tendency to self-glorification every day and hour of our lives; that no religious systems, no religious practices, are a protection against it” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon IX). “It signifies not under what pretext, philosophical, political, theological, we build altars to ourselves; the worship is in all cases equally accursed” (same). The faith that glorifies itself is the worst case: “More malice and wickedness have flowed and are flowing from that self-glorifying faith than from any other source whatever” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Faith of Protestants”). See self-will and sect.

  7. The glory of humanity. Maurice grants a real truth to those who speak of the glory of man, but humanity “cannot be contemplated merely as an abstraction; it must be seen in some one” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon IX). Christ “manifested forth the true state and glory of man, as the child of God” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon III). Men will either make their powers into substitutes for God, or “they will be seen to have derived their greatness and glory from the God who so loved the world as to give His Son for it” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XVII). See Son of Man and humanity.

  8. Glory as the beholding of God. The Collect for the Epiphany asks for “the fruition of Thy glorious Godhead” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Glory of the Chosen Nation Realized in the Illumination of the World”). Israel rightly felt that its discipline pointed to something higher than the sight of a perfect Man; “there is in us all a deep sighing for Home, a longing which nothing but this beholding of God can satisfy” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Glory of the Chosen Nation Realized in the Illumination of the World”). When a reconciling God is revealed, “The death of self is the beginning of a new life, of affections, energies, memories, hopes. These have their fruition in God. These realize their glory when He is revealed” (same). See knowledge and eternal.

  9. Glory to come. “The manifestation of Christ in the glory of His Father and of the Holy Angels is the highest object which we in this Dispensation are able to long for” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered as a Declaration of Christ’s Death to Mankind”). St. Paul hoped “because he believed that His glory is to fill the earth” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XL, “Suffering and Glory”), as the sun brings everything hidden into light. See judgment and hope.

Neighbouring words

  • manifestation and Epiphany (how glory appears). In weakness, in the cradle and on the Cross.
  • humiliation (glory’s other face). No entry into His glory without it.
  • self-glory and self-glorification (the counterfeit). Altars built to ourselves on any pretext.
  • glorying (the act). Rightly in the Cross alone; wrongly in being unlike other men.
  • fruition (glory possessed). The enjoyment of God for which the nations sigh.
  • light (the image). The sun’s glory, common to all who see it.
  • majesty and dignity (kingly glory). Held by Christ only through condescension.

Scripture. For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen (Matthew 6:13). But God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ (Galatians 6:14). For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory (2 Corinthians 4:17). For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6). A light to lighten the Gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel (Luke 2:32).
