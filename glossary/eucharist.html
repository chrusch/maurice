A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

Eucharist

Communion · the Lord's Supper

Frequency in the corpus: Eucharist and its adjective 269; communion 303 (all senses).

In brief. The sacrificial feast in which the Church is brought into the presence of God through the one finished sacrifice of Christ, and in which Christ is really present to the worshippers. It is the sign of continuance as Baptism is the sign of entry: the constant bond of a universal fellowship, given by Christ, and in Maurice’s view the fullest embodiment of his kingdom. Like Baptism, it asserts a reality that exists before the worshipper’s faith and does not depend on it.

Shades of meaning

  1. The highest service, interpreting the rest. Maurice introduces it as the act that gives worship its meaning: “there is one service which is supposed to be of a higher character than all the rest, and to give them their worth and their interpretation. This is the service which belongs to a feast, called sometimes the Lord’s Supper, sometimes the Eucharist, sometimes the Communion” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”).

  2. The kingdom embodied. The feast holds together what parties divide: Christ “had embodied in a living feast the complete idea of His kingdom, which we, looking at things partially, from different sides … are continually reducing under some name, notion, or formula of ours” and “had given it as a bond to all peoples and languages and generations” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”).

  3. A sacrificial feast, not a repeated sacrifice. Maurice calls it sacrificial and holds that “the character of the Eucharistic feast is sacrificial, that Christ is really present in it, and that the words of institution are to be taken literally” (“The Eucharist”). But the sacrifice is the finished one, entered into, not supplemented: he rejects the Roman view of “the Eucharistic sacrifice” as a means of obtaining “those advantages and blessings which Christ’s sacrifice has not fully procured for us” (“The Eucharist”).

  4. Entrance into the presence of God. The privilege the sacrament declares is corporate: “that the Church is brought into the presence of God, is the first principle of the New Dispensation, the one which is especially involved in this sacrament” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”). It belongs to each “not in proportion as we raise ourselves to some individual excellence, but in proportion as we renounce all such distinctions” (same paragraph, “The Eucharist”).

  5. More than a memorial. Against the Zwinglian “Simple memorial” Maurice argues from the Passover: “That it is the memorial of a past transaction is of course assumed in every word I have said. If it were not it could have no pretence to the name of Eucharist; it would bear no analogy to the Passover. But the Passover had not merely reference to the past” (“The Eucharist”).

  6. Real presence without a change in the elements. The presence is Christ himself, “the ground of faith, and not as grounded upon it” (“The Eucharist”); the elements are consecrated “not that they may acquire some essential and miraculous virtue, but that they may be diverted from their ordinary uses, that they may become purely sacramental” (“The Eucharist”). What is needed is that they “should be made a perfectly transparent medium through which His glory may be manifested” (same paragraph).

  7. A protection for the weak, not a reward for saints. In the Lincoln’s Inn sermons Maurice turns the doctrine toward ordinary hearers, warning against thinking “of the Eucharist rather as a transcendent service for the use of high saints, than as a protection for weak and sinful men from even the grossest and lowest vices” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Sacramental Character of the Bible”).

  8. The pledge of the new commandment. In the lectures on St John, the Eucharist is the institution that makes brotherly love a corporate reality: “The Eucharist was not a fresh burthen added to that commandment. It enabled the whole society to own the commandment as a living power” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII).

Neighbouring words

  • Baptism (companion sign). The sign of entry beside the sign of continuance. See baptism.
  • sacrifice (its content). The feast is sacrificial because it is communion in the one sacrifice (sense 3). The cross, says Maurice, is “the centre point of all fellowship” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”). See sacrifice.
  • communion, fellowship (what it effects). The same paragraph explains why the feast unites men to each other: “as self-will and disobedience are the obstacles to the communion of men with their Creator, so are they obstacles to communion with each other” (“The Eucharist”).
  • presence (what it declares). Sense 4 and sense 6.
  • Passover, feast (type). Sense 5.
  • memorial, notion, theory (reductions). The parties’ partial accounts of it (senses 2 and 5).
  • sacrament (genus). The Eucharist is one sacrament among many in a sacramental world: “No doubt the world is full of sacraments. Morning and evening, the kind looks and parting words of friends, the laugh of childhood, daily bread, sickness and death; all have a holy sacramental meaning” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”).
  • priest, absolution (ministry). The same person absolves and administers the Eucharist, and Maurice insists the two be understood together.

Scripture. Matthew 26:26, This is my body (KJV), which Maurice faults the Lutherans for not taking “in a sufficiently plain and literal sense” (“The Eucharist”); 1 Corinthians 11:26, ye do shew the Lord’s death till he come; 1 Corinthians 10:3–4, did all eat the same spiritual meat, the text of “The Sacramental Character of the Bible”.
