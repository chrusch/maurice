A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

Absolute

Infinite · absolute Being

Frequency in the corpus: absolute 280 (90 capitalised); infinite 365. The fullest treatment is in What Is Revelation?, Maurice’s reply to Mansel’s Bampton Lectures.

In brief. Maurice uses absolute in two ways. In theology it describes God: absolute Love, absolute Goodness, the absolute Will, the absolute Being from whom law and order come. In his controversy with Mansel and Hamilton it names what philosophy has always sought, the Absolute and Infinite, which they said men could not know. Maurice answers that the search becomes a contradiction only when we try to grasp the Absolute in a notion of our own; the Absolute himself has stirred men to it because he means to reveal himself, and has done so in the Son. Infinite most often qualifies love, goodness and charity.

Shades of meaning

  1. Absolute Love. Maurice declared his “most entire and cordial sympathy with the declaration of the Unitarians, that God is pure and absolute Love—that God is a Father” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter). Christmas and Passion Week alike are presented “as proceeding from the Eternal Absolute Love of the Father” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Passion Week; its Dangers and Blessings”). His parting words at Lincoln’s Inn: “I feel no sorrow and repentance for any words I have spoken to you, respecting the absolute and eternal love of God” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XXIII). See love.

  2. Absolute Goodness come forth. “The perfect Son, by His obedience, had revealed the perfect Father; the Absolute Goodness had come forth in all the relations and sympathies of the man” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XIV). Faith that Jesus is the Christ means “a living bond between me, the poor, helpless human creature, and the absolute perfect Being” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XVI). See Mediator and manifestation.

  3. The ground of order. “We are in an order, not a chaos, because there is an absolute Being, a living Being, a righteous Being, from whom law and order proceed. We look upon every mythology as a search after that Being” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Homily II: On Theology and Government in India”). The king’s position “has its ground in the acknowledgment of an unseen absolute Being” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “The Scriptural View of This Constitution”). See constitution · order and king.

  4. The absolute Will. “Only suppose the absolute will to be a will to good, and how can we imagine that happiness, obedience, freedom, have their origin any where but in Him” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). The Passion is “the simple surrender of the human will to the Absolute Will, the refusal of the Son to be anything except in union with His Father” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Passion Week; its Dangers and Blessings”). Calvinism was right to begin from “the absolute will of God” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”), wrong in what it made of it. See will and sacrifice.

  5. The philosophers’ search. From Hamilton Maurice takes the fact that “From Xenophanes to Leibnitz, the Infinite, the Absolute, the Unconditioned, formed the highest principle of speculation” (What Is Revelation?, Letter II), and turns it against him. Hamilton’s own spirit “demanded that resting-place in the Absolute and Eternal, which he said that men were not permitted, by the conditions of their intellect, to seek after” (same). See seeking.

  6. Revealed, not comprehended. “The search after the Absolute becomes a contradiction when we try to comprehend it in a notion of our own minds. But the Absolute Himself has stirred you to it, because it has been His purpose to reveal Himself to you” (same), Maurice would say to the Buddhist. See revelation and opinions · notions.

  7. Knowing the Infinite. The Prayer Book has us say every morning that in knowledge of God standeth our eternal life; what hypocrisy that would be “if we have thoroughly persuaded ourselves that to know the Infinite and Eternal is impossible!” (What Is Revelation?, “Letters to a Theological Student”). Worship in the English Church claims “the privilege of approaching at once through the one Mediator the throne of the Absolute and the Infinite” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”). See knowledge and worship.

  8. Infinite love and charity. In ordinary use infinite qualifies God’s love. “The Love, the infinite Love, must be God’s Love. If the death of Christ showed us what the infinite Love is, it showed us what God’s Love is” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XIII). The saints spoke “of the Trinity as the infinite, all-embracing charity” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XXVI). See Trinity.

Neighbouring words

  • Eternal (its companion). Senses 5 and 7; see eternal.
  • perfect (its moral form). Sense 2; “the absolute Perfect Being, whose name is Love” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XVI).
  • Unconditioned, Cause (the philosophers’ words). Sense 5.
  • notion, conception (how it cannot be held). Sense 6.
  • I AM, Being (the scriptural name). See I AM.
  • Mediator, Son (the way to it). Senses 2 and 7.
  • mere power, fate (its false forms). Of the Greeks: “the absolute Being was regarded as a dreary fate” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Signs of a Spiritual Society”).

Scripture. 1 John 4:8, God is love (sense 1); John 17:3, this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God (sense 7); Acts 17:27, that they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him (senses 3 and 5); 1 Kings 8:27, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee.
