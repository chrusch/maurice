A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

opinions

notions · notional

Frequency in the corpus: opinion and opinions 692; notion and notions about 1,100. The words are thickest in The Kingdom of Christ, “Pure Protestantism” and “Signs of a Spiritual Society”, and in the Dedicatory Letter to The Doctrine of Sacrifice.

In brief. What men think about God, Christ or the Church, as against God, Christ and the Church themselves. For Maurice an opinion or notion is a product of the human mind, partial and changeable, and it becomes dangerous when it is put in the place of the reality it describes. Faith is not a set of opinions; truth is not a notion; the Church does not stand on notions about the sacraments but on the sacraments. He welcomes an age that can no longer rest on opinions, because it may then be driven to the foundation. The words are nearly always pejorative, and he expects to be called a “notionalist” for attacking them.

Shades of meaning

  1. Notions about, not the thing. “Truth, according to St. John, is not dead, but living; not a notion, or set of notions which we comprehend, but a Being who comprehends us; not a theory concerning God, but God Himself” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XX). The Apocalypse uses signs for the same reason: “may not the signs denote his actual presence, while abstract words would only express some notions about him?” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture I). See truth and sign.

  2. Faith not a set of opinions. Maurice’s critics found him “wanting in the proper opinions” because one of them “regards the Christian faith as made up of a certain set of opinions—an opinion about the resurrection, an opinion about the judgment, an opinion about sacrifice, an opinion about the Trinity” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter). Luther, rightly, “did not call upon men to acknowledge either a new doctrine or an old one, to believe either in a certain opinion concerning justification or in a certain opinion concerning the atonement. He called upon them to believe in God the Father Almighty” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). See faith and Creed.

  3. Believing in our belief. The worst effect of resting on opinions is idolatry of them: “We shall think that it is our notions about Christ, not He Himself, who is upholding the pillars of Heaven and earth. We shall believe in our belief about the Trinity, not in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The World and the Church”). So Maurice is glad when men “cannot find any opinions upon which they may cast anchor” (same). See ground · root · foundation.

  4. Systematic religion. Edward Irving taught him how different true godliness was “from the sentimental religion, which consists in feelings about God; or from the systematic religion, which consists in notions about Him” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter). Notions are the stuff systems are made of; see system, sense 6.

  5. Truths turned into notions. A living truth can be degraded into a notion. The Divine Unity, “this awful, everlasting primary truth, had been turned by the (so called) Unitarians into a mere notion or dogma” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Signs of a Spiritual Society”). A false doctrine of Scripture “turns living ideas into mere notions and apprehensions of our minds” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Scriptures”). See unity, sense 4.

  6. Knowledge of what is. Against opinion stands knowledge: “What man wants is a knowledge of that which is; he cannot be content with opinions and notions about that which may be” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Of the Catholic Church and the Romish System”). Our self-made moral notions are to be cleared away as in natural science: “These notions are, indeed, poor things; we must try to get clear of them in moral studies as we do in physical” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XII). See knowledge.

  7. Notions about the signs. A Church may stand in its signs or in its opinions about them. “If the Romish body say that it stands in certain notions about sacraments and about orders, and not in its sacraments and in the orders themselves,” it condemns itself (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”). God “has not left it to the faith and feelings and notions of men; that He has given us permanent signs of its existence” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Modern English Systems”). See sacrament.

  8. The notionalist’s charge. Maurice knows how he will be read: “Any one who begins the work of encountering notions and theories, will himself be accounted the greatest notionalist and theorist” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Dedication”).

Neighbouring words

  • theory, dogma, doctrine (near synonyms). Senses 1, 5 and 8.
  • system (what opinions are built into). Sense 4; see system.
  • truth, fact, reality (their opposites). Senses 1 and 6.
  • faith, belief (what they are mistaken for). Senses 2 and 3; see faith.
  • knowledge (what man actually wants). Sense 6.
  • sign, sacrament (what witnesses beyond them). Senses 1 and 7.
  • foundation (what they cannot supply). Sense 3; see ground · root · foundation.
  • party, sect (their social result). The Catholic principle is “that all unity is to be in Christ, and that intellectual notions and opinions ought not to divide men from Him” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Modern English Systems”); see unity, sense 3.

Scripture. 1 Corinthians 3:11, other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ (sense 3); John 14:6, I am the way, the truth, and the life, behind sense 1; 1 Kings 18:21, How long halt ye between two opinions?
