A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

parable

parables · the parables of the world

Frequency in the corpus: parable and its forms in about 280 paragraphs. The main sources are Sermon VI of What is Revelation?, “Christ’s Parables” (also preached at Lincoln’s Inn as “The Parables of Christ”), and Lecture XII of The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, on the sower.

In brief. Maurice sets aside three common reasons given for the parables: that we cannot know divine things except through imperfect likenesses, that ignorant fishermen needed stories, and that parables were veils to hide truth from the profane. Christ’s own answer is that the disciples are given to know the mysteries of the Kingdom, and that the parables speak to those who see without seeing. The parables work because there is a real correspondence between the organs of the spirit and the organs of sense: sensible things, and the dealings of men, have been God’s lesson-books from the foundation of the world. So the divine things are not only spoken in parables but done in parables all about us, in every street and alley, and Christ withdrew the veil from common facts. The sower is the parable of parables and the key to all the rest. Parables and miracles are two forms of the same signs of the Kingdom. The apostles told so few parables because they preached the root of them, God’s union with mankind in a Mediator.

Shades of meaning

  1. Not likenesses, stories or veils. It has been said that we cannot know divine things except through “very imperfect likenesses derived from nature or the transactions of men” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”), that fishermen were best taught “through stories and apologues” (same), or that the parables were “veils purposely contrived to hide sacred truths from the gaze of the profane multitude” (same). Christ’s answer contradicts all three, for He tells the fishermen that it is given them to know the mysteries. See mystery and knowledge.

  2. The organs of the spirit. Seeing, hearing and understanding are gifts; there is something “exactly corresponding to these organs of sense in the spirit of man” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”). That correspondence explains the parables: “May not all sensible things, by a necessity of their nature, be testifying to us of that which is nearest to us, of that which it most concerns us to know, of the mysteries of our own life, and of God’s relation to us?” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”). See sign and conscience.

  3. God’s lesson-books. If the processes of Nature and the transactions of men “had been, from the foundation of the world, the lesson-books out of which God was instructing men, and revealing Himself to them” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”), then Christ fulfilled the prophet’s words “by withdrawing the veil which had been covering these common facts, by showing what was behind them!” (same). See revelation and nature.

  4. Done in parables. Mark’s version, Unto them that are without all things are done in parables, is the fullest: “The divine things are not merely spoken through parables, they are done in parables,—the parables are about us, only, through the want of a light coming from within, we do not recognize them” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”). Preachers have interpreted “the parables which they have found in every street and alley, as well as in every hill and stream” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”). We may become “deaf to the teachings of the Spirit within, blind to the parables of the world without!” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”). See Spirit and world.

  5. The parable of parables. The sower “may be called in one sense the parable of parables” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XII). If the King of men is also the King of nature, “we should certainly feel why the parable of the Sower must be the key to all parables” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The Miracles”). Before the disciples had its meaning they had learnt that “They were dwelling amidst mysteries” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XII). See kingdom and grace.

  6. Words and acts. “I have no wish, therefore, to divide those acts which we call miracles, from those words which we call parables. They are as distinct as words must always be from acts. They are illustrations of the same divine dominion” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XII). See sign and king · dominion.

  7. Nature renovated. The parables renew what is apt to become flat in Nature “by connecting it with the life of man, with the relations of God to man” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XII). “Every fact in nature becomes a fact of humanity, a fact of divinity” (same), and the meaning and mystery of the union of the two worlds is one “to which the peasant has as much access as the doctor” (same). See nature and fact.

  8. Particular parables. For the prodigal son “there is no sufficient commentary upon that parable but the Cross itself” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XIX). The unjust steward’s deepest meaning lies in the name: “For the Jew to believe that he was a steward—for us to believe that we are stewards—is the great necessity” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XX). See sacrifice and nation.

  9. Why the apostles told few. The apostles used few parables because, preaching a Son of God and a Son of Man, “they were going to the very root of this teaching” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”). Once God’s union with mankind in a Mediator is taken “as the kernel mystery of the Universe” (same), “we cannot suppose that we rise to conceptions of God through the things of time and sense, we cannot help supposing that through these things He is speaking to us” (same). See Mediator and Son of Man.

Neighbouring words

  • apologue (the reduced view). A story to make truth lively for the ignorant.
  • veil (the false view, and the true). Not a cover for truth, but the cover Christ withdrew from common facts.
  • mysteries of the Kingdom (what the parables show). Given to the disciples to know.
  • miracle (the act beside the word). Both are signs of one divine dominion.
  • analogy (Butler’s word). A real analogy between things of earth and things of heaven.
  • sower and seed (the key parable). The figure for how the Word meets the soil of man.

Scripture. Why speakest thou unto them in parables? (Matthew 13:10). Unto them that are without, all these things are done in parables (Mark 4:11). I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things which have been kept secret from the foundation of the world (Matthew 13:35). Behold, a sower went forth to sow (Matthew 13:3). And his disciples asked him, saying, What might this parable be? (Luke 8:9).
