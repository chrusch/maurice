A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

justification

justify · Justifier

Frequency in the corpus: justify, justification and their forms 682, many in the ordinary sense. The main sources are the Lincoln’s Inn sermon “God the Justifier of Man” and The Kingdom of Christ* I, “Pure Protestantism”.*

In brief. Justification is God’s act of making himself known to the ungodly as the Righteous Being and raising them, through trust in him, to the righteousness for which they were made. Maurice keeps the word bound to righteous and righteousness, as in St. Paul’s Greek. God is the Justifier; faith is the response. He rejects any notion that God holds men to be what they are not, which would ascribe a fiction to the God of Truth. Luther’s witness was to a Justifier, and Maurice faults the Lutheran system for teaching men to believe in justification by faith instead. Justification belongs to the race in Christ, whom God justified as his Son by raising him.

Shades of meaning

  1. Cognate with righteousness. Our translators have used “righteous”, “righteousness”, “justify” and “justification” for words which in St. Paul “are all cognate words, the relations of which to each other can never for an instant have been lost in the Apostle’s mind” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “God the Justifier of Man”). See righteousness.

  2. God the Justifier. In Romans 4:5 the reader fixes on the last words: “He sees that the Apostle is declaring God to be the justifier; he is certain that no assertion about faith or works can interfere with this primary one, or can be anything save an expansion or development of it” (same). See Father.

  3. Raising the spirit to what it lacks. “He Who, taking hold of the spirit of man, raises it to the full, distinct apprehension and possession of that which it is meant to apprehend and possess, and yet of which in itself it is destitute, justifies it” (same). The word shows “a personal Being owning His creature, claiming him for his proper state, answering his enemies and accusers for him” (same). In The Doctrine of Sacrifice, God “has freely justified them, or made them righteous, in that Son” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon X). See person.

  4. No fiction. “To ascribe a fiction to God,—to say that He holds men to be that which they are not really,—is to contradict the primary idea of justification, that it proceeds from a Righteous Being and that Righteousness is the end of it; it is to overthrow the foundations of all confidence in God and among men” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “God the Justifier of Man”). See truth.

  5. Faith the response. “The speech is responded to by the man’s believing it; the revelation of the Righteous Being is entertained by the man’s putting his trust in Him. He does not work; does not seek to commend himself to this Being; does not strive by great efforts to climb up to Him” (same). For Luther the Bible witnessed “that a man is justified by faith; no school phrases being used to express the idea, but every act of affiance in a Divine Person who had revealed Himself to man as the object of his trust and confidence being an exemplification of it” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). See faith.

  6. A Justifier, not a doctrine. Maurice’s objection to the Lutheran system is “that it does not bear witness for the all-importance of that fact which Luther asserted to be all-important; that it teaches us to believe in justification by faith instead of to believe in a Justifier; that it substitutes for Christ a certain notion or scheme of Christianity” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Of the Catholic Church and the Romish System”). The body founded to witness to it “has found that it must have a deeper ground than this to rest upon” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “On the Tendency of the Religious, Philosophical, and Political Movements”). See system and opinions · notions.

  7. Justified in Christ’s resurrection. God fulfilled the Jewish election “by raising Him from the dead, by justifying Him as His Son, by justifying in Him the nature which He had taken. Jews and Gentiles were all alike convicted of being sinners in themselves. Jews and Gentiles were alike declared to be righteous in Him” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “God the Justifier of Man”). Baptism renounces any standing-ground “save this, of trust in God, who has justified us by raising Christ from the dead” (same). See baptism.

  8. No tenet of ours. “This justification by faith is no tenet of ours, which is to distinguish us from other people, which is to raise us above any” (same). If it does not lead us to think nobly of the race “for which Christ died and rose again”, we have not entered into its meaning; “No dogma of divinity is it, but the staff of the pilgrim, the sword of the fighter, the strength of national as of individual life” (same). See nation.

Neighbouring words

  • righteousness, righteous (its root). Sense 1; see righteousness.
  • faith, trust, affiance (its response). Sense 5.
  • Justifier (the person behind the doctrine). Sense 6.
  • self-righteousness (its counterfeit). Baptism declares “all self-righteousness to be accursed and monstrous” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “God the Justifier of Man”); see self-will.
  • fiction, imputation (the false account). Sense 4.
  • resurrection (its ground). Sense 7.
  • election (its Calvinist companion). The second of the Protestant principles after justification (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”).

Scripture. Romans 4:5, to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness, the text of “God the Justifier of Man” (senses 2 to 5); Romans 4:25, raised again for our justification (sense 7); Romans 6:11, reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God (sense 7); Romans 5:1, being justified by faith, we have peace with God (sense 6).
