A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

ordination

episcopacy · bishop · ministry

Frequency in the corpus: ordination 21, ordain and ordained 67; episcopacy and episcopate 42; bishop and bishops 104; ministry 152. The central source is The Kingdom of Christ* II, “The Ministry”.*

In brief. The Church’s ministry is an order of servants, appointed by Christ and continued by ordination, whose authority is entirely from God and whose work is to present Christ to men and men to Christ. Maurice defends episcopacy, but not as a vicarial power held for an absent Lord: bishops are “the bonds of communication” between different lands, the great bond of a Christendom life, and the episcopate contains all the other orders. Ordination is a visible witness that ministers exist for the sake of men; secular and arrogant bishops are those who did not believe in their own ordination.

Shades of meaning

  1. Ministers, that is, servants. Every claimant to a Divine commission, “From the Bishop of Rome down to the founder of the last new sect in the United States of America” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Ministry”), takes the name of minister or servant as the description of his office. Maurice holds them to the name. See king, sense 5.

  2. Bishops as bonds between lands. “The overseers or Bishops of the Christian Church have felt themselves to be emphatically the bonds of communication between different parts of the earth” (same). By their office “they have held fellowship, and been obliged to hold fellowship, with those who lived in other districts, who spoke different languages” (same). Maurice speaks of “Episcopacy as the great bond of a Christendom life” (same). See Catholic and fellowship · communion.

  3. The episcopate contains the rest. The Liturgy “recognizes the Episcopate as the root of all the other orders, and supposes it to contain them all within itself” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”). It contains “the administration of the sacraments, the delivery of absolution, the preaching of the Gospel, the ministering to the sick and poor” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Ministry”). See absolution.

  4. Not vicars of an absent Lord. “Where are we to find one single hint that the Presbyter absolves or administers the Eucharist, that the Bishop exercises his own functions or that he ordains others, as the minister and delegate of one who is absent from his Church?” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”). The vicarial theory ends in the Popedom: “Ministers must not only be vicars of Christ, but there must be a vicar of Christ” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Ministry”). See Head and Mediator.

  5. Succession. “Admit ordination, and you admit the principle of succession” (same). Those who gave up episcopacy later felt that “a great link” between them and the older Church “had been cut off” (same), and tried to replace it with a succession of pure doctrine. See history.

  6. Ordination as witness against pride. If the clergy should become proud, there is no more terrible witness against them “than that ordination which has marked it out for the highest and lowest ministries; which has declared, that we exist for the sake of men, that all our authority is from God, that our only safety is in forgetting ourselves” (same). See witness and self-will.

  7. Worldly bishops disbelieve their orders. “The secularity of Bishops has been in all cases the effect of their not believing in the dignity and divinity of their own ordination; that the assumption of any particular Bishop has always been the effect of his denying the dignity and effect of his brethren’s ordination” (same). The feudal bishop “with his hounds and his falcons” (same) shows what happens when the office is thought to be the man’s own.

  8. Prophets and priests. The prophets of Israel were still “Old Testament ministers” (same), and men of genius today need a reminder “whence their power comes, under what conditions it must be exercised, who renders it effectual for the good of men” (same), even though they receive no visible designation. Ordination is the visible sign of what holds for every gift.

Neighbouring words

  • minister, servant (the name of the office). Sense 1.
  • priest, presbyter (the second order). Senses 3 and 4.
  • succession (its continuity). Sense 5.
  • vicar (the false conception). Sense 4.
  • sign, sacrament, ordinance (its kind). Sense 6; see sign.
  • Church, Catholic (what it binds). Sense 2.
  • absolution, Eucharist (its functions). Sense 3; see absolution.

Scripture. Matthew 28:19–20, Go ye therefore, and teach all nations … lo, I am with you alway (senses 2 and 4); Mark 10:43–44, whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister (sense 1); 1 Timothy 3:1, If a man desire the office of a bishop, he desireth a good work; Acts 1:20, his bishoprick let another take (sense 5).
