A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

education

educate · the education of mankind · pedagogue

Frequency in the corpus: education, educate and their forms in about 215 paragraphs.

In brief. Education, for Maurice, is first God’s work. The Bible is the history of a gradual education of men to understand the divine purpose through their own wants, weaknesses and sins. The law was a pedagogue that awakened the sense of evil but could not make men good. The whole education of man by his Maker is an education of hope, and God educates us still, as he educated Jacob to wrestle. Human education should follow the same method. It should lead men, the lowest classes as much as the highest, into the knowledge of laws and the meaning of their own order, not merely teach them facts and rules. And it cannot be handed over to a statesman who disclaims any power over men’s spirits.

Shades of meaning

  1. Scripture as an education. He calls the Scriptures a history because he finds in them “a gradual unveiling of the divine purpose and divine life to man, a gradual education of men to understand, through their own wants, and weaknesses, and sins, what this purpose and this life is, and how they may be the better for it” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture I). “Through all the patriarchal period of the Jewish history, through all the legal, through all the prophetical, I trace, and I wish you to trace, this revelation and this education” (same). See Bible · Scripture and revelation.

  2. The law as pedagogue. “Law, St. Paul discovered, serves this purpose in the education of a man or of a nation. It awakens the sense of evil; it shows them that they are at war with themselves and with God; it cannot make them right and good” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon IX). Christ redeemed men “from the vassalage which He had Himself ordained for their education. They were no longer under the Law as a curse; they were no longer under the Law as a pedagogue” (same). See law and dispensation.

  3. Out of idolatry. The Eucharist is “the consummation of all the sacraments by which God educated His people out of idolatry in the days of old” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Sacramental Character of the Bible”). See idolatry and sacrament.

  4. An education of hope. St. Paul regarded “the whole education of man by his Maker” as “an education of hope; every new hill that is surmounted opening some larger vision; every level plain and dusty road that is traversed leading still to some end” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Gospel of Hope”). See knowledge, sense 8.

  5. God educating us now. “He Who educated the old patriarch to wrestle, educates us of the latest generation in the same method” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “Christ Meeting with Doubters”). In prayer the disciple asks “in the assurance that He is educating you to desire what He desires, to work with Him for the overthrow of that which He hates” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XV). The Psalms were drawn out of a man “by One who was educating him to know what kinghood and manhood is” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer”). See prayer and Father.

  6. The parables as an education. The Parable of the Sower “was an education—a divine education—for poor men or rich men if their minds were only awakened to the most obvious facts of nature” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XX). Christ’s hearers were under “One who repulsed no questions, listened to all doubts, bore with all confusions” (What Is Revelation?, Sermon VI).

  7. Into the knowledge of laws. The spiritual teacher leads men “into the secret heart of things, in teaching them the laws of their own being, and their direct relation to the Creator. In this sphere of physical science he must act upon the same principle. He cannot merely teach facts and opinions, he must seek to guide his pupil into the knowledge of laws” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Modern Statesman”). The lowest classes “too, consist of men; of men who want to know, and who have a right to know, what that order is in which they are placed” (same). See law and constitution.

  8. The statesman as educator. The age contemplates “education as the only adequate means of preserving government and society.” But “The statesman demands education as a power for acting upon the spirits of men”, while having “distinctly and formally repudiated the notion that he can deal with the spirits of men” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Modern Statesman”). Against warring sects, he finds that “a sect-educator sets them at strife” (same). See nation and Church.

  9. Children and trust. “Trust is the great necessity of a child” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). “The secret of education lies in knowing how to turn the experience to profit. Let the child expect to find a friend in the lawgiver” (same). “The mischiefs of that education which to-day is rigid, to-morrow relaxed, are proverbial” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God”). See faith · trust and family.

  • pedagogue, schoolmaster (the law’s office). Sense 2.

Scripture.
