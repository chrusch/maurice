A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

prayer

pray · the Lord's Prayer · common prayer

Frequency in the corpus: prayer and pray in about 500 paragraphs. The main sources are The Lord’s Prayer, the Lincoln’s Inn sermons “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer” and “The Goading of an Adversary the Impulse to Prayer”, Lecture XVIII of The Epistles of St. John and “Forms of Worship” in The Kingdom of Christ II.

In brief. For Maurice, prayer begins in the sense of a Father: without it the impulse to pray would never have been awakened. Men pray because things are not as they ought to be, and suffering drives them to it whatever they think. True prayer is not an attempt to bend God’s will to ours; its great subject is the renovation of our own will, the request that His will be done. It is the confession of weakness grounded on the nature of God. Prayer is common, not private: even alone, a man prays in the midst of a family and says our, not my. Prayer presupposes a restoration already made in Christ, through whom it is offered.

Shades of meaning

  1. Grounded in the sense of a Father. When Christ bade His disciples say Our Father, “He explained the nature of prayer, the possibility of prayer, the necessity of prayer. If there had not been in men the sense of a Father, the impulse to pray would never have been awakened in them. The sky, the sun, the earth, would not have created that impulse” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XV). Because men had the yearnings of children, they could look for God in every object. See Father and seeking.

  2. The goad of evil. “We pray, because we feel that things are not as they ought to be; we pray, because we secretly confess or faintly hope that there may be some Power in the Universe which would set them right” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XVIII). The man who thought prayer was for children or women finds, in a sharp twinge of suffering, “that he has an adversary” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Goading of an Adversary the Impulse to Prayer”). See evil spirit and sin.

  3. The renovation of the will. The Psalmist’s cry, quicken thou me, contains “the very essence of prayer” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 4, “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer”): its necessity lies in a man’s discovery of his weakness, its hope in the nature of God. As long as a man thinks his bondage lies in something other than his will, he may ask for rain or relief from pain, but he will not understand that the prayer for God’s will to be done “is the greatest blessing which the creature can ask or the Creator can give; that by this the Creator becomes a Father, and the creature a child” (same). “Then it is that man begins to believe in God; for then he begins to believe that he himself is not God” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 4, “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer”). See will.

  4. Submission, not purchase. “Your prayers, if they are true prayers, are acts of submission and surrender to Him” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 5, “The Lord of Hosts and the Lord of Peace”). The prayer that God’s will be done “is specially needed to raise us above the plausible lies of our understandings” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon IV), and the point of view it gives “is obtained when our state is the lowliest” (same). See obedience and peace.

  5. Our, not my. “When thou art most alone thou must still, if thou wouldest pray, be in the midst of a family; thou must call upon a Father; thou must not dare to say my, but our” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Forms of Worship”). Whoever wants bread must desire it for all his brethren; whoever wants forgiveness finds “the gift is only received when it is circulated” (same). See family, worship and fellowship.

  6. Prayer supposes a restoration. To the objection that prayers for others assume the one praying is in a better state, Maurice replies: “Unquestionably; it is the very point which I have been pressing, that prayer does suppose a restoration” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Forms of Worship”). The Christian prays “through Him Who has on this earth manifested the perfect Will of God as a Will to the Redemption and Reconciliation of the Universe” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 4, “The Renovation of the Will the Great Subject of Prayer”). See Mediator and redemption.

  7. Inspired from above. The Spirit of God is “the awakener, the only awakener, of the spirit in man” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Forms of Worship”). The cry to be delivered from evil “was, in all cases, taught and inspired from above”; what the natural heart added was “just the false, confused element of it” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VIII). See Spirit.

Neighbouring words

  • Lord’s Prayer (pattern). The prayer that explains all prayer.
  • petition · asking (form). Asking according to His will.
  • Collect · common prayer (Church form). Prayers of a family, not of individuals.
  • will (subject). Prayer’s great subject is the renewal of the will.
  • charm · propitiation (corruption). Prayer as an attempt to compel or bribe a power.

Scripture. After this manner therefore pray ye: Our Father which art in heaven (Matthew 6:9). My soul cleaveth unto the dust: quicken thou me according to thy word (Psalm 119:25). If we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us (1 John 5:14). Men ought always to pray, and not to faint (Luke 18:1).
