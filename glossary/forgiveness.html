A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

forgiveness

forgive · remission · the law of forgiveness

Frequency in the corpus: forgive and forgiveness in about 330 paragraphs. The main source is Sermon VI of The Lord’s Prayer, “Forgive us our debts”, preached in Lent 1848. Others are Lectures III, IV and VII of The Epistles of St. John and the Lincoln’s Inn sermons on the Eucharist.

In brief. Forgiveness for Maurice is remission in the plain sense: sending away, the discharge of the debt itself, not merely release from a prison or a penalty. It belongs to the whole Lord’s Prayer: it is what a child may ask of a father, it hallows God’s Name, and it is part of His royalty. It is asked for us, never for me alone, because God has placed all men under a law of remission in Christ their head; to seek a separate pardon is the very essence of sin. The debts we owe cannot be discharged by ourselves or by those we have wronged, only by God, and at the point where we see they are owed to Him, despair turns to hope. Forgiveness is not tolerance of evil or escape from punishment; He forgives us that He may cleanse us. The condition, as we forgive, is no limit on the gift but its enlargement: we should pray for the spirit of forgiveness, not a passing sense of it, for forgiveness is expansive and dies if it is kept to oneself. If a forgiving Being governs the world, forgiveness is the law of the universe and of every common transaction.

Shades of meaning

  1. Remission, sending away. The Greek word common to both Evangelists means send away or remit, and “Every one feels the appropriateness of such an expression to a creditor’s release” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). We need not think of a prison or a penalty: “Discharge from the debt itself is that which the verb suggests” (same). St. John’s first lesson about God is “that He remits or sends away sins; for that is the force of the word” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). See sin and absolution.

  2. Part of the whole prayer. He who taught us to say Our Father bids us ask for full remission; “He must mean that it is such a request as a child should make of a father, and a father could grant to his child” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). The same holds for His Name, His Kingdom and His Will: rain and fruitful seasons are not more a sign of what He is than remission, “that one is as much an utterance of His disposition and purpose as the other” (same). See Father and prayer.

  3. Us, not me. The publican “claimed for himself a place among the whole body of sinners” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). A child can rise up after wrongdoing only if he feels “that there was a law of remission or forgiveness under which all were placed” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). To hope for a separate pardon will not do: “There is selfishness, there is separation from thy brother, there is the very essence of sin in that thought” (same). “You are not looked upon as a sinful race; you are looked upon as a race of which Christ the Son of God is the head” (same). See Head and mankind.

  4. To whom are the debts? If they are to ourselves, “we cannot release them. The more we try, the more hopelessly the coil is twisted round us” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). If to our fellows, even their forgiveness from the grave “would not set us free from that which has seemed to become a part of our own being” (same). They are debts to God: “Debts are Trespasses: we have not only forfeited an obligation, but committed a sin” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). “But at this point of despair hope begins. It is sin; sin against God” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). See conscience and repentance.

  5. Not impunity but cleansing. “A faithful and righteous Being is therefore a forgiving Being. We are unforgiving because we are unfaithful and unrighteous. But His forgiveness is not tolerance of an evil; such tolerance is cruelty. He forgives us that He may cleanse us” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture III). An advocate who won us escape from punishment would give no one what he longed for, for “it is not a blessing but a misery to escape the punishment of sin” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture IV); what a man wants “is to be set free from his sin” (same). See punishment and righteousness.

  6. As we forgive. Many lose the sense of forgiveness, and Christ’s parable explains it: the pardoned servant who took his fellow by the throat. Pay me that thou owest is said to servant, child, dependent, friend, wife and brother: “is not that the first natural thought of our hearts?” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). “We must thoroughly believe and understand that what seems to be a limiting condition of the request, is really an enlargement of its scope and power” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). If the grace of Christ is a forgiving Mind acting on ours, “an unforgiving habit is precisely the thing which opposes itself to that operation” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered as Interpreting All Other Acts of Devotion”). See grace and mercy.

  7. The spirit of forgiveness. We asked too little: we “should have prayed not for a momentary sense of Forgiveness, but for the spirit of Forgiveness” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI), for that mercy “which is no tolerance of wrong but the tormentor of it” (same). “Forgiveness is not forgiveness when it is turned to our ease and comfort. It is in its nature expansive, diffusive; it cannot be cooped up in the heart of any creature; it must go forth into the open air, or it dies” (same). See love and Spirit.

  8. The law of the universe. “If we believe that the world is governed by a forgiving Being, His forgiveness must be recognized as the Law of the Universe; the Law of our being” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). The alternative is to carry the principle of Individual Right to the highest ground, so that God becomes one whose main desire is “to assert His rights over us” (same), from which atheism is the natural refuge. See law and self-will.

  9. Fellowship and the feast. “There is all forgiveness to the man who accepts the deliverance from self as from a chain which he has forged; who enters into the state of fellowship with God and Man, which is the state which Christ has won for him” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered in its Relation to Confession and Absolution”). The Communion is “the feast of Forgiveness; the feast when we come confessing our sins against each other and against God” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, VI, “The Sick of the Palsy”). See Eucharist and fellowship.

Neighbouring words

  • remission and sending away (the root sense). The discharge of the debt itself.
  • debts and trespasses (what is forgiven). Obligations broken, and so sins against God.
  • pardon (the narrower word). Suspect when it means a separate pardon or escape from penalty.
  • cleansing (what forgiveness is for). Forgiveness is part of the cleansing.
  • absolution (the declaration). The Church’s word that the sin is sent away.
  • mercy (its character). The tormentor of wrong, not its tolerance.
  • unforgiving (the obstacle). The habit that shuts out the forgiving Mind.

Scripture. And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors (Matthew 6:12). For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you (Matthew 6:14). If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness (1 John 1:9). I write unto you, little children, because your sins are forgiven you for his name’s sake (1 John 2:12). But there is forgiveness with thee, that thou mayest be feared (Psalm 130:4). O thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all that debt (Matthew 18:32).
