A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

individual

individuality · individualism · each man

Frequency in the corpus: individual and individuality in about 450 paragraphs. The main sources are The Kingdom of Christ (“Philosophical Movements”, “Political Movements” and “Forms of Worship”), “On Regeneration” in Theological Essays, Sermon XVII of The Doctrine of Sacrifice and the Lincoln’s Inn sermons on the Eucharist.

In brief. Maurice almost never sets the individual against society without setting society against the individual in the next breath. He honours the Protestant sense of personal distinctness in each man, and he says the tendency to individuality has a true ground, but he holds that, left to itself, it starves worship and fellowship and leaves men without truths that belong to them as members of a race. The individual is not to be saved out of his race, nor absorbed into a pantheistic whole: in Christ, the Head of the race, the individual man becomes more distinct and more wonderful precisely because he is one of a society. Christ is the reformer of both the individual and society, so neither is to be reformed first. Confession and judgment remain individual; each man owns acts that he can lay on no one else.

Shades of meaning

  1. More distinct because one of a society. To those who feel “that a crowd of events and incidents and activities is pressing out the life of Individuals” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XVII) and would assert that each soul is sacred, he offers Christ, who bore the sins of each man and is the Head of all: “you can find that the individual man is only the more distinct, and the more wonderful, because he is but one of a Society” (same). See member and Head.

  2. Truths that belong to us as men, not as individuals. Protestantism “provides nothing to satisfy the demand for some truths or principles which shall belong to us, not as individuals, but as members of a race” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Philosophical Movements”). “Protestants say that every truth is to be realised by each man for himself, and that when a certain number of individuals have been made conscious of the same truth, they are to meet together and have fellowship in the profession of it” (same); they have not taught that there are truths “which do not depend for their reality upon our consciousness of them, but are the grounds on which that consciousness must rest” (same). See Protestant, mankind and ground.

  3. Personal distinctness, the spring of Protestantism. Maurice does not dismiss it. The Reformation’s assertion of national sovereigns was “closely intertwined with that feeling of personal distinctness in each man which is the main spring of Protestantism” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Political Movements”). But the two soon quarrelled, as monarchs found that “the belief in each individual’s right to act and think for himself trenched very inconveniently upon their authority” (same). See nation.

  4. Individual rights against communion. In France, where “society had been everything and human beings almost nothing,” came “the most vehement declaration of men’s individual rights” and “the death-struggle between those impulses which lead each person to maintain such rights, and those which lead him to seek communion with his fellows” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Political Movements”). The voice from Switzerland, Rousseau’s, proclaimed “that each man has in himself, apart from all social institutions and social civilisation, rights and power” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Political Movements”), and it went to the hearts of those who owned nothing but their limbs. See society and freedom.

  5. Individuality of thought and feeling. “There has been a constant tendency for several centuries towards greater individuality of thought and feeling. There is a true ground for this tendency, though it may have led to the most false results. But it is in itself, when unsustained by another tendency, unfavourable to the worship of God, as well as to fellowship among men” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Forms of Worship”). This is why he prefers the old common prayers to modern effusions. See worship and prayer.

  6. Neither individual first nor society first. Whoever holds to the Church’s ground “will displease those who say that you must reform the individual before you reform Society, for he declares that Christ is the Reformer of both, and that the individual who claims any relation to Him must own himself the member of a society” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). He will equally displease those who would reform society only by changing its circumstances, “not the assertion of a spiritual root and ground of it” (same). See society and regeneration.

  7. Not saving the individual soul out of the race. A man may come to the Lord’s table “because he thinks that Christ’s death, of which it testifies, will deliver, or may deliver his individual soul from the fate to which his race is doomed. He may come to it, because he desires to lose his individual soul in Christ, to be a partaker of the death which He died for the world” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered in its Relation to Confession and Absolution”). Those who seek themselves there and not Christ will not agree even with their own sect. See Eucharist and redemption.

  8. Losing oneself without absorption. “It is about losing ourselves that we are to think; the finding we leave to Christ” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered as a Participation in the Unselfish Life of Christ”). We must remember that what we lose we shall find, “lest we should fall into pantheistic dreams about an absorption of all personal life and distinctness into the great Whole or into the Divine Nature” (same). The Brahmin “may believe that he is absorbed into his God—that he becomes identical with him” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon V); the priest who shared the people’s sins knew he was no God. See person and self-will.

  9. Valuing the individual above the body. At Corinth the parties, the pride in wisdom, the abuse of the Communion and the denial of the resurrection all came from “the inclination to value the individual above the body, tongues and prophecies above charity” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The First Epistle to the Corinthians”). With it came the Greek contempt of the body: “Whatever was common and human was therefore despicable; whatever was peculiar and distinguishing was therefore glorious” (same). See body and Church.

  10. The individual and his race in death and confession. Through Christ’s death, death “is now, for the first time, common to the individual man with his race. He shall not die alone” (Theological Essays, “On the Resurrection of the Son of God from Death, the Grave, and Hell”). Yet confession stays personal: Lent “is a time for individual confession. Each one recollects acts which he has done, and which he can impute to no one except himself. But these are acts against the peace and order of society” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Body and the Members”). See death, confession and judgment.

Neighbouring words

  • member (the individual rightly understood). One of a body, more distinct for being so.
  • society and race (his correlatives). Christ is the reformer of both, and the Head of both.
  • personal distinctness (the true ground of individuality). The main spring of Protestantism.
  • individual rights (its political form). Set against the impulse to communion.
  • absorption and pantheism (the opposite error). Losing personal life in the great Whole.
  • self-will (its corruption). Seeking oneself and not Christ.
  • each man (his usual phrase). Christ bore the sins of each man and is the Head of every man.

Scripture. For as the body is one, and hath many members, and all the members of that one body, being many, are one body: so also is Christ (1 Corinthians 12:12). Now ye are the body of Christ, and members in particular (1 Corinthians 12:27). Whosoever will save his life shall lose it (Mark 8:35). The head of every man is Christ (1 Corinthians 11:3).
