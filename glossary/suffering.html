A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

suffering

sorrow · the Man of Sorrows · sufferer

Frequency in the corpus: suffering, sorrow and their forms in about 600 paragraphs. The main sources are Sermon XXV of Sermons Preached in Country Churches, “The Man of Sorrows”, the Lincoln’s Inn sermons “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope” (preached on the Day of Humiliation for the Indian Mutiny, 1857) and “The Glory and Deliverance of the Animal Nature”, and “On the Son of God” in Theological Essays.

In brief. Maurice does not offer a theory that explains suffering away. He holds that a scheme of optimism fails when calamity comes, and that the question why the innocent suffer will press on believer and sceptic alike. His answer is a person: Christ the Man of Sorrows, whose life was a life of joy because He pleased not Himself, and whose sorrow was that men’s reproaches against His Father fell on Him. Since they fell on the beloved Son, troubles are no proof of God’s hatred. Suffering is not to be read as a sign that the sufferer was a greater sinner. It is common, not private: the sufferings of the present time are those of the whole creation, travail-pangs of a nobler birth, and the Christian claims a share in them rather than exemption. Sorrow seems incommunicable, yet in the one body what affects one affects all. Pain can be a Father’s message, and much of our misery comes from trying to please ourselves.

Shades of meaning

  1. The Man of Sorrows whose life was joy. “Our Lord Christ is called The Man of Sorrows, and yet His life, even here on earth, was a life of joy. How could that be?” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”). He was never pleasing Himself: “He delighted to do His Father’s will, and to finish His work” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”). “My friends, the secret of most of our misery is that we are trying to please ourselves” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”). The poor sick man thinking of his wife’s kindness has more joy than the rich man contriving pleasures. See peace, self-will and the poor.

  2. Reproaches against the Father fell on Him. Men asked how there could be so much sickness, pain and oppression “if there is really a good God over all?” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”). “Now all these reproaches fell on Christ. They were anguish to Him” (same). Worse, as the express image of the Father, His goodness brought out the cruelty of men’s hearts, and “He who bore all this must have been indeed a Man of Sorrows” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”). See Incarnation and sin.

  3. Troubles no proof of God’s hatred. The Comforter “shows how the troubles which men have are not proofs of God’s hatred, seeing that they all fell upon His beloved Son, but may be means of drawing us to trust in Him, and give ourselves to Him” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXV, “The Man of Sorrows”). We are bound to think of Christ “as the man who bore all the sorrows that men have borne and do bear” (same), and His Spirit is with us “that we may enter both into His joy and His sorrow” (same). See Spirit and faith.

  4. The great sympathiser. In the sad hours of life, “the recollection of that Man you read of in your childhood, the Man of Sorrows, the great sympathiser with human woes and sufferings, rises up before you” (Theological Essays, “On the Son of God”). A memory of a man in Judea could not fill the blank of grief; the comfort is real because in Him and for Him we were created. Christ “enters into the wants of men, the sorrows of men, the death of men. He is the Universal Brother” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VI). See brotherhood and Head.

  5. Optimism fails. On the day of humiliation for the Indian Mutiny he faces the question, why this person and this “left to bear actually more than we can bear to speak or to think of?” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”). “No! a scheme of optimism must fail utterly at such times as these” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”). Optimism holds only when changed “into the belief of a Father Who is Light and in Whom is no darkness at all” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”). See Father and light.

  6. Not sinners above all others. Of the Galileans slain by Pilate and the eighteen crushed by the tower of Siloam, the onlookers “supposed that the Galileans who had suffered these things were sinners above all the Galileans” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture XVII). Christ refused that reading and turned it into a warning to the whole nation. The man who survives a calamity should ask instead, “What have I done that I should be better off than any of those who have fallen?” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Fatherly Character of God the Only Ground of Repentance, Consolation, Hope”). See judgment and nation.

  7. The sufferings of the whole creation. St. Paul’s present sufferings “are not those of the Apostle Paul, but of the creature Man on earth,—of the whole creation, of which man is the head. He is a partaker in those sufferings; he claims a right to partake in them. To be excluded from them would be to be excluded from human sympathy, from fellowship with the Son of Man, the great Sufferer” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Glory and Deliverance of the Animal Nature”). “They are travail-pangs; they are prophetic of a second and nobler birth” (same). See nature and hope.

  8. Sorrow incommunicable, yet shared. It often seems “that sorrow is incommunicable, that each person understands so little of his neighbour’s” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VII), yet in the body under one Head “what affects one, of necessity affects all” (same). The Church’s fasts are meant to let members of Christ’s body “sympathize in the sorrows of mankind” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Forms of Worship”), not to buy a blessing for themselves. See member and individual.

  9. Filial sorrow. The agony in the garden is not a vague horror: “The intensity of the sorrow is surely in this, that it is filial sorrow, the distinct will of the Son coming forth as if it were something separate and alone, yet striving in the agony of prayer to submit itself” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The Lord’s Supper”). See will and prayer.

  10. Pain as a Father’s message. “A sharp twinge of pain is not merely a twinge of pain” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXXI, “The New Covenant”); it is the Father’s way of writing His law in the heart that has forgotten it. Luther’s suffering in his cell taught him “that he himself was the cause of all the anguish which he was suffering” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Faith of Protestants”), and that only deliverance from the burden of himself would bring peace. See punishment and covenant.

Neighbouring words

  • joy (its companion in Christ). The Man of Sorrows lived a life of joy because He pleased not Himself.
  • sympathy (how Christ meets it). The great sympathiser with human woes.
  • optimism (the scheme that fails). True only as faith in a Father who is light.
  • travail (its meaning for creation). Birth-pangs of a nobler birth.
  • calamity (its public form). Not proof that the sufferers were worse sinners.
  • self-pleasing (the secret of misery). Most of our misery comes from it.
  • Comforter (its answer). The Spirit teaches that troubles are not God’s hatred.

Scripture. He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief (Isaiah 53:3). For even Christ pleased not himself; but, as it is written, The reproaches of them that reproached thee fell on me (Romans 15:3). Suppose ye that these Galilaeans were sinners above all the Galilaeans, because they suffered such things? (Luke 13:2). The whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now (Romans 8:22). Whether one member suffer, all the members suffer with it (1 Corinthians 12:26).
