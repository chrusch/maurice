A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

regeneration

regenerate · new birth · Regenerator

Frequency in the corpus: regeneration and its forms in about 110 paragraphs. The main source is Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”, which sets the doctrine against George Combe’s Constitution of Man and Bishop Butler’s sermons on human nature.

In brief. Maurice examines three common meanings of regeneration. The first, a special nature given to some persons at baptism or conversion in place of ordinary human nature, he rejects as incompatible with any belief that the human constitution is good. The second, the repair of a decayed building to its first plan, fits ordinary language but does not appear accomplished. The third, a life received from a Father here and now, is nearer, but it makes the spiritual society an exception among human societies. His own account is that Christ is the Regenerator of humanity. Baptism declares what is already man’s true constitution in Him, and the only condemnation is to shut one’s eyes to that light. So regeneration is social as well as individual, and it reaches the body and the earth as well as the spirit. The Church’s failures, Roman and Protestant, come from unbelief in it, and its message to the working classes should be that Christ restores humanity to its proper condition.

Shades of meaning

  1. A substituted nature. Regeneration may mean “the substitution, in certain persons, at some given moment (say in the ordinance of Baptism, or at a crisis called conversion), of a nature specially bestowed upon them, for the one which belongs to them as ordinary human beings” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). That doctrine cannot live beside belief in a good human constitution; to hold both is to make the heart “a mere shop or warehouse of opinions, in which no living processes are going on” (same). See constitution · order and opinions · notions.

  2. Restoration of a building. It may mean “the renovation or restitution of that which has fallen into decay, the repair of an edifice according to the ground-plan and design of the original architect” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). This agrees with common usage and with Butler’s idea of a constitution, but “are there any signs that it has been accomplished? Is the order, in this sense, restored?” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). So it is pushed into the future, as an object of hope. See redemption and hope.

  3. A life from a Father. Others hold that something is already received, and that the words birth and generation “must indicate a life communicated from a Father. A life of this kind they affirm they have received; it is renewed every hour” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). Maurice keeps this, but notes that on such a view a spiritual society seems “rather an anomaly and an exception among human societies” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). See Father and life.

  4. Christ the Regenerator of humanity. “If we may take Christ’s own exposition, if we may assume Him to be the Regenerator of humanity, a light seems to fall on all these different aspects of the theological doctrine” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). He is set forth “not merely as the author of forgiveness, but as the very ground and source of man’s eternal life” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). “But his perception does not make the fact which he perceives” (same). See Son of Man and fact.

  5. Baptism declares it. Christ has come “to claim men as spiritual beings, capable of this spiritual life, inheritors of this spiritual kingdom. Baptism declares this to be their proper and divine constitution in Christ” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). The baptized should know “that you belong to a body which He has regenerated, that you are yourselves regenerated. This, you ought to know, is your glory” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Circumcision and the Uncircumcision Merged in Christ”). See baptism and member · union.

  6. The only condemnation. The Spirit is given that men “may be as much born into the light of Heaven, into the light of God’s countenance, as the child is born out of the womb into the light of the sun” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). “And this is the condemnation, and this will be the only condemnation, that they do not come to it, that they shut the eyes of their spirit to it” (same). See light and judgment.

  7. Self-love and social. Butler’s maxim that we are social as well as individual becomes the hardest practical paradox: “I am certain that I have no self that I can love,—nay, that self must be an object of intense torment and hatred to me, unless I am the member of a body” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). “Bring in the belief of the one Head and Brother of each man the one Centre of Society, and that great moral contradiction is felt to be a great moral necessity” (same). See Head and society.

  8. Social regeneration. The clergy argued about baptismal regeneration while working men asked whether there is a God. Maurice says the fault was not too much earnestness, but that “we were needing such a Regeneration as they want and feel that they want,—a social as well as an individual Regeneration” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”), with Christ “as the Restorer of Humanity to its true and proper condition” (same). The gracious or regenerate state is not strange or irregular: “The gracious state is the human state” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XXIII). See grace and brotherhood.

  9. The body and the earth. Churchmen despised health and cleanliness “because we have forgotten that Christ took a human body, and spent the greater part of His time on earth in healing the sicknesses of it” (Theological Essays, “On Regeneration”). See body · resurrection and nature.

Neighbouring words

  • new birth and birth from above (the Johannine image). The birth of a spirit, from the Spirit, into a kingdom.
  • Regenerator (the person). Christ, of humanity and not of a select few.
  • restoration and renovation (the second view). True as hope, but not shown as accomplished.
  • constitution (what regeneration confirms). Man’s proper and divine constitution in Christ.
  • conversion (the crisis). Not the moment a new nature is swapped in.
  • baptismal regeneration (the controversy). Defended by Maurice, but not as a technicality.
  • new creation (St. Paul’s word). A body which He has regenerated.

Scripture. Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God (John 3:3). Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God (John 3:5). And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light (John 3:19). He saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost (Titus 3:5). Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature (2 Corinthians 5:17).
