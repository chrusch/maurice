A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

Atonement

atone · reconciliation

Frequency in the corpus: atonement and atone 62 uses, chiefly in Theological Essays, “Essay VII. On the Atonement”, and The Doctrine of Sacrifice.

In brief. At-one-ment: the union of God and man, and of man with man, made by God in his Son. Maurice reads the word in its old sense of reconciliation and insists that it originates with God; it is not something done to change his mind. He keeps every orthodox term, sacrifice, propitiation, satisfaction, but refuses explanations in which Christ appeases the Father, suffers an artificial substitution or pays a penalty instead of taking away sin. The Atonement is the fullest instance of sacrifice, which has its own entry.

Shades of meaning

  1. At-one-ment of God and man. The word means the reconciliation itself: in Christ “men are reconciled and atoned to God” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XIII). The verb is active and God is its subject: “These are the sure testimonies that God has atoned Man to Himself” (same). After Christ’s sacrifice, “The atonement of God and man, of earth and heaven, has been effected” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Great Sacrifice and the Feast of Deliverance”).

  2. Originating with God. “The Scriptures told us of an Atonement, originating with God; made with men in His Son, who entirely trusted and entirely obeyed His Father” (Theological Essays, “On the Trinity in Unity”). We learn “how the Atonement between us and Him must have been His work and not ours” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Three Things Which Abide”). See Father.

  3. The Atonement as sacrifice. The essay’s climax asks whether the Son’s perfect self-surrender is not “in the highest sense, Atonement” (Theological Essays, “On the Atonement”). Then the Cross is “the meeting-point between man and man, between man and God” (same). See sacrifice.

  4. What it is not. Maurice sets out the popular view that Christ “reconciled the Father, and made it possible for Him to forgive” (same), and tests it against the Creed. Christ cannot be thought “as changing that Will which He took flesh and died to fulfil” (same). “If we speak of Christ as taking upon Himself the sins of men by some artificial substitution, we deny that He is their actual Representative” (same). He rescued men from an enemy “not that He rescued them out of the hand of God by paying a penalty to Him” (same).

  5. Satisfaction by holiness, not punishment. Maurice keeps the word satisfaction and gives it its sense: Christ “satisfied the Father by presenting the image of His own holiness and love” (same). “How, then, can we tolerate for an instant that notion of God which would represent Him as satisfied by the punishment of sin, not by the purity and graciousness of the Son?” (same).

  6. Taking away sin itself. The Lamb takes away sin, not merely its penalty; to cut the bond between sin and death for a man’s benefit would not have magnified the law (same). See law and righteousness.

  7. Atonement of man with man. “That Atonement not only restores the union between men and God, it restores the union between men, whom time and space had put asunder” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The World and the Church”). It is universal: “The world is atoned or reconciled in Him” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture IV). See world and member · union.

Neighbouring words

  • sacrifice (its form). Sense 3; see sacrifice.
  • reconciliation, peace-offering (its synonym). Sense 1; The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XIII, calls Christ “the Peace-offering”.
  • propitiation, satisfaction (its orthodox terms, reinterpreted). Senses 4 and 5.
  • substitution, penalty (the theories rejected). Sense 4.
  • Mediator, Representative, Head (the one who makes it). Sense 4; see Mediator and Head.
  • Eucharist (its memorial). The Lord’s Supper declares “a perfected atonement for the whole world” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Christian Priest”). See Eucharist.
  • justification (its relation in the Reformers). Maurice notes the difficulty “about the relation between the idea of justification and the idea of atonement” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”).

Scripture. Romans 5:11, by whom we have now received the atonement (the KJV’s one New Testament use, where the RV reads reconciliation); 2 Corinthians 5:19, God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself, not imputing their trespasses unto them; John 1:29, the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world (sense 6); Hebrews 2:14, that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death (sense 4); Leviticus 16, the day of atonement.
