A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

baptism

baptize · baptized

Frequency in the corpus: baptism 387; baptized or baptised 164.

In brief. The sacrament by which God declares to a person what that person already is in Christ: a child of God, a member of Christ, a citizen of the kingdom. For Maurice it makes nothing new in the person considered in himself; it witnesses to a relation already established and gives the warrant on which a man may claim it. It is also the first sign that the kingdom exists at all.

Shades of meaning

  1. A declaration of a state. The defining sense: baptism affirms, it does not manufacture. “I have contended that Baptism affirms a man to be in a certain state, and affirms the presence of a Spirit with him, who is able and willing to uphold him in that state, and to bring his life into accordance with it” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV, “The Romish System”). Hence his objection to the Roman doctrine, which in his view “turns a sacrament into an event” (same chapter).

  2. Adoption into sonship, which cannot be lost. Because the state declared is a relation, sin does not undo it: “We do not, as I have again and again urged, cease to be children because we are disobedient children. If therefore Baptism were looked upon as the adoption into the state of children, and if its virtue were believed to consist in this, the notion we are considering would be impossible” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV).

  3. Union with a Person, and so with mankind. “Baptism asserts for each man that he is taken into union with a Divine Person, and by virtue of that union is emancipated from his evil Nature” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV). The same paragraph moves at once to the society that rests on that union, and from there to the Name of the Trinity beneath both.

  4. Renunciation of self. Since the state is union with Christ, baptism excludes any holiness a man might own in himself: “To be something in himself is man’s ambition, man’s sin. Baptism is emphatically the renunciation of that pretence” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV).

  5. The warrant for faith. Baptism is the particular word spoken to the individual, on which he is entitled to believe: “The words of the Bible generally, the assurance of baptism to you particularly, give you the right to believe” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. II, “Pure Protestantism”). In the parish sermons it becomes the preacher’s appeal: “If any who were baptized could say, ‘God has given us the sign that we are His children,’ all who were baptized could say” the same (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XV, “The Spirit and the Flesh”).

  6. The first sign of the kingdom. Baptism is evidence, in history, that a spiritual and universal society exists: “I look upon Baptism as the first sign of the existence of a Catholic Church or Kingdom of Christ in the world” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV). The argument of that chapter starts from the plain historical fact of the rite.

  7. Baptism into the Name. Maurice almost always gives the formula in full, because the Name is the content of the sacrament. The whole Apocalypse, he says, “is occupied throughout with that Divine Name into which the Christian Churches are baptized” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture I). See Name.

  8. Infant baptism, and the key to all ordinances. The baptism of infants shows that the gift comes before any feeling or act of the recipient. In the dedication to The Kingdom of Christ Maurice says the promise of his hope came “in the words in which our infants are baptized”; and so, he goes on, “I was led to consider the meaning of this ordinance of Baptism as a key to the nature of ordinances generally” (The Kingdom of Christ I, Dedication).

  9. Christ’s baptism with the Spirit and with fire. Distinct from the rite but bound up with it. John’s baptism was “unto repentance”; Christ’s “gives the spirit and power whence repentance and every right act must flow; it brings the subject of it under the discipline of that purifying fire whereby the old and evil nature is to be consumed” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV).

Neighbouring words

  • Catechism (statement of its meaning). The formulary that states what baptism declares: “The Catechism, which we teach to all children who have been baptized, tells them that they are members of Christ, children of God, inheritors of the kingdom of Heaven” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter). The three titles are the three things baptism declares. See Catechism.
  • sign, sacrament, ordinance (genus). Baptism is the first of the signs and the key to the ordinances (senses 6 and 8). See sign.
  • Eucharist (companion sign). The sign of continuance beside the sign of entry: in the Apocalypse lectures the grace “was assured to them in their Baptism”, and the bread and wine were “a witness of its perpetual, unchangeable vitality” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XXIII). See Eucharist.
  • adoption, children of God, sonship (what it declares). What baptism declares (sense 2).
  • justification (rests on it). Maurice reads Luther’s justification by faith as resting on the baptismal warrant: “Believe on the warrant of your Baptism, You are grafted into Christ; claim your position” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV). See justification.
  • covenant (what it seals). “It is the covenant of our Baptism” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXXI, “The New Covenant”).
  • Name, Trinity (content). The content into which one is baptized (senses 3 and 7).
  • world, self (what it frees from). What baptism emancipates from: the evil nature in each man, and “the World—the society which is bound together in the acknowledgment of, and subjection to, the evil selfish tendencies of each man’s nature” (The Kingdom of Christ I, ch. IV).

Scripture. Matthew 28:19, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost (KJV; RV into the name), is the text behind sense 7, and Maurice’s habitual baptized into the Name anticipates the Revised Version’s rendering of the Greek, published after his death; Matthew 3:11, he shall baptize you with the Holy Ghost, and with fire, behind sense 9.
