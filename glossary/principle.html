A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

principle

principles · positive principle · living principle

Frequency in the corpus: principle and principles in about 900 paragraphs, the most concentrated use in The Kingdom of Christ.

In brief. A principle, in Maurice, is a living truth that men discover rather than invent: something positive, belonging to mankind, that one acts on and is governed by. Its opposite is the notion, a thought in the head, and its counterfeit is the system, which a party builds by tearing one principle apart from the rest. Each sect’s positive principle is true and is a note in the divine harmony. Its system is a disturbance of that harmony. Christ delivered the principles of his kingdom, which are the foundation of civil order and not its rival. A question of principle cannot be settled as a question of degree or of safety.

Shades of meaning

  1. Principle and system. Calvin sought the Church’s foundation in individual election: “Thus the Calvinistic system is formed—a system essentially distinct from the Calvinistic principle, but necessarily involved in the constitution of the Calvinian Church” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). The sects “have made a system out of some principle which they have torn apart from the rest; that they have destroyed that principle by its separation” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Modern English Systems”). See system and sect.

  2. A note in the harmony. “We may find, that there is a divine harmony, of which the living principle in each of these systems forms one note, of which the systems themselves are a disturbance and a violation” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Modern English Systems”). “I believe the great principles, which each of these systems has developed, have been made known to us for the wisest purposes” (same). See Catholic.

  3. Positive, not negative. The three principles of the Reformation “seem to me, in the strictest sense, positive principles. They are not the less so because they were brought forth in opposition to certain popular notions and current practices” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). The Reformers saw “that certain great moral principles, involved in the very idea of a relation between God and His creatures” were outraged by the Roman system (same). See Protestant.

  4. Principle and notion. “It is Mr. Mansel’s misfortune that he cannot distinguish between a Principle and a Notion.” Butler’s method is to face practical difficulties, believing “that we may find a principle which will enable us to act rightly, be the notional perplexity ever so great” (What Is Revelation?, Letter VIII). Through the old conflict of the schools “we may discover true principles which do not belong to the schools but to mankind” (What Is Revelation?, Letter IV). See opinions · notions.

  5. A uniting, living principle. The Gospel either “shows how a uniting principle may be an effectual, living principle—how those tendencies which lead to separation may be overcome,—or it fails to do what it professes” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Law of Christ the Law of Humanity”). The opposite, the readiness to take transgression “as the principle of human life and fellowship, was precisely the lie of the evil nature” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The Sign from Heaven”). See unity and society.

  6. The principles of the kingdom. Jesus “goes into a mount to deliver the principles of His kingdom” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “The Scriptural View of This Constitution”). He was not proclaiming “a transcendental morality—but principles which were the foundation of civil order and obedience” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture X). See kingdom and law.

  7. The principle of our constitution. “When we are best we are only what is involved in the very principle of our constitution; we are only acting as if we were what we say in our catechism that we are” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “The Sin of Man and the Justification of Man”). The prohibition of oaths “associates a rule of daily life with the most deep principles of our being” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Objections of the Quaker”). See constitution.

  8. Principle, not degree. In the opening dialogue the Quaker says of leaving his body for safety’s sake: “this is a question of principle not of degree, and when I set principle at nought, I believe these words, great and terrible as they sound, lose all their meaning to me” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Introductory Dialogue with a Quaker”). Maurice does not exchange “phrases about moral principles and philosophical truths for phrases about religious opinions and feelings” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Theology and Morality”). See morality.

Neighbouring words

  • system (its counterfeit). Senses 1 and 2; see system.
  • notion, opinion (its opposite in the mind). Sense 4; see opinions · notions.
  • ground, foundation (what a principle stands on). See ground · root.
  • law (principle as governing). Sense 6; see law.
  • fact (what principles mean). See fact.
  • truth (its other name). Sense 4; see truth.

Scripture. Matthew 5–7, the Sermon on the Mount (sense 6); Hebrews 6:1, leaving the principles of the doctrine of Christ (sense 3); 1 Corinthians 12:12–26, many members in one body (sense 2); Micah 6:8, do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God (sense 8).
