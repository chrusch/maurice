A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

mystery

mysteries · mysterious · the Christian mysteries

Frequency in the corpus: mystery and its forms in about 370 paragraphs. The main sources are Sermon VI of What is Revelation? on the parables, the section on the Eucharist in The Kingdom of Christ, which answers the claim that the Christian mysteries continue those of Eleusis, and the lecture on Ephesians in The Unity of the New Testament.

In brief. For Maurice a mystery is something given to be known. Christ told ignorant fishermen that it was given to them to know the mysteries of the Kingdom of Heaven: the eternal things cannot be conceived, but they can be known. So he resists both the claim of a few to be initiated into higher mysteries than common Christians, and the view, which he finds in Mansel, that the deepest truths of the faith must be received as dogmas that the reason cannot touch. With the old pagan mysteries he grants one real likeness: in every faith and every society the deepest principle must be mysterious. The Christian mystery, in St. Paul’s sense, is a secret hidden from the ages and now made manifest: the relation of Christ to humanity, which husbands, wives and children enter into by living it out. The clergy are stewards of these mysteries, answerable for how they have dispensed them.

Shades of meaning

  1. Given to know. Christ says to the fishermen “it is given to know the mysteries of the Kingdom of Heaven” (What is Revelation?, Sermon VI, “Christ’s Parables”). People say that what is eternal is mysterious and inconceivable. “Our Lord admits that it is. He speaks of mysteries. But He speaks of knowing mysteries” (same). The disciples “have a capacity for knowing these mysteries, these eternal things which cannot be conceived” (same). See knowledge and kingdom.

  2. Not a dogma galling to the reason. That the Father is in the Son is called “the deepest mystery of the Christian faith” (What is Revelation?, Sermon IV, “The Miracles”). Must it then “be received as a dogma transmitted from other ages, very galling to the reason, but against which it cannot remonstrate, because it has no capacity for dealing with the Infinite?” (same). Maurice answers with Christ’s words, that men may come to know it. See revelation and Absolute · Infinite.

  3. Against the initiated. St. John wrote against teachers “who boasted that they had a knowledge of divine mysteries to which vulgar Christians could make no pretensions. They were the initiated; the rest were novices” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XII). He met their vaunting by joining the knowledge of God to keeping the commandments, against men who “exulted in their knowledge of mysteries, who identified this with the knowledge of God” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VI). See sect · party and obedience.

  4. The deepest principle of every faith. Against those who make the Christian mysteries a late form of the pagan, Maurice grants that the likeness lies in “the deep acknowledgment which there has been in all ages of a something which the senses cannot grapple with” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”). “That this feeling belongs to the most permanent part of our being; that it cannot satisfy itself; that of every faith, and every society, the deepest principle must be mysterious: this is admitted by both parties” (same). The old mysteries were confused because the physical world was unexplored; the philosopher at last “begins to speak of the world as the one great mystery” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”). See religion and sacrament.

  5. Hidden from the ages, now manifest. St. Paul wanted all to see “what is the economy of the mystery that has been hidden from the ages in God, who hath created all things” (The Unity of the New Testament, “Epistle to the Ephesians”), God “gradually revealing the mystery of His Will” (The Unity of the New Testament, “Epistle to the Ephesians”). It is “that very mystery of Christ’s relationship to humanity which he had been speaking of before, which the former ages had not known, which was now made manifest” (The Unity of the New Testament, “Epistle to the Ephesians”). See manifestation and dispensation.

  6. Entered by living it. Each husband and wife “might enter more and more into this mystery by carrying it out in their love and reverence to each other” (The Unity of the New Testament, “Epistle to the Ephesians”), and children and parents, in obedience and in the discipline of the Lord, “were also entering into this mystery, carrying out the meaning of this revelation” (The Unity of the New Testament, “Epistle to the Ephesians”). See family and member · union.

  7. The mystery in a face. The doctors in the Temple could copy the letters and reduce what lay beneath them into Cabbala, “But which of them could penetrate the awe and mystery of that countenance, clear and bright as it was?” (What is Revelation?, Sermon II, “Christ among the Doctors”). Christ went on “unveiling some of the deepest mysteries of the Kingdom of Heaven, by asking questions” (What is Revelation?, Sermon II, “Christ among the Doctors”). See Son of Man and education.

  8. Mysteries of love. At Pentecost “The Holiest of the Holiest had been opened, and a mystery of Love had been revealed, as that which no loveless creature had been able to look into” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered as Interpreting All Other Acts of Devotion”). “The mysteries of Christmas Day and of Passion Week are not severed from each other” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Passion Week; its Dangers and Blessings”). See Trinity and love.

  9. Stewards of the mysteries. St. Paul bids the Corinthians count him and Apollos “ministers of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Christian Priest”). If we have been unfaithful stewards, God will give the mysteries to other tribes of the earth, “who cry for that revelation of the Unseen and Eternal which we have made light of, and at last have denied that we possess” (What is Revelation?, Letter IX). See ordination · episcopacy and judgment.

Neighbouring words

  • revelation and unveiling (what a mystery is for). Hidden from the ages, now made manifest.
  • the inconceivable (what Maurice grants). The eternal cannot be conceived, yet can be known.
  • the incomprehensible (Mansel’s word). Maurice will not let it mean that the reason may not look.
  • initiation (the false claim). The boast of a few to higher knowledge than the common flock.
  • the pagan mysteries (the old form). Eleusis, true in its sense of the unseen, confused in its notions.
  • steward (the minister’s office). One who dispenses the mysteries and must answer for them.

Scripture. It is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 13:11). Having made known unto us the mystery of his will (Ephesians 1:9). The mystery, which from the beginning of the world hath been hid in God, who created all things by Jesus Christ (Ephesians 3:9). This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32). Let a man so account of us, as of the ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God (1 Corinthians 4:1).
