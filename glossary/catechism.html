A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

Catechism

Articles · Prayer Book · Liturgy

Frequency in the corpus: Catechism 18; Articles (of the Church) about 40; Prayer Book 25; liturgy and liturgical 27. The key source is The Kingdom of Christ* II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”.*

In brief. The formularies of the Church of England, which Maurice reads as the faith of a Church and not as a system. The Catechism declares to every child what Baptism made him, and Maurice says it taught him most of what he knew. The Liturgy is the sign of a universal society, the worship of a body and a family, built on the assumption that worshippers have not lost their baptismal privileges. The Articles are the Church’s dogmatic language, protective, with the Catholic articles first and an appeal always open to Scripture. He would keep all of them against those who would sacrifice them to progress.

Shades of meaning

  1. A rediscovered power. Maurice describes how familiar words “presented themselves to me, with a power which I had never dreamed was in them. I mean the words of our Articles, of our Catechism, of our Prayers” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Dedicatory Letter). What he teaches “is to be found in every creed of the Catholic Church; in the Prayers and Articles of the Church to which I belong; most emphatically in the Bible, from which they derive their authority” (same).

  2. The Catechism and Baptism. “I will tell you plainly that it is the consideration of Baptism and of its meaning as it is set forth in our Child’s Catechism, which has given me the little apprehension I have of this truth” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). Its words, that the baptized are members of Christ, children of God and inheritors of the kingdom, are a lesson for every day: “we have to learn our child’s Catechism afresh every day” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). See baptism.

  3. The Liturgy a sign of universal society. “Now a Liturgy is of this kind. I have shewn how remarkably it is the sign of a universal society” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”). The Prayer Book “enables us to feel that we have a fellowship with each other, and with men of all ages, in our deepest wants” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Sacramental Character of the Bible”). It is national but not only national: “It came from Greek and Latin sources” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Faith of Englishmen”). See society and fellowship · communion.

  4. Built on baptism; interpreted by the Eucharist. “The whole of our Liturgy is constructed upon the principle that the men who engage in it have not lost their baptismal privileges” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”). And “the communion service in our liturgy interprets the rest of our worship. Throughout, it is the worship of a body, of a family” (same). See Eucharist and sacrament, sense 5.

  5. Scripture taken into worship. “The Scripture is adopted into our worship, the service explains the lessons, the lessons explain the service” (same). To his village hearers: “I should not have spoken to you of anything in the Prayer Book, if I did not think its lessons were in the Bible, and if they did not send you back to the Bible” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, IX, “The Spirit the Help to Prayer”). See Bible.

  6. The Articles: Catholic first, then the sixteenth century. In the Thirty-nine Articles “the first Article being upon the Trinity, the second is upon the Incarnation, and that the first eight Articles relate to truths directly connected with the being of God” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”); only “When this Catholic foundation has been laid” (same) do they turn to the questions of free will, justification and election. The Church expressed itself “in the Liturgy; and when it required a dogmatical language, in the Articles” (same). See Trinity.

  7. An appeal to Scripture. Against the claim that the Articles contain the whole meaning of Scripture: “But the Articles themselves assert that an appeal does lie from them to the Scripture” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Sacramental Character of the Bible”). “The Articles assume that the Scriptures are an intelligible document, more intelligible than any which human wit can invent” (same).

  8. The faith of a Church, not a system. “So far as it is represented in our liturgy and our articles, it is the faith of a Church, and has nothing to do with any system at all” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Modern English Systems”). Hence his resolve: “I would hold fast those prayers, those creeds, that catechism, which the men of progress tell us that we must sacrifice to the interests of a general Humanity” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “The Faith of Englishmen”). See system and Creed.

Neighbouring words

  • Creed (the formulary of formularies). The Liturgy makes the Creeds “parts of our worship; acts of allegiance” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The English Church and the Systems Which Divide It”); see Creed.
  • collect (its prayers). See unity, sense 4, on the Trinity-Sunday collect.
  • sacrament (the ground of the Prayer Book). Sense 4; see sacrament.
  • system, confession (what they are not). Sense 8.
  • Bible (their standard). Senses 5 and 7.
  • worship (what the Liturgy is). Senses 3 and 4.
  • English Church (their owner). Sense 8; see nation.

Scripture. Matthew 28:19, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost, behind sense 2; Acts 2:42, they continued stedfastly in the apostles’ doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers (sense 3); 2 Timothy 1:13, Hold fast the form of sound words (sense 8).
