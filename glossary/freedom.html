A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

freedom

liberty · free · the law of liberty

Frequency in the corpus: freedom and liberty in about 320 paragraphs. The sermon “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal” in Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3 is the main source, with The Doctrine of Sacrifice and Sermons Preached in Country Churches.

In brief. Maurice denies that the Bible discourages the desire of freedom as secular. The Ten Commandments rest on a deliverance out of the house of bondage, and the Bible instructs men in the nature of freedom and stirs an appetite for it. The chains men feel are finally in themselves, in the will. The Son makes us free because He brings us the adoption of sons, and freedom and obedience meet when we yield our wills to the Father. God’s law is a law of liberty: He judges a man not for his bad desire but for not asking to be set free from it. Freedom is personal, national and universal, and a Universal Church is not a danger to it but the way to a diviner liberty.

Shades of meaning

  1. Not discouraged by the Bible. “It is a common notion, sanctioned by divines, eagerly adopted by infidels, that the desire of Freedom which men are wont to cherish is discouraged in the Bible as secular and profane” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). Maurice answers that the Bible “does not aspire to provide checks against the excesses of freedom, but to instruct us in the nature of freedom, to stimulate an appetite for it, to make us ashamed of our contentment without it” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). See Bible and religion.

  2. The house of bondage. The words at the threshold of the Ten Commandments are the ground of them all: God brought Israel out of the house of bondage. “How comes it that the sternest part of this record, that which most inculcates obedience, most warns of the consequences of disobedience, is the history of a national deliverance?” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). The freedom of the Bible differs from what men intend only in that “they desired they knew not what; when it was given them they shrank from it” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). See Deliverer and law.

  3. Bands within. Men blame their bondage on laws, governors, customs and fashions, but the Bible teaches the tacit truth “that after all it is in ourselves that we must be slaves or free; that no outward yoke could crush us if we had not put it on” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). Whatever the oppressors are called, “if we are oppressed in our own selves, that is enough” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). See will and self-will.

  4. The Son makes us free. “It is the Son Who makes us free, because He brings us the adoption of sons” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 3, “The Bible the Book of Freedom, Personal, National, Universal”). At the cross men “find their bondage turned into freedom. The incubus that sat upon their hearts and took away their breathing is gone” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XV). See Father, redemption and evil spirit.

  5. Freedom and obedience. Freedom is not the opposite of obedience. “Obedience and Freedom meet and embrace each other when we believe that He asks us to yield up our wills as sacrifices to His” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 6, “The Honour and Degradation of an Official”). The more St. Paul was possessed by the Spirit, the freer his conscience: “he could eat or abstain, be a Jew or Gentile, as served his Master’s cause best; his conscience every day acquired more freedom” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 2, “The Apostle the Chief of Sinners”). See obedience and conscience.

  6. The law of liberty. To be judged by the law of liberty means that God will not ask a man why he had a bad desire but “Why didst thou not ask Me to set thee free from the desire, to give thee power over it?” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXXIII, “The Law of Liberty”). “I did not give thee My law to frighten thee away from Me, but to draw thee to Me” (same). See law and judgment.

  7. A diviner liberty. Maurice asks whether the longing for a Universal Church, “which we sometimes regard as pregnant with rebellion, sometimes as perilous to freedom”, may not be “the very instruments which God is using for leading us to a higher obedience, to a diviner liberty” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel 2, “The Faith of Catholics”). The true priest “will be ever leading men on to that spiritual freedom, without which they cannot serve the God whose name is the Redeemer” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XVIII). See Church and Catholic.

Neighbouring words

  • bondage · slavery (opposite). The house of bondage, inward and outward.
  • obedience (partner). Freedom’s condition, not its enemy.
  • deliverance · redemption (the act). God’s setting free, from Egypt to the cross.
  • adoption · sonship (ground). Freedom is the freedom of sons.
  • licence (counterfeit). Wild acts done in freedom’s name, which make timid minds distrust it.

Scripture. I am the Lord thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage (Exodus 20:2). If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed (John 8:36). So speak ye, and so do, as they that shall be judged by the law of liberty (James 2:12). The glorious liberty of the children of God (Romans 8:21).
