A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

duty

duties · the sense of duty · obligation

Frequency in the corpus: duty and duties in about 300 paragraphs. The main sources are “The Honour and Degradation of an Official” in the Lincoln’s Inn sermons, Lectures I and VII of The Epistles of St. John, Lecture X of The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven and “Christian Services and Christian Rewards”.

In brief. For Maurice duty is the sense that something is due from us, and it is the threshold of manhood and of freedom: until it awakes, a man is not even on the way to becoming a man. Duties grow out of relations, to parent, child, neighbour, nation and God, and Christ grounds every relation on the common relation to Himself. He refuses the split between religious duties and common duties; the lawyer’s, physician’s and tradesman’s work is as much self-sacrifice as gifts to the poor. Duty to God is the surrender of the will to His will, which binds men to one another. Maurice knows the English temptation of the dry official who does his duty like a machine, and his remedy is not impulse but the faith that the One who sets the task works without caprice, so that obedience and freedom meet. Faith in our union with Christ is itself the greatest of all duties.

Shades of meaning

  1. Duty and the birth of the person. When a boy first feels himself an individual, self-will appears, and left to itself makes a savage. The dread of punishment may deter from crimes, but “Till we understand that there is something due from us, till the sense of duty is awakened, we have no freedom, we are not even in the way to become men” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture I). The search for what duty is led him to conscience, “not a power of its own, but is a witness to us of some One speaking to us, commanding us” (same). See conscience and freedom.

  2. Duties grounded in relations. St. Peter “constitutes all relationships of husbands and wives, parents and children, masters and servants, upon the basis of the common relation to Christ” (The Unity of the New Testament, “St. Peter. First Epistle”), and the duty to civil governors and all mutual services rest on the same ground. St. Paul “recognises the relation of younger to elder as a very deep relation, involving duties, calling for subjection” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “Christian Reverence and Subjection”). See family, society and constitution.

  3. No religious duties apart from common duties. He bids us return to the old maxim, “for we are strangely forgetting it amongst notions about religious interests and civil interests, religious duties and common duties” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “Cry for God’s Judgments”); religious duties among the Jews made war on Christ. “The care of our children, the performance of our duties as lawyers, physicians, tradesmen, just as much implies a relation to other human beings, habitual obligations to them, daily and hourly self-sacrifice, as gifts to the poor or labours for them” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Christian Services and Christian Rewards”). See religion and morality.

  4. Duty to God as surrender of the will. Christ “does not destroy any one precept of duty to God or to our neighbour. He fulfils it by baptising with His Spirit; by making duty to God the surrender of man’s will to His Will which is working in us; that Will binding men to each other as members of the same body” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture X). See will and obedience.

  5. The dry official. The English temptation is the man who does his work “with all the success of a machine; with nothing of the feeling or life of a man” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Honour and Degradation of an Official”), and some say that nowhere but in England “does the dry sense of duty so absorb into itself the passion for glory, personal affection, the delight in what is good for its own sake” (same). Yet he asks whether there is not good at the bottom of it, and “how the individual freedom, of which Englishmen are tenacious, may sustain and not destroy the sense of duty which is also so strong in them?” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Honour and Degradation of an Official”). See nation and individual.

  6. St. Paul an official. “St. Paul, then, is an official. He has a duty which is to be performed. It is a prescribed duty. There is something to be done, and he cannot fix what that something shall be” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Honour and Degradation of an Official”). He knew he might do it unwillingly, and that taskwork brings no joy. Willingness could not be manufactured; it came from knowing whose will had laid the necessity on him. See Church and ordination.

  7. Obedience and freedom meet. “The sense of duty draws all its strength and nourishment from the acknowledgment of a God Who never acts from caprice or self-will, Who governs all things in heaven and earth according to the order which He has imposed upon them” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Honour and Degradation of an Official”). “Obedience and Freedom meet and embrace each other when we believe that He asks us to yield up our wills as sacrifices to His Who has first made the great sacrifice for us” (same); then “no office can be looked upon as anything less than a calling” (same). See sacrifice and freedom.

  8. The duty of love made bearable. Without the sacraments as pledges of God’s relation to us all, “the duty of loving one another, which we cannot be discharged from, would seem to us an oppressive duty that must be performed, but which we had no strength to perform” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). In every nation “there has been a sense of the duty of forgiveness, and there has been also the sense of its being a divine quality” (What Is Revelation?, Letter X). See sacrament, love and forgiveness.

  9. Faith as the greatest duty. Since sin is the refusal to be what we are, it follows “that faith in this union is a duty, the greatest of all duties, and that it can never cease to be a duty. A man has no right to believe a lie” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Signs of a Spiritual Society”). St. Paul held “that it was their duty to call upon God as their Father, and to acknowledge themselves as His children” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “How Self-Examination is Possible”). See faith and child.

  10. Duties unfulfilled. In the Lord’s Prayer the word debts names “the burden of duties unfulfilled, words unspoken, or spoken violently and untruly; of holy relationships neglected; of days wasted for ever” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon VI). The publican “does not try to prove that he has done his duty to God or his duty to his neighbour” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XIV, “The Publican”). See sin and confession.

Neighbouring words

  • obligation and debt (its forms). Something due from us; unfulfilled duties are the debts of the Lord’s Prayer.
  • relation (its ground). Every duty grows out of a relation, all resting on the relation to Christ.
  • calling and office (its dignity). No office is less than a calling when the will is yielded.
  • officialism (its corruption). Duty done like a machine.
  • conscience (its witness). The voice of One commanding.
  • religious duties (the false division). Set up against common duties, they crucified Christ.
  • freedom (its partner). Duty awakens freedom, and the two meet in obedience.

Scripture. We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do (Luke 17:10). For if I do this thing willingly, I have a reward: but if against my will, a dispensation of the gospel is committed unto me (1 Corinthians 9:17). Woe is unto me, if I preach not the gospel! (1 Corinthians 9:16). Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man (Ecclesiastes 12:13). Render therefore to all their dues (Romans 13:7).
