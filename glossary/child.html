A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

child

children of God · little children · childhood · innocence

Frequency in the corpus: child and children in about 1,200 paragraphs, most of them in the phrase children of God. The main sources are the Lincoln’s Inn sermon for Innocents’ Day, “Christian Innocence”, Lecture VII of The Epistles of St. John, “The Children, the Youths, the Old Men”, and the sections on Baptism and the Eucharist in The Kingdom of Christ.

In brief. For Maurice every man is a child of God by right, because the Only Begotten has made Himself our Brother by taking our flesh. Baptism does not create the relation but claims it, and Luther and the Reformers built justification on it: you are children of God, so do not live as if you belonged to the devil. He resists the sentimental worship of infant innocence as a lost Eden to be mourned. To become as a little child is to be regenerate, and the innocence that matters is not the unconscious infant’s but the forgetting of self in another, which grown men can attain. A child is meant to grow into a distinct person, and every discipline of family and school is God’s instrument for keeping His child from becoming its own slave. Trust is the child’s great need, and the child’s first lesson about God is that He sends sins away.

Shades of meaning

  1. Children by right of Christ’s flesh. Men “may claim to be sons of God by adoption, because the Only Begotten has made Himself their Brother. If Baptism admits one and another into this condition, if it gives one and another the filial name and the filial privilege, it does so because Christ had first vindicated that name and those privileges for all human beings, by taking their flesh” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”). Baptism “can confer no separate or independent grace upon any creature” (same). See baptism, Incarnation and brotherhood.

  2. Baptism as the ground of the child’s claim. The Reformers held “that we are adopted into Christ by Baptism and are therefore children of God, and may draw nigh to Him in all duties and services” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “The Eucharist”). The Galatians’ false teachers told them they were “not really taken to be children of God” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XV, “The Spirit and the Flesh”) and must earn it under law. To walk in the Spirit is to “Live as if this were true which I have told you, and as if you were God’s children” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XV, “The Spirit and the Flesh”). See justification and flesh · spirit.

  3. Against the worship of infant innocence. The popular view regards the child “as not only the father but as the model of the man” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”), takes the moment after baptism as the summit of perfection, and then laments: “What can we do but lament that this innocence has departed, and try to regain what faint image of it or substitute for it we can?” (same). Baptism itself refutes this, since it was first proclaimed to a corrupt generation, and no holy man in Scripture wastes “one sigh that Adam had not fallen” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”). See sin and history.

  4. Becoming as a little child. Christ’s words “scatter all notions that we are to sigh after the state of Infancy as one which has been left far behind us; for He tells grown men that they may and must attain all which is blessed in that state, notwithstanding their experience of the world and of sin” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”). Whatever it means to become a little child “is included in the word Regenerate” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”). See regeneration and repentance.

  5. Innocence as forgetting self in another. “The purity or innocence of any human creature is not and cannot be his own; that we are only innocent so far as we claim nothing of our own, so far as we look out of ourselves, so far as we forget ourselves in another” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”). The reverence for a child’s unconsciousness is a silent homage to this. Yet there must be a self to give: “For how can there be any giving up of self if there is not a self to give up?” (same). See self-will and individual.

  6. The child growing into a person. A child who never learned to say this is mine and this I am to do would be called idiotic, and anxious parents ask whether to awaken the sense of self or stifle it, until “They are obliged to confess that they are not creators, that another discipline than theirs has been at work on the being which has been committed to them” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”). We are to welcome every energy, “even of vehement self-assertion, whatever possibilities of evil may be latent in it” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”), and to see all the discipline of family and school “as God’s instruments for preventing His child from becoming its own slave, instead of His free servant” (same). See education and freedom.

  7. Trust, the child’s great need. “Trust is the great necessity of a child. It wants to feel that it may go to those who are over it with confidence. It wants to feel that it may go to them when it has done wrong” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). So St. John’s word to the little children is that their sins are forgiven: “the first lesson of all to be learnt concerning God, is that He remits or sends away sins; for that is the force of the word” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). We need a new reformation “to make our people understand the length and breadth of this child’s truth” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). See forgiveness and faith.

  8. Children, young men and fathers in one family. St. John’s three addresses show that Christian ethics do not “reduce us all to an indiscriminate mass, because they speak of all as sinful, and of all as redeemed in Christ” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII). “In a united family each finds out by degrees what he is fit for, and what each of his brothers and sisters is fit for” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture VII), and “The child is glad to be led by those stronger and more experienced; they gain as much from the simplicity of the child” (same). See family and Church.

  9. The Child among the doctors. Even proud deniers may be led “to come, as little children, not only to the Bible, but to that Child whom the Bible declares to be the firstborn of Men, the perfect manifestation of God” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel V, “Christ Among the Doctors”). The records of Christ’s boyhood “tell us that the boy has his prototype as well as the man” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Christian Innocence”). See Son of Man and Head.

  10. Children gathered home. The Nativity is a prophecy of “the true gathering in of the children to their Father’s house, as the time when the word home is to find its full signification, when Society shall be a fact, and not a name” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Christmas Gathering”). Parents’ care follows God’s: “we are to be provident for our children, because God is provident for His” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”). See Father and society.

Neighbouring words

  • son and adoption (the Pauline form). Sons by adoption because the Only Begotten is our Brother.
  • baptism (the claim made visible). It confers no separate grace; it claims what Christ has won.
  • innocence (the sentimental idol and the true thing). Not infant unconsciousness but self-forgetting.
  • regeneration (becoming a child). The meaning of the text about little children.
  • trust (the child’s need). That it may go to the parent when it has done wrong.
  • education and discipline (the child’s growth). God’s instruments against becoming one’s own slave.
  • family (the child’s setting). Each finds out what he is fit for.

Scripture. Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 18:3). I write unto you, little children, because your sins are forgiven you for his name’s sake (1 John 2:12). Ye are all the children of God by faith in Christ Jesus. For as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ (Galatians 3:26–27). The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God (Romans 8:16). And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature (Luke 2:52).
