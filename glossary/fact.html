A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

fact

facts · the great facts · fact and notion

Frequency in the corpus: fact and facts in about 1,200 paragraphs, with the polemical sense, set against notion, theory or opinion, in about 70.

In brief. A fact, for Maurice, is something that is, as against a notion about it: an act of God or a truth of human life that men find and confess. The Gospel is a proclamation of facts concerning man and God, and the Creed is a set of facts, not theories. Doctrines and principles are the meaning of facts, so the two cannot be pulled apart. There is something in man that takes hold of fact, and without it there could be no morality or truth. Theories that explain away the facts of experience, such as sin or love, must fail. The resurrection is a fact, and also a power.

Shades of meaning

  1. A fact to proclaim. Of George Fox: “he had discovered the very thing itself; he had a fact to proclaim, not a theory or a system. From the very first therefore he began to denounce dogmas and formulas as corrupting and misleading” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “On the Principles of the Quakers”). He “had actually discovered a law to which he himself was subject—to which every other man was subject” (same). See witness.

  2. Great facts concerning man and God. The Roman abuses “set at naught great facts concerning man and concerning God—facts which had been announced by an express revelation from heaven. Here was a standing-point” (The Kingdom of Christ I, “Pure Protestantism”). The Reformers feared that sacramental grace had made the Romanists “substitute something else for the great facts of which the Bible is the record” (same). See revelation and Creed.

  3. Doctrine as the meaning of facts. On the distinction between facts and doctrines: “Any one who considers doctrines as I have considered them in these Essays, finds it exceedingly hard to separate them from facts,—doctrines and principles he supposes to be the meaning of facts” (Theological Essays, “On Inspiration”). The question about St. Luke is whether he brings out an artificial theory “or the revelation of a Divine principle apart from which the facts in the life of Jesus of Nazareth would not mean any thing or account for any thing” (The Unity of the New Testament, “St. Luke”). See principle.

  4. What takes hold of fact. Kant “could not bring himself to deny that there is something in us which takes hold of fact, something which will not be circumscribed by notions and conceptions, which confesses that which is. He felt that if there is no such faculty in man as this, there is not and cannot be any morality for man, there is not and cannot be any truth for man” (What Is Revelation?, Letter V). See faith.

  5. Not a notion in its place. A man facing death must have a solution “which does not hide any part of the fact, which does not impose a notion upon him as a substitute for the fact” (Theological Essays, “The Resurrection of the Son of God from Death, the Grave, and Hell”). If man is only a body with a soul intruded into it, “then there can be no polity in Heaven, the notion is a fond dream, an outrage upon fact” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Earth and Heaven”). See opinions · notions.

  6. Facts that theories cannot dissolve. “We do feel the sins of our youth” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon X), and we do not get rid of these facts “by adopting a theory that they mean nothing” (same). Comte, awakened by human love, “must deny facts—facts which he has realized—if he pretends that his notion of Science is sufficient to explain them” (The Epistles of St. John, “On Positivism and its Teacher”). Some deny “the glory of humanity” and reject “the Apostle’s doctrine and these facts together” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel II, “God’s Judgment and Our Judgment”). See sin.

  7. Primary facts of the new dispensation. If the prodigal is a son from the first, “we must be prepared to ask ourselves manfully, whether these are not the primary facts and laws of the New Dispensation, whether the Gospel is not a claim for men as men to be sons of God” (The Unity of the New Testament, “St. Luke”). The translators of Romans 5 preserve “the great fact, that the justification is coextensive with the condemnation” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The Epistle to the Romans”). See dispensation and justification.

  8. A fact and a power. “We dwell upon the fact of Christ’s Resurrection; upon the evidences which establish it; upon the inferences which may be drawn from it. St. Paul also dwelt upon the fact. It was the very ground of his Gospel to mankind.” But “Fact, Evidences, Inferences, were all inseparably bound up with the idea which is expressed in the words of the text” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “The Resurrection a Power as Well as an Event”), the power of his resurrection. Of the revivals of history: “We can explain the facts in no other way. The power of His resurrection has been there” (same). See body · resurrection.

Neighbouring words

  • notion, theory, opinion (its opposites). Senses 1, 5 and 6; see opinions · notions.
  • doctrine, principle (its meaning). Sense 3; see principle.
  • experience (where facts are met). Sense 6.
  • event, power (fact as act of God). Sense 8.
  • reality, that which is (what fact confesses). Sense 4; see I AM.
  • revelation (the facts announced). Sense 2; see revelation.

Scripture. 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, he was buried, and … he rose again the third day (sense 8); Philippians 3:10, the power of his resurrection (sense 8); 1 John 1:1–3, that which we have seen with our eyes … declare we unto you (senses 1 and 4); Luke 15:11–32, the prodigal son (sense 7); Romans 5:18, the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life (sense 7).
