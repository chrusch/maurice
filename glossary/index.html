A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

The words that carry the weight of Maurice’s thought, and what he means by them. Many are ordinary words, kingdom, world, sacrifice, Head, that he uses in a sense of his own.

Each entry gives the meaning in brief, then its shades of meaning, each shown in Maurice’s own words, with every quotation linked to the paragraph it comes from in these editions. It ends with the words he defines it with or against, and the passages of Scripture behind it. The counts at the head of an entry say how often the word occurs in his published works.

The entries, in brief

AbsoluteInfinite · absolute Being
Maurice uses absolute in two ways. In theology it describes God: absolute Love, absolute Goodness, the absolute Will, the absolute Being from whom law and order come. In his controversy with Mansel and Hamilton it names what philosophy has always sought, the Absolute and Infinite, which they said men could not know. Maurice answers that the search becomes a contradiction only when we try to grasp the Absolute in a notion of our own; the Absolute himself has stirred men to it because he means to reveal himself, and has done so in the Son. Infinite most often qualifies love, goodness and charity.
absolutionabsolve · Absolver
The setting free of the conscience from the bonds of sin. Maurice treats absolution as the Church’s chief work for men and as the office every Christian minister has claimed in some form. But the true Absolver is Christ, the Son who makes men free by bringing them into sonship; the minister absolves only as his representative, in the congregation, joined with the Eucharist, never as a vicar with powers of his own. A daily absolution is what every man needs. The counterfeit is a priestly absolution that does not set free at all.
Atonementatone · reconciliation
At-one-ment: the union of God and man, and of man with man, made by God in his Son. Maurice reads the word in its old sense of reconciliation and insists that it originates with God; it is not something done to change his mind. He keeps every orthodox term, sacrifice, propitiation, satisfaction, but refuses explanations in which Christ appeases the Father, suffers an artificial substitution or pays a penalty instead of taking away sin. The Atonement is the fullest instance of sacrifice, which has its own entry.
baptismbaptize · baptized
The sacrament by which God declares to a person what that person already is in Christ: a child of God, a member of Christ, a citizen of the kingdom. For Maurice it makes nothing new in the person considered in himself; it witnesses to a relation already established and gives the warrant on which a man may claim it. It is also the first sign that the kingdom exists at all.
BibleScripture · the Scriptures
For Maurice the Bible is not a book of doctrines or a code of rules but the history of God’s revelation of himself to a family, a nation and mankind, in which the events are interpreted as his acts. It is the record of a kingdom, and so it belongs with the Church: each explains the other. Its authority rests on what it reveals, a living Person, not on any theory of its letters. Maurice therefore holds fast to its plain sense and resists both the system-builders who turn it into propositions and the critics who turn it into a mere collection of documents.
bodyresurrection · resurrection of the body · spiritual body
Maurice holds the resurrection of the body without modification, against the charge that he made it merely spiritual. But the body raised is not a gathering of decayed relics at some distant day. It is the living powers and principles of which our bodies consist, redeemed from the corruption that belongs to them because we have sinned. Christ’s body saw no corruption and so showed what the human body truly is. His Resurrection was not proof that men are immortal; it declared men sons of God, and redeemed the body and the earth as truly as the spirit, which makes every physical study a sacred one. The Church too is a spiritual body, the members gathered in their one Head.
brotherhoodbrother · the Universal Brother · fraternity
Brotherhood, in Maurice, is grounded on Fatherhood and on Christ the Universal Brother, the Head and bond of all men. Without confessing a Father there is no brotherhood, which is why he takes the French cry of Fraternity seriously and calls it a duty to find out how it may become real. Under the Gospel brotherhood takes the place of neighbourhood: before, it was sin to hate a neighbour; now it is sin to hate a man. Brotherhood is no feeling about the Christian body at large but help to the particular brother, and it binds rich and poor together. Its enemies are the self-will that destroys a world made for brotherhood, and those who will not belong to a brotherhood but assert their independence.
CatechismArticles · Prayer Book · Liturgy
The formularies of the Church of England, which Maurice reads as the faith of a Church and not as a system. The Catechism declares to every child what Baptism made him, and Maurice says it taught him most of what he knew. The Liturgy is the sign of a universal society, the worship of a body and a family, built on the assumption that worshippers have not lost their baptismal privileges. The Articles are the Church’s dogmatic language, protective, with the Catholic articles first and an appeal always open to Scripture. He would keep all of them against those who would sacrifice them to progress.
CatholicCatholic Church · catholicity
Universal, in the strict sense: belonging to mankind and not to a sect, a party or a period. The Catholic Church is the kingdom of Christ for all men, grounded in the union of God and man in Christ, existing in every age and confessed in the Creeds and sacraments. Maurice distinguishes it sharply from the “Romish system”, which he regards as its deadly opposite, and he holds that Protestant truth and Catholic order need each other.
childchildren of God · little children · childhood · innocence
For Maurice every man is a child of God by right, because the Only Begotten has made Himself our Brother by taking our flesh. Baptism does not create the relation but claims it, and Luther and the Reformers built justification on it: you are children of God, so do not live as if you belonged to the devil. He resists the sentimental worship of infant innocence as a lost Eden to be mourned. To become as a little child is to be regenerate, and the innocence that matters is not the unconscious infant’s but the forgetting of self in another, which grown men can attain. A child is meant to grow into a distinct person, and every discipline of family and school is God’s instrument for keeping His child from becoming its own slave. Trust is the child’s great need, and the child’s first lesson about God is that He sends sins away.
ChurchChurches · Catholic Church · Universal Church
The society of men constituted in Christ and baptized into the Name of the Father, the Son and the Spirit, which exists to witness to all men of the order they already belong to. For Maurice the Church is not a company of the saved set apart from mankind, nor one religious body among others, but human society as God made it, restored to its centre. It is universal by nature and yet distinct, since its whole meaning lies in bearing witness to what the world denies.
confessionconfess · confession of sin · confession of the Name
Maurice uses the word in two senses that he keeps close together. To confess sin is not to list faults against an imaginary standard but to own that one has departed from the actual law under which all men live, and above all to own a broken relationship: no one says we have sinned until he feels he has been distrusting a Father. Confession of sin is therefore a renunciation of the sin, made to God because the truth in us is His. It is grounded on God’s faithfulness, it is individual and also social and national, and it needs no confessor’s casuistry. To confess the Name, or that Jesus is come in the flesh, is to acknowledge a fact and a Lord: one Mediator between the visible and the invisible, a government to bow to. The two senses meet because confession of sin is always confession of the God from whom we have turned.
conscience
The faculty in man that answers to God. Maurice denies that it is “a power of its own”: it is a witness to Someone who speaks, commands and judges. The conscience tells each man that he is a person and responsible; it protests against his self-will; and it is what revelation addresses. It can be burdened, fettered or awakened, and Christ’s sacrifice is said to purify it. Maurice also appeals often to “the conscience of mankind” as a common court of judgment.
constitutionorder · divine order
The structure God has given to human life and to the universe, which men find and live within but do not invent. Maurice speaks of a “spiritual constitution” of mankind, made of family, nation and a universal society, and of a Divine order beneath all the disorders of history. Christ is its revealer and assertor; his miracles restore it rather than break it. Men can rebel against the constitution, and can build a counterfeit order out of their own lusts; but the true order is never changed, and every judgment reveals it. Maurice’s order is the positive term against which system is measured.
covenantnew covenant · Covenant of Promise
A covenant is God’s act of binding a people to himself and declaring himself to them. It comes first; law and sacrifice follow and witness to it. God’s covenant with Abraham was a blessing for all nations, not a set of commands, and the curse of the law came later. The new covenant, sealed in the blood of Christ, is God’s promise to write his laws in men’s hearts by his Spirit; it is the covenant of baptism, made with the whole race in the Son. Nations, including England, stand under God’s covenant and fall when they forsake it for gods they can see.
CreedCreeds · I believe
For Maurice the Creeds are acts of allegiance to a Name, said by a person, not summaries of doctrine. Their form is I believe; what they confess is a Father, a Son and a Spirit and what they have done. That is why they belong to worship and to every peasant and child, and why he sets them apart from the dogmatic Articles and confessions that systems draw up.
deathdying · eternal death
Bodily death is the great darkness, the Accuser’s chief argument that God means harm by his world, since it seems to cut off every purpose and every bond. Christ answered it by dying: he entered death in obedience to his Father, not to Death, and rose. So death is no longer lord of anything that lives, nor a barrier between us and him. Eternal death is something else. It is a present state of the spirit, separation from God, which every man who has known sin has known. It is not a future term of years, and Christ brings men out of it now.
Delivererdeliver · deliverance
The first name by which God made himself known to Israel, and so for Maurice the key to the character of God. The God of the Ten Commandments speaks as the Lord thy God which brought thee out of the house of bondage, the deliverer of slaves from a tyrant. Christ is the Deliverer from sin, death and the evil spirit, who sets free body and spirit alike. Men are always tempted to think of God as the oppressor from whom they need deliverance; Maurice’s constant answer is that they know him only by the name of Deliverer.
dispensationold dispensation · new dispensation
A dispensation is one of God’s ordered ages of dealing with men, and especially the Jewish age before Christ and the age that followed it. Maurice uses the word freely but distrusts it. The new dispensation does not swallow up the old: the same God, the same Christ and the same commandments run through both, and the old witnesses to distinction while the new declares union. Its end came with the fall of Jerusalem, and each later age may also have its end. The word goes wrong when “the Christian dispensation” stands in for the living Kingdom of Heaven, or when the Quaker reads “spiritual dispensation” as a reason to cast off signs and sacraments.
dutyduties · the sense of duty · obligation
For Maurice duty is the sense that something is due from us, and it is the threshold of manhood and of freedom: until it awakes, a man is not even on the way to becoming a man. Duties grow out of relations, to parent, child, neighbour, nation and God, and Christ grounds every relation on the common relation to Himself. He refuses the split between religious duties and common duties; the lawyer’s, physician’s and tradesman’s work is as much self-sacrifice as gifts to the poor. Duty to God is the surrender of the will to His will, which binds men to one another. Maurice knows the English temptation of the dry official who does his duty like a machine, and his remedy is not impulse but the faith that the One who sets the task works without caprice, so that obedience and freedom meet. Faith in our union with Christ is itself the greatest of all duties.
educationeducate · the education of mankind · pedagogue
Education, for Maurice, is first God’s work. The Bible is the history of a gradual education of men to understand the divine purpose through their own wants, weaknesses and sins. The law was a pedagogue that awakened the sense of evil but could not make men good. The whole education of man by his Maker is an education of hope, and God educates us still, as he educated Jacob to wrestle. Human education should follow the same method. It should lead men, the lowest classes as much as the highest, into the knowledge of laws and the meaning of their own order, not merely teach them facts and rules. And it cannot be handed over to a statesman who disclaims any power over men’s spirits.
eternaleternity · everlasting
For Maurice eternal does not mean endless, or without beginning and end. It names what is real and substantial, belonging to God and not to time: God’s own righteousness, truth and love. Eternal life is the knowledge of God, possessed now; eternal death is the loss of it, also known now. The refusal to equate eternal with endless in the Theological Essays (1853) cost Maurice his chair at King’s College, and the word is the centre of that controversy.
EucharistCommunion · the Lord's Supper
The sacrificial feast in which the Church is brought into the presence of God through the one finished sacrifice of Christ, and in which Christ is really present to the worshippers. It is the sign of continuance as Baptism is the sign of entry: the constant bond of a universal fellowship, given by Christ, and in Maurice’s view the fullest embodiment of his kingdom. Like Baptism, it asserts a reality that exists before the worshipper’s faith and does not depend on it.
evil spiritdevil · Accuser · spirit of selfishness
Maurice holds the doctrine of an Evil Spirit openly, against Unitarian scorn, because he finds it explains experience better than the alternatives: that evil comes from circumstances, or that human nature is itself depraved. The Devil is above all the Accuser or Slanderer, who whispers against God, against our brethren and against ourselves. He is the spirit of selfishness, tempting each man to make himself his own centre. He does not force anyone to sin. Belief in him keeps sin in the will and keeps God wholly good. Christ has answered his accusations by his death.
factfacts · the great facts · fact and notion
A fact, for Maurice, is something that is, as against a notion about it: an act of God or a truth of human life that men find and confess. The Gospel is a proclamation of facts concerning man and God, and the Creed is a set of facts, not theories. Doctrines and principles are the meaning of facts, so the two cannot be pulled apart. There is something in man that takes hold of fact, and without it there could be no morality or truth. Theories that explain away the facts of experience, such as sin or love, must fail. The resurrection is a fact, and also a power.
faithbelieve · trust
Faith is trust in a living Person, God the Father revealed in his Son, and not assent to propositions or confidence in one’s own state. It is receptive, not creative: it answers a truth that is there already and calls it forth. It is God’s gift, and it saves a man by emptying him of himself. Maurice keeps the Reformation’s justification by faith and insists that it means faith in a God who justifies, not faith as a new kind of merit.
familyuniversal family · the whole family
The first and most basic order of human life, and the sign of the order that holds mankind together. Maurice treats family relationships as divine and spiritual. Fatherhood, sonship and brotherhood on earth exist because God is Father and Christ is Son, and they prove that men are not isolated units. The Bible’s history moves from the family to the nation to “the universal Family” in Christ, the whole family in heaven and earth that is named from the Father.
Fatherfatherly · our Father
The name of God that Christ reveals, and the ground of every other name. For Maurice the Godhead is not self-willed power but a Father and a Son in a relation of love and obedience. Because God is Father in his only begotten Son, he is the Father of all men, and the one who knows this has a right, and a duty, to arise and go to him. The fatherhood of God is also the pattern of the family, and every human father bears his name.
fearthe fear of God · terror · dread
Maurice separates two fears that his age ran together. The fear of God, which Scripture calls the beginning of wisdom, is awe before One who is substantial, holy and merciful, and it grows the nearer a man comes to Him. The fear of punishment is a miserable substitute for it: a terror of a distant or false god, which ends in superstition and slavery. Religion that rests on terror, materialising the torments of the future to drive men to obedience, presents the Father as though He were the Evil Spirit. Yet Maurice does not explain terror away: there is a real dread of eternity and of oneself that no priest invented, and only a gospel of forgiveness answers it. Trust, not argument, puts terror to flight; perfect love casts out fear; and the access to the Father without dread is the root of every other fruit of the atonement.
fellowshipcommunion
Shared life with God and with other men; Maurice uses fellowship and communion as synonyms. It is the highest end of man, and it is possible because God first seeks it. Fellowship with God carries fellowship with men in it; its source is the communion of the Father and the Son, and its centre is Christ, who in his sacrifice claimed fellowship with every sinner. Life itself is fellowship, going out of oneself; death is the self wrapped in itself. The communion of saints reaches across death.
fleshspirit · the flesh and the spirit · carnal · spiritual man
St. Paul’s distinction between the flesh and the spirit is for Maurice one of the great facts of every man’s life: in every man there is a spirit which seeks righteousness and a flesh which stoops to evil. The two are not reason and appetite. Each has strong lusts, and the spirit is as fervent as the flesh. Flesh is not the body; it is the self that seeks the earth it came from, and the zeal of a party can be fleshly. The spiritual man is not the one who sets himself apart but the one who seeks what is common. God in Christ adopts the spirit, quickens it by His own Spirit, and promises to raise the body too out of its bondage to the flesh.
forgivenessforgive · remission · the law of forgiveness
Forgiveness for Maurice is remission in the plain sense: sending away, the discharge of the debt itself, not merely release from a prison or a penalty. It belongs to the whole Lord’s Prayer: it is what a child may ask of a father, it hallows God’s Name, and it is part of His royalty. It is asked for us, never for me alone, because God has placed all men under a law of remission in Christ their head; to seek a separate pardon is the very essence of sin. The debts we owe cannot be discharged by ourselves or by those we have wronged, only by God, and at the point where we see they are owed to Him, despair turns to hope. Forgiveness is not tolerance of evil or escape from punishment; He forgives us that He may cleanse us. The condition, as we forgive, is no limit on the gift but its enlargement: we should pray for the spirit of forgiveness, not a passing sense of it, for forgiveness is expansive and dies if it is kept to oneself. If a forgiving Being governs the world, forgiveness is the law of the universe and of every common transaction.
freedomliberty · free · the law of liberty
Maurice denies that the Bible discourages the desire of freedom as secular. The Ten Commandments rest on a deliverance out of the house of bondage, and the Bible instructs men in the nature of freedom and stirs an appetite for it. The chains men feel are finally in themselves, in the will. The Son makes us free because He brings us the adoption of sons, and freedom and obedience meet when we yield our wills to the Father. God’s law is a law of liberty: He judges a man not for his bad desire but for not asking to be set free from it. Freedom is personal, national and universal, and a Universal Church is not a danger to it but the way to a diviner liberty.
generationgenerations · from generation to generation · this generation
Maurice uses the word in three ways. First, God is the Same from generation to generation, a living God and not one who acted once in the past. Second, the forms He has given, Creeds, sacraments and common prayers, bind each generation to every other, so that the language of one generation can be the language of all. Third, each generation has its own questions, its own sins and its own judgment: no man is of use to his generation unless he takes up the questions it is asking, and the judgment the Baptist and the Apostles preached fell on the generation they spoke to.
gloryglorify · glorying · self-glory
God’s glory, for Maurice, is the glory of a Being whose name is Love, and so it is the opposite of self-glory. It does not crush men or compete with their good; God raises His creatures above themselves by giving them Himself to behold. That glory was shown in human weakness, in a cradle and a carpenter, and it could only come forth fully in suffering and the Cross. So the glory a man may rightly seek is never his own: it is a common glory, like the sun’s, which all may see and none may own. The opposite is glorifying oneself, on one’s nation, one’s sect, one’s gifts or even one’s faith, which is the root of party and persecution and which no religious system protects us from. St. Paul’s glorying in the Cross is the reversal: the Cross nails down every boast of man against man and binds him to all men. Glory to come is the full manifestation of Christ, the fruition of God for which every nation sighs.
gracegracious · the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ · means of grace
Maurice refuses to let grace become a technical word. The grace we admire in a face, a movement or a character always points to the living person within, and it is human, not merely individual. The other common sense, a favour from a superior, cannot be separated from the first, for an unrighteous king cannot be gracious. A gracious government is one that produces in its subjects the quality that was first in the ruler, and that is what Scripture means by a kingdom of grace: a Ruler in whom all grace dwells, acting on men’s wills to make them gracious and free. So the gracious state is the human state, not an exception for a few. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ is a forgiving Mind acting continually on ours, which an unforgiving habit shuts out. It surrounded the heathen before any preacher came, it can be resisted, and yet the divine Will has resources to subdue the most untractable will.
groundroot · foundation
What everything rests on and grows from. Maurice’s governing claim is that the foundation is laid, not made: no teacher or body of men put it there, and all they can do is point to it. That foundation is a Person, Christ, who is the root of humanity and the ground of society, of the universe and of all theology. Behind him stands the Father as an Absolute Root of truth and good. To build on anything else, opinions, self, or the will of a party, is to build on rottenness. The three words are nearly interchangeable in Maurice; root adds the sense of life drawn from below.
HeadHead of every man · Head of the race
Christ’s relation to all men, from 1 Corinthians 11:3, the head of every man is Christ. For Maurice it is a fact about mankind, not a privilege of believers: Christ is Head of the Church because he is Head of every man. The word carries his doctrine of humanity, his doctrine of the Church, and his case against any human head set up in Christ’s place.
hellthe kingdom of Hell · death and hell · descended into hell
For Maurice, hell is not first a place of future torment but a state: the want of righteousness, truth and love, separation from God, the darkness of those who love darkness. Because Christ reveals heaven, He necessarily makes hell known as its opposite. The kingdom of hell is about us now, as the kingdom of heaven is, and each man at each moment may be earning the wages of its ruler. Christ descended into hell and rose from it; He has the keys of hell and of death, and in the end death and hell are cast into the lake of fire. On these words see also eternal and death.
historythe Bible a history · the history of mankind
For Maurice history is where God shows himself. The Bible is to be read as a continuous history, the history of the unveiling of God to the creature made in his image, and above all the history of a nation. That national history interprets the history of every nation, since each has a calling from God. The history of mankind is an upward education through work, suffering and sin. Other books give the records of a religion; the Bible gives the history of God’s dealings, his goodness, forbearance and longsuffering to a people. Maurice will not deny history any more than theology, since history has expounded theological truth.
holyholiness · a holy nation · consecration
Holiness for Maurice is not an attainment that sets a few men apart from the many. It is being called and claimed by God: Moses was holy because God separated him to be His minister, and Israel was a holy nation because God chose it and put His name upon it. The nation’s holiness came before the holiness of its saints. Sin is therefore the forgetting of a holy state, not the failure to reach one. Every Englishman may say, God has called me, therefore I am holy, and confess his sin as a departure from what God has declared him to be. Things are holy too, when they are bound to God’s service.
hopethe God of Hope · despair · the sure and certain hope
Hope for Maurice is a human privilege, like memory and trust, not an animal one: it binds man to man. Its ground is not anything in us but God, whom St. Paul dares to call the God of Hope, and its object is the full revelation of Christ. The Scriptures are Scriptures of hope for every nation, not a balance of hope and fear. Despair is the only utter perdition, because it binds a man in the prison of his evil nature; so we ought to stir up hope in every human being. Hope is often gained through despair, when we find we have no fountain of it in ourselves. And it is not limited to this life: a Christianity without hope beyond the grave would leave men more miserable than the heathen.
I AMthe I Am · Jehovah
The name revealed to Moses at the bush. For Maurice it is the ground of Israel’s law, freedom and national life: God declared himself not as a power or a notion but as the living, personal, righteous Being who is, the Deliverer of slaves and the unchangeable Lawgiver. The I AM is the one pillar of the law and freedom of Israel. In the New Testament this national name is expanded into the Name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost, the foundation of the universal family.
idolatryidols · idolater · the visible
Idolatry is worship of the visible in place of the unseen Lord: making God a thing of sense, or the soul’s thirst turned to things unworthy or unreal. It is natural to every man, and the whole Bible is a testimony against it and a record of how prone men are to it. Idols are gods who are not deliverers but tyrants, and so idolatry ends in fear, slavery and loss of courage, though it begins as an escape from the awe of an invisible Presence. Modern idols are Pleasure, Money and Fame, and the idols of the mind. Those who will bow to no visible thing see that all visible things are good.
Incarnationincarnate
God’s union with man in the person of his Son: for Maurice a fact, not a dogma, and the centre from which theology should start. It reveals God’s character, so no language about his love can be too strong. It is the ground of the Church and of the sonship of all men, and it hallows the earth. He blames Calvinism for moving it from the centre and making it a remedy that follows the Fall.
individualindividuality · individualism · each man
Maurice almost never sets the individual against society without setting society against the individual in the next breath. He honours the Protestant sense of personal distinctness in each man, and he says the tendency to individuality has a true ground, but he holds that, left to itself, it starves worship and fellowship and leaves men without truths that belong to them as members of a race. The individual is not to be saved out of his race, nor absorbed into a pantheistic whole: in Christ, the Head of the race, the individual man becomes more distinct and more wonderful precisely because he is one of a society. Christ is the reformer of both the individual and society, so neither is to be reformed first. Confession and judgment remain individual; each man owns acts that he can lay on no one else.
judgmentjudge · Judgment Day
God’s judgment is first discernment: the bringing to light of what is true and what is false. It is the work of Christ as Light, and it is going on now; the last judgment is the full unveiling of a tribunal before which men already stand. The Psalmists longed for it as the earth’s deliverance, and Maurice wants the Church to long for it again. The Judge and the Saviour are one Person with one purpose.
justicejust · do justly · justice and mercy
Maurice’s chief quarrel about justice is with the idea that justice and mercy are at war in God and needed a scheme to reconcile them. He calls that idea revolting to the conscience and says it comes from the Evil Spirit, who tempts men to be both unjust and hard-hearted. In God, justice and mercy are one nature, and God’s justice is shown in forgiving and cleansing, not in a satisfaction that lets some men off a penalty. Justice is not made right by a bare decree, nor discovered by philosophy alone: the command to do justly needs the revelation of a Just Being who shows what He is by what He does, and Christ is that revelation. Among men the just and the merciful are not two kinds of character; each fails without the other. Human justice, the law court and the punishment of wrongdoers, keeps its dignity as a witness that righteousness rules the earth.
justificationjustify · Justifier
Justification is God’s act of making himself known to the ungodly as the Righteous Being and raising them, through trust in him, to the righteousness for which they were made. Maurice keeps the word bound to righteous and righteousness, as in St. Paul’s Greek. God is the Justifier; faith is the response. He rejects any notion that God holds men to be what they are not, which would ascribe a fiction to the God of Truth. Luther’s witness was to a Justifier, and Maurice faults the Lutheran system for teaching men to believe in justification by faith instead. Justification belongs to the race in Christ, whom God justified as his Son by raising him.
kingkingly · dominion · sovereign
Christ is the true King of men, and his royalty is the opposite of the world’s: he takes the nature of his subjects, stoops to the lowest of them, suffers with them and delivers them from their oppressors. The King is the Son, and his kingly power is exercised in his Father’s name. Earthly sovereigns are his ministers for good, and their royalty is a reflection of his. Man was given dominion over the earth at creation; it can be abused till it becomes slavery, and it is redeemed in Christ, who makes men kings and priests. The same word names what usurps that rule: the dominion of visible things, of evil spirits and of the lie.
kingdomKingdom of Heaven · Kingdom of God · Kingdom of Christ
The actual government of God over men, revealed and set up in Christ, under which all men are already living whether they acknowledge it or not. It is Maurice’s name for the whole of what the Gospel announces. It is not chiefly a future state, an inner experience, or a religious society alongside other societies; it is the order of the world as God has constituted it, with a King at its centre.
knowledgeknowledge of God · to know God
For Maurice the knowledge of God is eternal life, as Christ says (John 17:3), and eternal death is the loss of that knowledge. It is knowledge of a person, gained as one knows any person, by sympathy, fellowship and love. It is not knowledge about mysteries, possessed by a few. St. John ties it to keeping the commandments, so that it belongs to humble people and grows with obedience. Only the Spirit of God can give it. Reason cannot make its Maker, but by its own law must receive the knowledge of him. The knowledge of God and the knowledge of oneself go together, and every sign that men are seeking it is to be welcomed.
lawlaws · the law of love
Two things in Maurice. The first is the written, regulative law, the Ten Commandments and the Mosaic code. That law is good and divine, but it presumes separation between God and man and cannot give life. The second is the law of man’s being, which is love and self-sacrifice. That law is as real as gravitation, and Christ embodied it. The law of liberty is the law God enables men to keep.
lifethe life of God · Lord and Giver of life · the Living God
Life, for Maurice, is derived. Man’s life is not his own possession but flows from the life of the Son of God, the Lord of every man, in whom is life, and whose life is the light of men. It becomes a vain show when a man wishes to have a life of his own, seeking it in the things he should rule. To love is the state of life and to hate the state of death, because love is communion with the life of God. Eternal life is the knowledge of God, and that knowledge does not earn the life; it is the life. The Living God, not a God read of in a book, is the Bible’s name for him, and the Spirit is the Giver of life.
lightGod is light · darkness · walking in the light
God is light, and in him is no darkness at all is, for Maurice, the first proposition of Christian ethics. Light is the symbol of goodness, wisdom, justice and truth, and God, who is all of these, may be called by the one word. Light, unlike goodness, can never be held as a possession; we only walk in it and receive it. That is why St. John kept the living word. The heathen dream of a God of light was true, and Christ fulfilled it by showing the light in a person. Darkness is each man’s being shut up in himself. It is an utterly unsocial state, so walking in the light means fellowship with one another, and to deny our sin is to shut the light out.
lovecharity
The nature of God and therefore the law of the universe. Maurice holds that “God is love” is not a pious feeling but the starting point of theology: the Church must “assert the love of God absolutely” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “Passion Week; its Dangers and Blessings”) and reason from it. Human love is not something we own but a share in his, received by submitting to him. Charity is the same word in the language of 1 Corinthians 13 and the Prayer Book, and he uses it for love as the ground of all things.
manifestationmanifest · show forth
Maurice’s word, taken from St. John, for God making himself known in his Son. Christ’s manifestation does not change God’s mind or create his love; it shows forth what God eternally is, so that men may know him and have fellowship with his life. Every act of Christ is a manifestation, an epiphany; the Cross is the manifestation of God’s righteousness in forgiving sin; the coming of Christ at the end is the full manifestation that will make the sons of God what they are meant to be. It is close to revelation and unveiling, but stresses the showing forth of a Person.
mankindthe race · humanity · the human race
For Maurice, mankind is not a collection of individual souls from which some are to be saved. It is a race with a Head, and God redeems mankind, not certain elect souls out of mankind. Christ died and ascended for the race, and humanity is complete only in Him. So Maurice resists both the evangelical shrinking of redemption to the elect and the century’s worship of humanity, which raises man to the throne of heaven. The universal is seen in the individual, and every man is to be honoured as a member of the race that Christ has claimed.
Mediatorone Mediator
Christ as the one in whom God and man actually meet and are united. Maurice’s Mediator is not a third party who shields men from an angry God, but the eternal Word in whom God made the worlds and in whom he has always held converse with men. Because there is one Mediator for all men, no priest, saint or system can stand between man and God, and every human ministry takes its meaning from his. Maurice thinks the denial of a real Mediator has consequences for politics as well as for religion.
memberunion · communion
The state of every man as part of the body whose Head is Christ. “Member of Christ” is the Catechism’s phrase, and Maurice takes it as a fact about all the baptized, and in principle about all men, not a reward for the converted. Membership brings union with God and communion with every other member, and it binds men to all nations and classes. The opposite of union is separation, which is sin and death.
mercymerciful · mercy and truth · the mercy-seat
Maurice will not let mercy stand on one side of a balance with truth, justice or righteousness on the other. Solomon’s text, by mercy and truth iniquity is purged, assumes that the two work together and are both enemies of iniquity. Mercy as the Psalmists implored it is the mercy of being delivered from unrighteousness, asked of One who gives it because He is righteous; mercy that indulges sin they dared not ask for. So God, being merciful and gracious, will not clear the guilty, and much human mercy is cruel while severity may hide mercy. Christ showed mercy and truth united, divided only by the evil that would destroy both. The mercy-seat is God Himself, the Propitiator who puts away sin because He is righteous. Mercy in men is not a beautiful quality to admire but a power to love, given by the Spirit of mercy and forgiveness.
moralitymorals · ethics · common morality
Morals and ethics, for Maurice, mean the manners and habits that belong to us as human beings: character, not deportment. True morality is common, homely and practical. It is the morality of the ordinary citizen, not a refined or saintly one, and it is sacred. It is universal and immutable, and a national Church exists to affirm that it is meant for all. Morality needs a ground. Precepts alone are a burden, and a universal morality needs the revelation of a moral Being, so theology is its basis, not its enemy. Christian ethics reverse the world’s ethics: we do not become God’s children by being good, but can be good because we are his children.
mysterymysteries · mysterious · the Christian mysteries
For Maurice a mystery is something given to be known. Christ told ignorant fishermen that it was given to them to know the mysteries of the Kingdom of Heaven: the eternal things cannot be conceived, but they can be known. So he resists both the claim of a few to be initiated into higher mysteries than common Christians, and the view, which he finds in Mansel, that the deepest truths of the faith must be received as dogmas that the reason cannot touch. With the old pagan mysteries he grants one real likeness: in every faith and every society the deepest principle must be mysterious. The Christian mystery, in St. Paul’s sense, is a secret hidden from the ages and now made manifest: the relation of Christ to humanity, which husbands, wives and children enter into by living it out. The clergy are stewards of these mysteries, answerable for how they have dispensed them.
Namethe Name · into the Name · Hallowed be thy Name
A name, for Maurice, is the disclosure of a character, and the Name of God is God made known: as Deliverer to Israel, and as Father, Son and Holy Spirit in the baptismal command. That Name is the foundation of the Church and of any universal society, the object of the Creeds, and the first thing the Lord’s Prayer asks for. To be baptized into the Name is to be taken into the relation it reveals.
nationnational · national life
A divine order of human society, standing between the family and the universal Church. Maurice reads the Bible as moving from family life to national life to the life of mankind, with God revealed at each stage. National life is holy, not a worldly accident to be outgrown, and the Church exists to teach and bless nations, never to replace them. Israel is the pattern and the abiding sign of this.
naturethe King of nature · the powers of Nature · evil nature
Maurice uses nature in two main ways. Of the visible world, he teaches that the King of men is also the King of nature, who made the world for man and ordered it by laws that show themselves fully only when they bear on man. Nature is not to be worshipped: the heathen powers of Nature or a Lord of Nature divide God into many forms, and a God who merely plays with natural powers is an enchanter. Man is the lord of nature, and its study is sacred. Of man’s inner life, Maurice distinguishes the evil or selfish nature, which Christ delivers us from, from the true state or constitution in Christ to which He restores us.
obedienceobey · obedient · cheerful obedience
Obedience for Maurice is not the opposite of freedom but its condition. Man alone among creatures has the power of disobedience and so the privilege of cheerful, voluntary obedience; the stars and rivers obey by law, men by consent. The obedience of Christ to His Father is the ground of all: it rectifies the disorder that self-will has made. Obedience cannot purchase a blessing, and an obedience done to be seen is the Pharisee’s. True obedience is trust: it confesses its Master, consents to be raised, and becomes obedient.
opinionsnotions · notional
What men think about God, Christ or the Church, as against God, Christ and the Church themselves. For Maurice an opinion or notion is a product of the human mind, partial and changeable, and it becomes dangerous when it is put in the place of the reality it describes. Faith is not a set of opinions; truth is not a notion; the Church does not stand on notions about the sacraments but on the sacraments. He welcomes an age that can no longer rest on opinions, because it may then be driven to the foundation. The words are nearly always pejorative, and he expects to be called a “notionalist” for attacking them.
ordinationepiscopacy · bishop · ministry
The Church’s ministry is an order of servants, appointed by Christ and continued by ordination, whose authority is entirely from God and whose work is to present Christ to men and men to Christ. Maurice defends episcopacy, but not as a vicarial power held for an absent Lord: bishops are “the bonds of communication” between different lands, the great bond of a Christendom life, and the episcopate contains all the other orders. Ordination is a visible witness that ministers exist for the sake of men; secular and arrogant bishops are those who did not believe in their own ordination.
peacethe Lord of Peace · My peace · peace-maker
Peace for Maurice is not ease, quiet, or freedom from conflict. Christ gave His peace on the night of His betrayal, and the Apostles went at once into the worst disquiet of their lives. His peace is that by which He passed through opposition and agony: trust in the Father. It belongs to the very nature of God, the Lord of Peace, who is also the Lord of Hosts. It is not bought by prayers or good deeds but given directly. It harmonizes all energies instead of stifling them, demands the destruction of what is base, and makes peace-makers of those who receive it.
personliving Person · personal
Maurice’s constant contrast is between a Person and a notion. What God reveals is not a doctrine but a living Person; the foundation of the Church and of society is a Person, and not a notion; faith is trust in a Person, not in our faith or in a dogma about him. God is a living, personal Being who says I am, and the Spirit is a Person, not a floating influence. The habit of thinking of God and Christ as persons is for Maurice the root of all his theology.
the poorpoverty · charity · alms · preaching to the poor
For Maurice the gospel is to the poor not because the poor are a class with special needs but because there is a man in every beggar whom God addresses as His child. The poor need what the noble and the learned need, the common and human, not something adapted to their condition, and the preacher who cultivates tricks for each class has missed it. The Beatitudes told poor men in Galilee and in the London courts and alleys that the God who governs them is not one who favours the rich, and they carry a real woe on riches that give consolation. On alms he holds two truths together: the Incarnation forbids leaving men to the consequences of their folly, and it forbids lazy giving that keeps men as animals or vagabonds instead of raising them as sons of God. Charity should awaken the poor to providence, and the higher classes may need to be reformed by them. Gifts to the poor belong with all our ordinary work, as part of the regular business of life.
prayerpray · the Lord's Prayer · common prayer
For Maurice, prayer begins in the sense of a Father: without it the impulse to pray would never have been awakened. Men pray because things are not as they ought to be, and suffering drives them to it whatever they think. True prayer is not an attempt to bend God’s will to ours; its great subject is the renovation of our own will, the request that His will be done. It is the confession of weakness grounded on the nature of God. Prayer is common, not private: even alone, a man prays in the midst of a family and says our, not my. Prayer presupposes a restoration already made in Christ, through whom it is offered.
principleprinciples · positive principle · living principle
A principle, in Maurice, is a living truth that men discover rather than invent: something positive, belonging to mankind, that one acts on and is governed by. Its opposite is the notion, a thought in the head, and its counterfeit is the system, which a party builds by tearing one principle apart from the rest. Each sect’s positive principle is true and is a note in the divine harmony. Its system is a disturbance of that harmony. Christ delivered the principles of his kingdom, which are the foundation of civil order and not its rival. A question of principle cannot be settled as a question of degree or of safety.
ProtestantProtestantism · pure Protestantism · Romanist
Maurice sees Protestantism as positive, not merely negative. The Reformation asserted three great principles: justification by faith, election (the Absolute Will at the root of all things) and the Bible as the revelation of that Will, with a fourth, the religious ground of the nation. These were true witnesses against Roman denials. But when Protestants made them into systems for deciding who was justified or elect, they split the Church into sects, reduced the Bible to dry propositions, and lost the common object of faith. Protestant and Romanist each hold half a truth, and the Catholic Church, which belongs to both, is the reconciliation of the two.
punishmentpunish · penalty · eternal punishment
Maurice’s whole teaching on punishment turns on one reversal: what men have to dread is sin, not punishment. To escape the punishment of sin while the sin remains is not a blessing but a misery, and a man who has done evil wants to be set free from it, not let off. Hence his objection to every scheme, priestly or Protestant, that makes deliverance from punishment the gift of the gospel, and to the notion of a God satisfied by punishment rather than by the obedience of His Son. God’s punishments are real and just; they are the expressions of His tenderness, His way of educating wills and not crushing them, and the forgiven sinner would not wish to escape them. Eternal punishment, read beside eternal life, is the punishment of being without the knowledge of God who is love. Human punishment keeps its place: the law of an eye for an eye showed an order in the world, and the magistrate still has to punish.
redemptionredeem · Redeemer
Redemption keeps for Maurice its simplest scriptural sense: deliverance out of a real bondage. God is the Redeemer; he has visited and redeemed his people, from Egypt and from the vain traditions, sins and false gods that enslave men. The sacrifice is the means God himself uses, not a price paid to change his mind; its costliness lay in the Son becoming the servant of all. Redemption is of mankind, not of a select few: God has redeemed mankind in his Son, and the Church exists to witness to it. It reaches the body and the whole creation.
regenerationregenerate · new birth · Regenerator
Maurice examines three common meanings of regeneration. The first, a special nature given to some persons at baptism or conversion in place of ordinary human nature, he rejects as incompatible with any belief that the human constitution is good. The second, the repair of a decayed building to its first plan, fits ordinary language but does not appear accomplished. The third, a life received from a Father here and now, is nearer, but it makes the spiritual society an exception among human societies. His own account is that Christ is the Regenerator of humanity. Baptism declares what is already man’s true constitution in Him, and the only condemnation is to shut one’s eyes to that light. So regeneration is social as well as individual, and it reaches the body and the earth as well as the spirit. The Church’s failures, Roman and Protestant, come from unbelief in it, and its message to the working classes should be that Christ restores humanity to its proper condition.
religionour religion · the religious world
Maurice is suspicious of the word. The Gospels do not announce a religion, and the word does not occur in them. They announce a King and a kingdom. “Our religion” is man’s conception of God and man’s way of carrying it out, and its history, taken by itself, is black. What saves it is a Christ ruling through it. A religion can be a scheme set against other schemes, a support to law for statesmen, a trading bargain, or a source of terror. The “religious world” is the counterfeit of the Church. Yet the religions of mankind express a real longing for fellowship with God, and the Bible gives a Gospel concerning God that meets it.
repentancerepent · conversion · change of mind
To repent is to change one’s mind about God: to stop thinking of him as an enemy to be fled from or bribed, and to believe him a Father. Conversion is the turning that follows, and the two belong together. Only then can a man truly say that he has sinned. Repentance is too hard for man to accomplish. It is Christ’s gift, and the call to it is itself the gift. The call is the same for the drunkard and the most religious. Weighing out contrition to earn acceptance is pride. Repentance may be national as well as personal, and it is always tied to the nearness of the kingdom.
revelationreveal · unveiling
God’s disclosure of himself to men: not the supply of information about God, nor a religion, but the unveiling of a Person, the Father, in the Son, by the Spirit. Maurice insists on the literal sense of the Greek apokalypsis: the drawing back of a veil from what is already there. His controversy with H. L. Mansel in What is Revelation? (1859) turns on whether such a direct manifestation of God is possible at all.
righteousnessrighteous · the Righteous Being
First of all the character of God, “the Righteous Being”, who can only do right and whose whole government aims to make men right. Human righteousness is not a possession of one’s own but trust in that righteous God and participation in a righteousness that is in another, in Christ. For Maurice the Bible’s theology is the unveiling of the Righteous Being to man, and righteousness and love turn out to be one.
sacramentsacramental
A sacrament is a given pledge of God’s presence and of men’s union with him and with each other. It is God’s act, not man’s, and it testifies of what is already true. Maurice widens the idea: the events of Israel’s history were “Sacraments of God’s presence”, and the Bible has a sacramental character. He holds that the sacraments are stronger than every theory about them, and that they will end only when signs are no longer needed, when God’s servants see his face.
sacrificeself-sacrifice
The giving up of oneself to God, which Maurice finds at the root of all true life: in the Son’s eternal submission to the Father, in Christ’s death, and in every right human act. True sacrifice comes from God and expresses his will; false sacrifice is man’s attempt to buy God off. The Doctrine of Sacrifice (1854) traces the word through the Bible, and nearly every other doctrinal term in Maurice is touched by it.
sectparty · sectarian
A body of men bound together by their own choice around certain opinions, badges and objects, as against the Church, which God has planted and which witnesses to his love for all mankind. A sect chooses Christ for his doctrine, and then chooses a teacher with the best form of it, so that Christ himself is lost in opinions about him. A party is the same thing in English form, a sect organized with flags and watchwords. Maurice believes every such plant will be rooted up, but that the work is God’s, not ours; the worst sin is to form a new party to overcome the old ones. Each sect may yet bear witness to a truth.
seekingseek God · feel after Him · God seeking men
Maurice’s seeking runs in both directions, and the second is the deeper. Men seek God, if haply they might feel after him and find him, and it was God who stirred up that search, ordering the bounds of the nations for it. Every honest seeker after truth is under his teaching. But the Bible is the history of God seeking men, not of men seeking God. The great reversal of conversion is to learn that God is not seeking to condemn but to justify, and to bring men into union with himself. Seeking goes wrong when men seek God where he is not, in visible things, or seek righteousness in themselves instead of in him. Self-seeking is its counterfeit.
self-willself-willed
The root sin: the will that sets itself up as independent of the Father. Maurice opposes it to self-sacrifice as the two possible principles of power, and asks which one expresses the being of God. A self-willed God would be a false God; Christ reveals a God whose power is sacrifice. In men, self-will is the condition from which they need to be delivered, and the freedom of the will begins only there.
signsigns · token · sign and pledge
A thing or act that shows a fact which already holds and does not depend on the person who sees it. Maurice uses the word for Christ’s miracles, for the sacraments and other ordinances of the Church, and for events in history. A sign never creates what it signifies, and it does not merely stand for an idea: it testifies to a present reality, above all to a Person.
sinsins · sinner · sense of sin
Sin is not first a crime against society or a bad habit of character. It is a wrong against a Being to whom one is bound, and it is known only when a man can say, Father, I have sinned against thee. Its essence is distrust: the suspicion of God, the denial that we are his children, the claim to be independent of him. So it is also separation from our fellows. Sin is unmanly and inhuman, not something proper to human nature. It is a contradiction, and no definition reaches it. Maurice refuses to make sin the starting point of theology. Christ, not Adam, is the ground, and the Deliverer is stronger than the sin.
societysocial · universal society
The common life of men, which Maurice treats as a reality given by God and not a contract or construction. Society has its ground in God’s righteous nature and is held together by sacrifice; it cannot rest on opinions, on interest or on individual rights. Men feel the need of a universal society, and every attempt to construct one by will has failed; the true universal society is the Church, constituted in the Person of Christ, which expands family and nation instead of destroying them. Against it stands the world, the society bound together by each man’s selfishness.
Son of Man
The name Christ most often gives himself, which Maurice takes to declare him the Head and representative of mankind, not of one nation or of believers only. It answers what all nations had wanted, a ruler who is also one of them. Maurice nearly always pairs it with Son of God: the two names together say that God and man are united in one Person, and that men may see the Father in a man.
SpiritHoly Ghost · Comforter
The third Person of the Trinity, a living and personal presence, not an influence. Maurice insists on personality: the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son and is the bond of their union, and he dwells among men now. He is the source of every human gift, of healing, speech and government, and the power who unites men in one Church. He is also the Spirit of truth, set against the spirits of lies and party.
sufferingsorrow · the Man of Sorrows · sufferer
Maurice does not offer a theory that explains suffering away. He holds that a scheme of optimism fails when calamity comes, and that the question why the innocent suffer will press on believer and sceptic alike. His answer is a person: Christ the Man of Sorrows, whose life was a life of joy because He pleased not Himself, and whose sorrow was that men’s reproaches against His Father fell on Him. Since they fell on the beloved Son, troubles are no proof of God’s hatred. Suffering is not to be read as a sign that the sufferer was a greater sinner. It is common, not private: the sufferings of the present time are those of the whole creation, travail-pangs of a nobler birth, and the Christian claims a share in them rather than exemption. Sorrow seems incommunicable, yet in the one body what affects one affects all. Pain can be a Father’s message, and much of our misery comes from trying to please ourselves.
systemsystematizer · method
A system is a scheme of thought made by men from one principle torn away from the rest, and then imposed as the ground of a party. Maurice refuses to build one and is suspicious of all of them, the most complete most of all. Against system he sets method: the living order that is found in history, in the Bible and in the Church, and that is discovered rather than invented. He reads each system for the truth it witnesses to, which is always a truth of the divine order it replaces.
TrinityTrinity in Unity
The revealed name of God, Father, Son and Holy Ghost, and the ground of the Church and of humanity. For Maurice the Trinity is not an abstract dogma to be defended with proof-texts but a revelation of real Persons in real unity, made “substantially” in Christ. It is a mystery of love, and the foundation of every human unity. He holds it most firmly when he refuses to use it as a weapon.
truththe Truth · God of Truth · Spirit of Truth
Truth, for Maurice, is first a character in God: he is the God of Truth, who hates every lie and is pledged to destroy lies. Christ is the Truth as a living Person, not a proposition, and the Spirit is the Spirit of Truth, who guides men into all truth. So truth is something men seek and are received into, never something they make. It is required in the inward parts, against every self-deception and evasion. Living truths stand opposed to abstract notions and to the opinions of parties. The worst decay of a nation is when its teachers make a principle of not speaking the truth.
unityone · uniformity
The oneness that God has already given to men, in families, in nations and in the Church, and whose ground is the unity of God himself, the Father and the Son in the Spirit. Maurice’s governing point is that unity is to be kept, not made. Every attempt to create it by scheme, system or force destroys what it means to secure. Unity is also distinct from uniformity: it holds diversity together and does not abolish it.
willthe Will of God · a loving Will · the will in man
For Maurice the Will of God is the ground of everything right, and that Will is a will to all good, a loving Will, not a bare sovereignty. It cannot be known as mere decree; it expresses itself in the relation of Father and Son. In Christ the Divine Will appears as the will of surrender. The will is also the centre of man: the Gospel is addressed to it, God and the devil both claim it, and it must submit to one of them. God does not crush the human will but educates and raises it, until it is subdued to His and, in that subjection, set free.
witnesstestimony · bear witness
The calling of every man, nation and Church: to testify of a God who is present and of a truth that already is. Christ is the faithful and true Witness; Israel was called out to bear witness for a living God among idolaters; the Church is a witness to the world of facts that the world naturally denies. Maurice’s habit of reading sects and systems for what they witness to belongs here: each bears witness to some truth, even while it distorts it. A witness that ceases to testify loses its reason for existing.
WordWord of God · Eternal Word
For Maurice the Word of God is first a living Person, the Son, who utters what is in the Father as a word utters the thought that it makes known. He is the light that has lightened every man, the Teacher who speaks in the conscience and reason of men in all ages, the Creator by whom the worlds were made, and the Word made flesh. Maurice insists that the Word must be thought of as a Person and not a decree, and that the Word is known as the Lamb that was slain. Scripture is the word of God because it speaks of him.
worldthe world · worldly
Maurice keeps two senses of the word together and refuses to let either swallow the other. The world is God’s creation and the mankind he loves and has redeemed. It is also a principle at work in every family, nation and Church: society organised round each man as his own centre and living in what passes. The second is not a separate population but the first without God, and no man belongs to it by right.
worshipforms of worship · common worship
Worship is the act in which men, as members of one family, confess and adore the one God who has made himself known to them. For Maurice it is never first an individual’s request for something he wants; it is the prayer of a body, the act in which fellowship is most fully realised, and so the great sign of a universal society. All division comes through idolatry, all union through the adoration of the living God. The old forms of worship, used across nations and centuries, witness that the gift of Pentecost has never been withdrawn. All worship is communion, either with God or with lower things.
