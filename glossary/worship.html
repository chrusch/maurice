A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

worship

forms of worship · common worship

Frequency in the corpus: worship and its forms 1,026; idolatry 277; ordinance and ordinances 138. The central source is The Kingdom of Christ* II, “Forms of Worship”.*

In brief. Worship is the act in which men, as members of one family, confess and adore the one God who has made himself known to them. For Maurice it is never first an individual’s request for something he wants; it is the prayer of a body, the act in which fellowship is most fully realised, and so the great sign of a universal society. All division comes through idolatry, all union through the adoration of the living God. The old forms of worship, used across nations and centuries, witness that the gift of Pentecost has never been withdrawn. All worship is communion, either with God or with lower things.

Shades of meaning

  1. The act of fellowship. If Baptism brings us into God’s family, “we must feel that acts of worship should be, of all acts, those which most belong to our position, and in which our fellowship is most entirely realised” (The Kingdom of Christ II, “Forms of Worship”). See baptism and fellowship · communion.

  2. Union against idolatry. “That all division comes through idolatry; that all union comes through the adoration of the one living and true God; these are the two texts of the Bible” (same), from Babel to Pentecost. Men have turned it the other way, making it “the slave of their circumstances, the badge of their divisions, the instrument of their hatred” (same). Of Babylon in the Apocalypse he says it draws men “from the worship of a Spiritual Being, who was their Lord, and Friend, and Redeemer, to be worshippers of a multitude of natural things or powers” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture XVII). See unity.

  3. Forms as a sign. Forms written by Greeks in the third and fourth centuries are still in use in England. “I say that these forms of worship, preserved through so many generations, adapted to every locality, are such a sign; I say, that using these, I have a right to believe that the blessings of the day of Pentecost have been given once, and never withdrawn” (same). See sign and Catechism · Articles · Prayer Book.

  4. The prayer of a body. Against those for whom prayer is individual, Maurice argues that the prayer of the congregation “is not an aggregate of such individual prayers, but the prayer of a body, each member of which professes to have renounced his own selfish position, that he may come as one of a family to seek the Father of it” (same). Even alone, “thou must not dare to say my, but our” (same). See Father and member · union · communion.

  5. Prayer supposes a restored humanity. “Prayer does suppose a restoration; that the idea of prayer and the idea of a Church can never be separated, each implying the other; and that a Church which is not built upon the confession of a restored humanity is a contradiction in terms” (same). Those who pray claim no superiority to the worst member of their race; “they identify their neighbours’ sins with their own” (same). See Church.

  6. All worship is communion. On the meats offered to idols: “All worship is communion. You are brought into communion with the eternal God the Father. In the body and blood of Christ you are united to Him and united to each other” (The Unity of the New Testament, “The First Epistle to the Corinthians”). Sacrifice to idols expresses “the communion of men with beings of a lower order, subjection to mere powers of nature” (same). The Communion service is “the centre of a Christian worship: with it all our worship stands or falls” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eucharist Considered in its Relation to Confession and Absolution”). See Eucharist.

  7. A common worship the human want. All the religions of the earth show that men cannot devise a common worship, “and do not all these religions also contain a declaration that there must be a common worship” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Meaning of the Gift of Tongues”), and that God must come forth and tell them what He is? United worship is possible only when there is “One Being, eternal, immortal, invisible, to whom all may look up together” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon I). Of the saints in the Apocalypse: “their bond is a common worship” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture VII).

  8. Worship and daily life. “Our worship must always be dreary, if it stands aloof from our daily life; if it does not interpret and transfigure that life. The heavenly worship is continuous only because growth in knowledge is continuous, and because all free action is continuous” (Lectures on the Apocalypse, Lecture IV). The counterfeit is self-worship: whatever the pretext on which “we build altars to ourselves; the worship is in all cases equally accursed” (The Lord’s Prayer, Sermon IX). See self-will.

Neighbouring words

  • prayer (its chief act). Senses 4 and 5.
  • adoration, praise, thanksgiving, confession (its parts). Sense 3; Maurice defends praise against those who confuse it with flattery.
  • idolatry, sensual worship (its opposite). Sense 2; see idolatry.
  • Liturgy, forms, ordinances (its vehicles). Sense 3; see Catechism · Articles · Prayer Book.
  • communion, fellowship (what it is). Senses 1 and 6.
  • Eucharist, Mass (its centre). Sense 6; the word Mass, he notes, came to give the impression “that this worship is essentially the offering of a Sacrifice” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Christmas Gathering”).
  • Name (what it confesses). See Name.

Scripture. John 4:23–24, God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth, quoted in “Forms of Worship” and “The Eucharist”; Genesis 11:4 and Acts 2:11, Babel and Pentecost (sense 2); 1 Corinthians 10:16–21, The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? (sense 6); Revelation 7:9–12 (sense 7).
