A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

fear

the fear of God · terror · dread

Frequency in the corpus: fear in about 450 paragraphs, terror and dread in about 150. The main sources are the Lincoln’s Inn sermons “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God” and “The Gospel of Hope”, Lecture XV of The Epistles of St. John, and “Revivals”.

In brief. Maurice separates two fears that his age ran together. The fear of God, which Scripture calls the beginning of wisdom, is awe before One who is substantial, holy and merciful, and it grows the nearer a man comes to Him. The fear of punishment is a miserable substitute for it: a terror of a distant or false god, which ends in superstition and slavery. Religion that rests on terror, materialising the torments of the future to drive men to obedience, presents the Father as though He were the Evil Spirit. Yet Maurice does not explain terror away: there is a real dread of eternity and of oneself that no priest invented, and only a gospel of forgiveness answers it. Trust, not argument, puts terror to flight; perfect love casts out fear; and the access to the Father without dread is the root of every other fruit of the atonement.

Shades of meaning

  1. The fear of God against the fear of punishment. The Old Testament teaches that the fear of God is the beginning and end of wisdom. “But it is the fear of God, to which holy men give this title, not that miserable substitute for it, the fear of punishment. It was the fear of the Lord, the Righteous and Merciful Lord; not the fear of some false being, some creature of their own thoughts, clothed with their own evil qualities” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God or in the Transactions of Men”). See punishment and mercy.

  2. The fear that keeps from evil. Neither an all-pitying indulgence nor the dread of an unbending Lawgiver keeps men from evil. “But the fear of One in Whom dwells all Mercy and Truth; to be separated from Whom is to be separated from Mercy and Truth; from Whom comes restoration as well as life” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God or in the Transactions of Men”) is the fear that, “when it penetrates the whole man, will keep us from every evil way” (same). Despair and indulgence end in the same place: “Wretchlessness of unclean living is as much the escape from the bondage of terror, as it is promoted by the expectation of perpetual indulgences” (same). See sin.

  3. Awe, reserved for the holy. Scripture does not balance hope against fear. It warns of what follows from distrust in God, and it encourages “another kind of fear” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “The Gospel of Hope”), the fear of the righteous God. Idols could be played with even by those who trembled before them. “Awe must be reserved for that which we feel to be substantial and holy, for One Whom we did not make, but Who made us” (same). See hope and holy.

  4. Terror at a distance, reverence near. “The more near he is brought to God, the greater he feels is the necessity for adoration and worship; while he contemplates Him at a distance there is terror, but not reverence or awe” (Theological Essays, “On the Trinity in Unity”). The old divines should be read so that we may feel “how impossible it is to cultivate the fear of God in any sense in which the Scriptures speak of it, if we regard Him as a distant, unknown Being whose Nature and Character we cannot enter into, or partake of” (What Is Revelation?, Letter VIII); such fear alternates between the feeling of slaves towards a cruel taskmaster and that of idolaters. See worship and Trinity.

  5. Superstition begins by fleeing fear. “Superstition and idolatry terminate in universal fear; but they begin with the effort to get rid of fear. A man does not like the feeling of an Invisible Presence, of One Who is near him by night and day, spying out all his ways” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Mercy and Truth Not Opposed in the Nature of God or in the Transactions of Men”). Each of us carries “some visible or invisible terror, which is always ready to make a coward of him” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “Revivals”), and reasoning fails when it is most wanted; it is “Trust, simple Trust, in One Who is above all and through all” (same) that puts the vision to flight. A religion that ceases to be one of trust “passes into one of slavish fear” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel VI, “Revivals”). See idolatry and faith.

  6. Terror as a tool of government. In the Latin Church he finds “the efforts to materialise the terrors of the future world, and to make those terrors the great motives to obedience” (Theological Essays, “Concluding Essay: On Eternal Life and Eternal Death”). The Father, hidden behind mediators, “was continually presented to the conscience as the great object of its terror, in the very likeness of the Evil Spirit” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Preface). See hell and Church.

  7. The real dread of eternity. Maurice will not call all terror a priestly trick. “Men tremble at eternity, whether they think of it as present or to come; they must tremble at it, unless there comes forth from it a Gospel of forgiveness and reconciliation. It is no sham terror, begotten by the arts of priests, which I feel” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “The Eternal Not Opposed to the Present”) when he knows he cannot shake eternity from him. “We cling to the one because we are afraid of the other. There is a Conscience which makes cowards of us all” (same). See eternal and conscience.

  8. Whom to fear. He reads Christ’s command to fear him who can cast into hell as a warning against the enemy, not against the Father: “fear that spirit of lies and hatred” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Daylight in the Heart the Preparation for the Day of Christ”), who will succeed “unless you trust in that Father” (same). See devil.

  9. Perfect love casts out fear. The love that is one-sided is anxious and jealous, because it is not perfected. “In every case that perfected love which each lover realizes in the other casts out fear. The torment of doubt and suspicion is over; there is no balancing to ascertain who has most; there is no mine or thine” (The Epistles of St. John, Lecture XV). Between the Father and His children there would then be “no fear of any fuller manifestation of His glory; only a continual hope of it” (same). Nor is fear humility: a man who doubts whether he does not love God better than God loves him “is not humble” (same). See love and judgment.

  10. Access without dread. “To have the right of approaching Him without dread, to be warranted in calling Him, Father,—to be sure that that which binds us to Him is mightier than all that is making us feel at a distance from Him,—this is that fruit of the Atonement” (The Doctrine of Sacrifice, Sermon XIII) which underlies all the rest, “the peace with our fellow-men, the freedom from terror of the future, the hope of good things to come” (same). See atonement, peace and freedom.

  11. Rising above the terror of opinion. Fear of men’s judgment is the commonest fear. No one can learn from public opinion “till he has risen in some measure above the terror of it” (Theological Essays, “On the Judgment Day”), any more than a man can admire a storm while trembling lest it destroy him. The cure is to live as answerable to a more righteous Judge. See judgment.

Neighbouring words

  • awe and reverence (the true fear). What the distant God cannot draw out, and the near God does.
  • terror and dread (the false or unanswered fear). The fear of punishment, of a false god, of eternity without a gospel.
  • superstition (fear’s religion). Begins in flight from God’s presence and ends in universal fear.
  • trust (its cure). Simple trust, not argument, puts the vision to flight.
  • love (what casts it out). Perfected love leaves no room for fear.
  • slavery and bondage (its social form). Slavish fear crouches before powers and rulers.
  • hope (what Scripture sets beside the fear of the Lord). Not a balance of hope and fear, but both grounded in God.

Scripture. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Psalm 111:10). By the fear of the Lord men depart from evil (Proverbs 16:6). There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment (1 John 4:18). Fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul (Matthew 10:28). That we being delivered out of the hand of our enemies might serve him without fear (Luke 1:74).
