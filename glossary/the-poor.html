A Glossary of Maurice’s Terms

the poor

poverty · charity · alms · preaching to the poor

Frequency in the corpus: the poor, poor men and poverty in about 240 paragraphs. The main sources are the Lincoln’s Inn sermons “Christmas Charities” (preached for the District Relief and Provident Societies) and “Homily III: On Preaching to the Poor”, and Lecture X of The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, on the Beatitudes.

In brief. For Maurice the gospel is to the poor not because the poor are a class with special needs but because there is a man in every beggar whom God addresses as His child. The poor need what the noble and the learned need, the common and human, not something adapted to their condition, and the preacher who cultivates tricks for each class has missed it. The Beatitudes told poor men in Galilee and in the London courts and alleys that the God who governs them is not one who favours the rich, and they carry a real woe on riches that give consolation. On alms he holds two truths together: the Incarnation forbids leaving men to the consequences of their folly, and it forbids lazy giving that keeps men as animals or vagabonds instead of raising them as sons of God. Charity should awaken the poor to providence, and the higher classes may need to be reformed by them. Gifts to the poor belong with all our ordinary work, as part of the regular business of life.

Shades of meaning

  1. A man in every beggar. “Our Gospel is to the poor, only because there is a man in every beggar, whom the god of this world is trying to hold down in death, whom the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ is speaking to as His child” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel I, “The Gospel the Manifestation of God”). The sign that Christ was the Ruler and not only a teacher was that “the poor have the Gospel preached to them” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Tests of the Deliverer”). See child and Deliverer.

  2. The common and the human. What all require is “that we should give them credit for being like ourselves, for needing what we need, for having a capacity to receive whatever is most precious to us. The common and the human, not the peculiar, is what in his heart of hearts the noble, the doctor, the peasant sighs for” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Homily III: On Preaching to the Poor”). The plan of adapting oneself to the tastes of each class, with “certain tricks of oratory for the purpose of recommending ourselves to them” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Homily III: On Preaching to the Poor”), is full of fallacy: “All these arts commence in vanity, end in disappointment” (same). See Son of Man and mankind.

  3. Searching but human. Preaching the Kingdom would be “searching to individuals, but it would be essentially human” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Homily III: On Preaching to the Poor”), naming the frauds of priest, advocate and tradesman alike. Every real effort “to evangelize the outlying portions of society” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Homily III: On Preaching to the Poor”) has shaken the whole society and forced the question whether we have any good news at all. Doctors and sanitary reformers often preached better: “How much better, medical men have preached it to the poor at their houses and in hospitals than we have done!” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Homily III: On Preaching to the Poor”). See kingdom and repentance.

  4. Yours is the Kingdom. Poor men had naturally supposed that God regarded the rich with affection, and the difficulty “was not more difficult for the people who dwelt about the coasts of Tyre and Sidon than for the people who dwell in the courts and alleys of London” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture X). So the Beatitude meant: “You have a Father in Heaven who is seeking after you, watching over you, whom you may trust entirely” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture X), and “must they not have been true for all that crowd, for every thief and harlot in it?” (same). See Father and kingdom.

  5. The woe on riches. The woes on the rich “cannot be evaded. And I hold it is greatly for the interest of all of us who are leading easy and comfortable lives in the world, that it should not be evaded. If any amount of riches, greater or smaller, does give us consolation, it is well for us to understand that there is a woe upon those riches” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture X). “If the civil ruler sanctions one law for the rich and one for the poor, he offends against the maxims of the Kingdom of Heaven” (The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven, Lecture X). See justice and law.

  6. Alms and the Incarnation. When a beggar asks alms “for the love of Christ” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”), may he hold back his hand? Christmas forbids treating charity as a seasonal exception: “What is right at Christmas must be right always. Either the Incarnation means nothing, and we should pay no heed to it, or it determines what our thoughts and deeds should be, all our lives through” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”). If Christ, who was rich, became poor, “it cannot be a true maxim that men should generally be left to the consequences of their own acts” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”). Christ did not choose the meritorious; “if we had to prove our title to live, we must all be left to perish” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”). See Incarnation and mercy.

  7. Against lazy giving. Yet Christ stooped to us “that He might raise us” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”). “It is clearly not good for any individual that he should live as a mere animal, when God has intended him for a man; as a slave, when he ought to be free” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”). Hence the condemnation of “that lazy self-indulgent giving, into which we are all apt to fall, and which we know in our consciences has no title to the noble name of Charity” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”), and of institutions that kept up “a race of vagabonds who never learnt the worth and glory of labour” (same). See freedom and love.

  8. Relief and providence. He praises the district societies for joining relief with providence, and for letting the giver come as a member of a district, not as a lone benefactor. Providence is not a secular virtue: “Our high classes and our moneyed classes may need to be reformed by them. England may again become a thrifty and provident nation, may be preserved from sinking into a nation of gamblers, by her labourers” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel III, “Christmas Charities”). Their providence must rest on the gospel “that Christ by His nativity has claimed for them a second birth” (same). See society and nation.

  9. Giving as ordinary business. St. Paul bade the Corinthians collect for the poor “not as some act of virtue or sanctity, but as part of their regular business” (Sermons Preached in Lincoln’s Inn Chapel IV, “Christian Services and Christian Rewards”). The family, the profession and the poor are neglected alike “by any man who once resolutely acts upon the principle that he is sent into the world to care for himself” (same). Christ “came into the very midst of all the troubles of all the poor people who were tormented with different ailments” (Sermons Preached in Country Churches, XXIV, “The Lord of the Winds and Sea”). See duty and self-will.

In his life. Maurice led the Christian Socialists from 1848, helped found working men’s associations, and in 1854 founded the Working Men’s College in London, of which he was the first principal. (General knowledge, not drawn from the corpus.)

Neighbouring words

  • beggar (the test case). A man whom God addresses as His child.
  • charity (its true and false forms). Not lazy self-indulgent giving; the love that raises.
  • alms and relief (the act). Best joined with what awakens providence.
  • providence and thrift (the virtue charity should awaken). Grounded on the second birth.
  • the rich (the woe). Riches that give consolation lie under a woe.
  • the common and the human (what all classes need). Not adaptation to a class.
  • labour (its dignity). The worth and glory that the vagabond never learns.

Scripture. For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor (2 Corinthians 8:9). Blessed be ye poor: for yours is the kingdom of God (Luke 6:20). But woe unto you that are rich! for ye have received your consolation (Luke 6:24). The poor have the gospel preached to them (Matthew 11:5). If a man say, I love God, and hateth his brother, he is a liar (1 John 4:20).
